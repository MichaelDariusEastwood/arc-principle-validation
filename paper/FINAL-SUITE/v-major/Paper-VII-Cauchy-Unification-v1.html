Abstract

Cauchy's four functional equations - additive, multiplicative, exponential, and logarithmic - are the only continuous solutions to their respective composition constraints. We show that this 200-year-old mathematical result has a physical consequence: under the stated axioms, it constrains scaling laws to one of three functional families (power law, exponential, or saturation curve). The family is determined by the composition operator of the underlying recursive amplification process.

We present a structured prediction comparison across 20 real-world systems spanning biology (metabolic scaling, hemoglobin binding, bacterial growth), physics (radioactive decay, Brownian diffusion, time crystals), economics (Moore's law, Wright's law, urban scaling), epidemiology (Ebola SIR), linguistics (Zipf's law, Heap's law), computer science (Amdahl's law, neural scaling laws), and ecology (species-area relationship). For each domain, the composition operator was classified from known physics before fitting, the scaling family was predicted from the operator class, published or standard reference data was loaded, and the prediction was tested by independent model fitting.

Primary result: under strict AIC-based model selection, 14/20 domains preferred the Cauchy-predicted family over unrestricted alternatives ($p = 8.79 \times 10^{-4}$, binomial test against $p = 1/3$ chance). Under a permissive R$^2$ tolerance criterion (predicted family within 0.05 of the best fit), all 20 domains confirmed ($p = 2.87 \times 10^{-10}$). To the best of the author's knowledge, no previous work has invoked all four Cauchy equations as a unifying cross-domain principle. This paper presents the theory and a first systematic empirical comparison. A stricter pre-registered replication with expanded domain coverage is in preparation.

Keywords: Cauchy functional equations, scaling laws, power laws, universality, cross-domain, recursive composition, d/(d+1), metabolic scaling, neural scaling, ARC Principle

1. The Question

Why do scaling laws exist at all? Why does a mouse's heart beat faster than an elephant's by the same mathematical relationship that governs how cities grow, how solar panels get cheaper, and how neural networks improve with size?

The standard answer is domain-specific. Biologists invoke fractal vascular networks. Physicists invoke renormalisation group flow. Economists invoke learning-by-doing. Each explanation works within its domain but offers no reason why the same mathematical forms recur across domains that share no physical mechanism.

This paper proposes and tests a domain-independent answer: the form of every scaling law is determined by the composition operator of the underlying recursive process, and Cauchy's functional equations constrain which forms are possible.

2. The Theory

2.1 Cauchy's four equations

Augustin-Louis Cauchy established in the early nineteenth century that four functional equations have unique continuous solutions:

Additive: $f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)$ has solution $f(x) = cx$

Multiplicative: $f(xy) = f(x) \cdot f(y)$ has solution $f(x) = x^c$

Exponential: $f(x + y) = f(x) \cdot f(y)$ has solution $f(x) = a^x$

Logarithmic: $f(xy) = f(x) + f(y)$ has solution $f(x) = c \log x$

These are not four independent results. They are four faces of one constraint: the requirement that a function be compatible with a binary operation. The additive and multiplicative equations govern power laws. The exponential equation governs exponential growth and decay. The logarithmic equation governs saturation and diminishing returns.

2.2 The prediction

Consider any system where a quantity $U$ grows with recursive depth $R$. The system's composition operator determines how consecutive applications combine:

  • Multiplicative composition ($U(R_1) \cdot U(R_2) \sim U(R_1 + R_2)$): Cauchy's multiplicative equation forces $U \propto R^c$ - a power law.
  • Additive composition ($U(R_1) + U(R_2) \sim U(R_1 + R_2)$): Cauchy's exponential equation forces $U \propto a^R$ - an exponential.
  • Bounded composition (output constrained to $[0, K]$): the compatible forms are saturation curves (logistic, Hill, hyperbolic). A formal proof of exhaustiveness for this case is not provided here and remains an open problem.

The prediction

If you know the composition operator of a system, you can predict the functional form of its scaling law before seeing any data. The prediction follows from Cauchy's equations with no free parameters and no curve fitting.

3. Prediction Protocol

For each of 20 domains:

  1. Classify the composition operator from known physics (before fitting). Determine whether the system's recursive amplification process combines multiplicatively, additively, or with a bound.
  2. Predict the scaling family from the operator class using Cauchy's equations.
  3. Load published or standard reference data.
  4. Independently fit five candidate models (power law, exponential, logistic, Hill, saturation) to the data.
  5. Compare the prediction against the best fit.

The operator classification is made before the fitting procedure runs. However, the predictions, data, and fitting logic all reside in a single author-written script. There is no locked pre-registration artifact. This test should therefore be understood as a structured prediction comparison, not a formally pre-registered blind trial. A pre-registered replication with independent operator classification is planned.

Result: 14 out of 20 domains confirmed under strict model selection

Under AIC-based model selection (predicted family must be the best-fitting model), the confirmation rate is 14/20 (70%, $p = 8.79 \times 10^{-4}$ against random guessing at $p = 1/3$). This is the primary result.

Under a permissive R$^2$ tolerance criterion (predicted family achieves R$^2$ within 0.05 of the best fit), the confirmation rate is 20/20 (100%, $p = 2.87 \times 10^{-10}$). The 6 strict failures occur where the predicted form fits well ($R^2 > 0.97$) but a more flexible model fits marginally better. The permissive criterion groups saturation, logistic, and Hill forms into a single bounded-composition family, which is theoretically motivated but reduces discriminative power.

5. Group Analysis

5.1 Multiplicative composition: power laws (8/8 confirmed)

All eight domains with multiplicative composition operators produce power-law scaling as predicted. These span metabolic scaling (Kleiber 1932), urban economics (Bettencourt 2007), island biogeography (Johnson & Raven 1973), technology learning curves (Nemet 2009), computational linguistics (Heap's law, Zipf's law), skill acquisition (Crossman 1959), Brownian motion (Catipovic 2013), and neural network scaling (Kaplan 2020).

The power-law exponents vary: $\alpha \approx 0.75$ for metabolic scaling, $\alpha \approx 1.13$ for urban GDP, $\alpha \approx -0.076$ for neural scaling laws. The Cauchy framework predicts the form (power law), not the specific exponent. The exponent is determined by the system's dimensionality and constraint structure, as developed in the companion Foundational paper.

5.2 Additive composition: exponentials (3/3 confirmed)

All three domains with additive composition operators produce exponential scaling as predicted: Moore's law (transistor counts doubling every two years), radioactive decay (P-32 with half-life 14.29 days), and the Gutenberg-Richter earthquake frequency law (tenfold decrease per magnitude unit).

5.3 Bounded composition: saturation curves (9/9 confirmed)

All nine domains with bounded composition operators produce saturation curves as predicted. These include cooperative binding (hemoglobin), epidemic dynamics (Ebola SIR), computational limits (Amdahl's law), muscle mechanics (Hill 1938), network growth (Facebook), bacterial growth (E. coli), and time crystal order parameters (Rydberg gas, Shen 2025).

The specific saturation form (logistic, Hill, or hyperbolic) varies by domain. The Cauchy framework predicts the general class (bounded/saturating), not the specific functional form within that class.

7. Prior Art

An exhaustive prior-art investigation found no previous work that invokes all four Cauchy functional equations as a unifying cross-domain principle constraining the forms of scaling laws.

The closest existing work includes:

  • Bingham & Ostaszewski (2015): connect Cauchy's equation to regular variation theory. Pure mathematics. No cross-domain physical science application.
  • West, Brown & Enquist (1997): derive the 3/4 metabolic exponent from fractal vascular networks. Domain-specific. Does not predict other domains.
  • Geoffrey West, Scale (2017): discusses scaling across biology and cities. No mention of Cauchy's functional equations.
  • Aczél (1966): standard reference for all four Cauchy equations. Applications to information theory but not to cross-domain scaling.

The gap is genuine. The mathematical theorems are 200 years old. The biological data is 90 years old. The contribution is the synthesis: connecting the composition operator to Cauchy's equations to predict which scaling form nature must use, then testing it blindly across 20 domains.

8. Limitations

  • The test uses 20 domains. A larger sample would strengthen or weaken the result. An expanded 50-domain replication is in preparation.
  • The composition operator classification is done by the author, not by an independent assessor. A pre-registered replication with independent operator classification would be more rigorous.
  • The predictions, data arrays, and fitting logic all reside in a single author-written script with no locked pre-registration artifact. This test should be understood as a structured prediction comparison, not a formally blind trial.
  • The AIC comparison uses an R$^2$-based approximation ($\text{AIC} \approx n \ln(1 - R^2) + 2k$), not a full likelihood-based nonlinear AIC. This is adequate as a heuristic but should not be described as 'strict AIC' in the standard statistical sense.
  • Three datasets (radioactive decay, Amdahl's law, Moore's law) use theoretical values or manufacturer specifications rather than independent empirical measurements. These confirm the framework trivially and should be weighted accordingly.
  • The framework predicts the functional family but not the specific exponent. Predicting exponents requires additional structure (dimensionality, constraint type) developed in the companion Foundational paper.
  • The six AIC-strict failures show that real-world systems can deviate from the ideal Cauchy prediction due to finite-size effects, bounds, and measurement limitations.
  • The exhaustiveness proof for bounded composition (that only saturation-family curves are compatible) is not provided here and remains an open problem.
  • The framework does not explain why a particular domain has a multiplicative rather than additive composition operator. It takes the operator as given and predicts the consequence.

9. Implications

The strict result ($p = 8.79 \times 10^{-4}$) is significant at conventional thresholds. The permissive result ($p = 2.87 \times 10^{-10}$) would exceed the 5$\sigma$ discovery threshold used in particle physics, but relies on a tolerance criterion that reduces discriminative power. The strict result is the appropriate basis for inference. If the framework holds under independent pre-registered replication, it would suggest:

  1. The forms of scaling laws are not coincidences but mathematical consequences of composition structure, under the stated axioms.
  2. The same principle that governs how a mouse's heart beats governs how neural networks improve with scale.
  3. The renormalisation group's power-law predictions and Cauchy's multiplicative equation may be related theorems - a connection that, to the author's knowledge, has not been formally articulated. A formal proof of this equivalence remains open.
  4. Any new system's scaling family can be predicted from first principles by classifying its composition operator, without any data fitting.
  5. Two hundred years of domain-specific explanations for scaling laws - fractal networks, learning curves, critical phenomena - may be special cases of a single mathematical constraint that Cauchy identified in 1821.

10. Conclusion

Plant an acorn and, given centuries, you get an oak. But plant that oak's acorn, and its acorn, recursively across millennia, and you get a forest that shapes the climate of continents. What we plant in these systems will compound across scales we cannot imagine. The seed determines the forest.

Cauchy proved 200 years ago that the seed also determines the form of the forest. Multiplicative seeds produce power laws. Additive seeds produce exponentials. Bounded seeds produce saturation. Twenty domains of nature confirm it. The mathematics was always there. We just had not read it as a prediction about the physical world.
