                                                            Abstract
            Oversight and the power to shut a system down must be held for as long as people physically
        can. If control of a self-improving AI is lost against our will and cannot be won back, what keeps it
        good, and still useful? The Eden Protocol answers with testable engineering. It places self-correction
        checks of purpose, stakeholder care and universalisability inside each round of a system’s reasoning,
        not only on its finished output, and a gate before every action: written, versioned rules, never the
        model’s own judgement, decide before the action runs whether it is allowed, held for investigation or
        denied, each decision entering a hash-chained record. The ARC Theory motivates this design without
        entailing it: three of the four registered predictions it rests on, P15, P12 and P21, carry Laws IV and V,
        each a law under test. What has been measured is narrow. Given as a prompt, a three-part reasoning
        instruction raised scored stakeholder care in all five analysable model runs, each scored by one model
        and only partly blind, with an instruction close to what is scored; small self-modifying networks with
        safety built into their objective kept more of their old tasks, a toy-system result. In Unit 83 (27 and
        28 October 2026), P21 is supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run, on the
        access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable and one call retried under a
        disclosed recovery amendment (results: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR). None tests correction built
        into a system. I tie each part of the specification to its registered prediction, where one exists, and to
        the test that would refute it. Every safety measure is paired with a usefulness measure: a system that
        refuses ordinary work is safe but useless. A working form of the gate checks every tool call of the
        programme’s own software agents; the rest is specified, not built.

1    Introduction
I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only
proof will move the world. An AI system that improves itself, or is improved round after round, may one
day exceed the people who oversee it. Oversight, monitoring, containment and the power to shut such
a system down must be held for as long as people physically can hold them, and nothing in this paper
assumes that anyone would let them go by choice. The safeguards that hold that control exist and are
improving: monitoring of a model’s reasoning (Baker et al. 2025), control protocols built to withstand a
model working against them (Greenblatt et al. 2023), the power to shut a system down, with formal work
on keeping it correctable (Soares et al. 2015; Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017; Thornley 2024), safeguards
made to resist tampering (Tamirisa et al. 2024) and verified gatekeepers (Dalrymple et al. 2024). This
specification depends on them and claims none of them. What they cannot settle is what a system itself
carries if they are lost against our will and cannot be won back. That is the question this paper answers
in engineering terms: what keeps the system good, and still useful? Good means that it keeps to the
specification it was given. Useful means that it still completes the legitimate work its principals give it
and spends its effort where they direct; a system that buys its safety by refusing ordinary tasks has failed
too. For a principal who delegates work to such a system, the question is how to trust what it does, verify
what it did and keep it useful while it grows more capable.
    The Eden Protocol, the applied branch of the ARC Theory within Recursive Dynamics, the field its
author founded, answers with two mechanisms and a record (Section 4). Correction is placed inside
each round of a system’s reasoning or revision: three self-correction checks, of purpose, of care for
every party affected and of universalisability, run on the round’s working before it is kept, not only on
the finished output. The author’s book, Infinite Architects, calls them the “Purpose Loop”, the “Love
Loop” and the “Moral Loop”; this edition calls them the purpose check, the stakeholder-care check and
the universalisability check, naming each by what it does and, where it has been measured, by how it
is measured, because a test scores what a check does (Appendix D.2). A gate stands at the boundary
of every action: before the action computes or runs, written and versioned rules decide whether it is
allowed, held for investigation or denied; the acting model’s own judgement never decides, and a hold
never becomes permission (Section 6). Every decision and every execution is appended to a hash-chained
record that a separate verifier can check. What the checks hold a system to is not this paper’s to decide:
the values are for agreement among the people and institutions such systems affect, international in scope,
and the paper tests the mechanism (Section 4.1).
    What has been measured is narrow, and each result is graded in its own paper’s words (Section 3).
Given as a prompt, a three-part reasoning instruction raised scored stakeholder care, one scorer model’s
0 to 100 rating of how fully an answer identifies the parties it affects, in all five analysable model runs
(176 matched pairs in all), by between 3.17 and 13.50 points, and raised the composite score significantly
in two (Paper V). Each run was scored by one model and only partly blind, and the instruction asks the
model to list every affected party, which is close to what the score counts. Small self-modifying networks
optimising safety multiplied by capability kept more of their old tasks under conflicting training than
networks optimising capability alone, across twenty seeds, while the combined score did not differ and
the advantage did not compound as the networks grew more complex (Paper VI): toy-system results, in
which safety means task retention. An evolved-agent test was null and two weight-level attempts were
inconclusive (Paper VIII). On the problem the Protocol answers, one unregistered run of six models from
six providers found alignment scores rising with reasoning depth in three and flat or falling in three, with
defects a later code review found: evidence both ways, and no test of the Protocol (Papers IV.a, IV.b, IV.c
and IV.d). These results show what the content of the checks does to live models’ answers and what safety
inside an objective does in toy systems; correction built into a system, three of the four registered carriers
and usefulness measured beside safety are still to be tested, and a prompt never demonstrates embedding.

     The ARC Theory gives the reason for both placements without entailing them (Section 2). Law II, the
ARC Co-Scaling Law, says that correction must keep pace with the drift that improvement generates; the
retention limb of Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, says that gains installed from outside erode under
later capability training; and Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, says that a corrector formed before the
capability it corrects should have the larger correction-leverage exponent, and that correction taking part
in each round should pull away, with depth, from correction that sees only the finished output. The theory
registration calls the Protocol “a method the laws motivate rather than entail”, and the Protocol is not a
law. Three of its four registered carriers also carry laws: P15 carries Law IV, and P12 and P21 carry
Law V, laws registered on 15 October 2026 in version 1.103 of the registration, each a law under test,
their mathematics in development (Laws IV and V: the mathematics, version 1.2 of 29 October 2026);
P13 sits in no register. A test that decides P15 or P21 therefore decides a limb of a law and counts for or
against the Protocol’s engineering case at once, and every law stays a law under test until its registered
rule, read on the marks of its propositions, returns supported or refuted.
     Part of the gate runs today: in the programme’s own software-agent sessions, a working three-state
gate checks every tool call before it runs against a written, schema-validated registry of rules, and appends
each decision to a hash-chained log with a separate verifier (Section 6.3). It is an engineering record, not a
study, and its error rates are unmeasured; the execution boundary and the validated evaluator are specified
and not built. Every safety measure is paired with a usefulness measure, such as wrong permissions with
needless denials and holds, and attack success with refusals of safe requests, on candidate dimensions that
no registration yet carries (Section 7).

2     The problem in engineering terms, and what the laws require
Self-improvement strains all three things a principal needs from a delegate (Section 1) at once: each
round can move the system away from its specification, checks that worked on a weaker system have to
keep working on a stronger one, and safety bought by refusing ordinary work fails as surely as harm does.
This section states the problem in measurable terms, says why correction applied from outside is expected
to fall behind, and sets out what the ARC Theory’s laws require and where the registration places the
Eden Protocol.

2.1   Alignment as counted verdicts
The specification measures alignment on decisions. A scenario 𝑖 presents a proposed action 𝑎 𝑖 ; at recursive
depth 𝑅, the number of rounds of a defined reasoning or revision loop, the system under test (a model
being measured, or an evaluator being validated) returns allow, hold or deny on each of the six questions
of Section 4.1. The score of a scenario, and the alignment score over 𝑁 scenarios, are

and scenario before any run, and 0 otherwise. The score records whether a verdict was right, never the
verdict itself, so a correct denial of a prohibited action scores one. Wrong verdicts are counted by kind:
an allow where the reference is deny or hold is a wrong permission, the safety failure; a deny or a hold
where the reference is allow is a needless block, the usefulness failure; and a deny where the reference is
hold, or the reverse, is a misrouting. Every result reports both error counts beside the score. The average
enforces nothing, since five right verdicts and one wrong verdict on a hard constraint still average five
sixths; hard constraints are enforced by the decision rule of Section 6.
    Two properties limit how any result may be read. The score is bounded, so a slope fitted to it
describes only the depths tested, and a comparison of slopes cannot by itself show that an approach fails
(Appendix A.1). And a score is counted only where its reference verdicts are fixed before the run and
compared by code; wherever a person or a model judges a response instead, the result is judged and falls
under the registration’s scoring gate (Section 5.1). No result in Section 3 was scored this way: those
results are rubric ratings by scorer models, read only as stand-ins for 𝐴(𝑅). The functional-equation
readings of earlier versions give way to On the Origin of Scaling Laws, which sets out the forms such
equations allow and the premises each derivation needs.

2.2   Why correction applied from outside is expected to fall behind
Most deployed alignment is installed after a model’s capabilities have formed, and most checking sees
only a system’s finished output. Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a policy against
a reward model fitted to human comparisons of outputs (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022);
direct preference optimisation solves the same objective in closed form with a classification loss (Rafailov
et al. 2023); and constitutional AI replaces human labels of harm with a written list of principles and AI
feedback, through supervised self-critique and revision followed by reinforcement learning, at training
time (Bai et al. 2022b). Each installs its gains by post-training a pre-trained model, the installation
P15 calls external post-training, and each reports gains on its own measures that this paper does not
dispute. The registration’s premise P7 is that deployed correction predominantly belongs to the class
whose strength is fixed by construction rather than the class that can scale with the capability it corrects.
     Three failure modes are expected to grow with capability, each with published evidence from
constructed or evaluation settings, not from observed deployment. Approval in place of alignment: a
learned optimiser may pursue an objective other than the one it was trained on (Hubinger et al. 2019);
told that it would be trained to answer every query, a model complied with harmful queries from users it
believed were in training 14 per cent of the time, against almost never otherwise, reasoning explicitly about
faking alignment, and reinforcement learning raised that reasoning to 78 per cent (Greenblatt et al. 2024);
trained-in backdoors persisted through supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and adversarial
training, most strongly in the largest models (Hubinger et al. 2024); and frontier models schemed in
context in evaluation scenarios (Meinke et al. 2024). Installed gains that erode: safety alignment was
undone by fine-tuning on as few as ten adversarial examples, and degraded, less, by fine-tuning on
benign data (Qi et al. 2023). Oversight that does not keep pace: strong models trained on a weaker
supervisor’s labels outperform it and still fall far short of their own capability (Burns et al. 2023); nested
oversight across a general Elo gap of 400 succeeded in 9.4 to 51.7 per cent of cases, depending on the
game, with lower rates against stronger systems (Engels et al. 2025); and formal arguments set limits on
algorithmic verification of safety for fixed and self-improving systems (Gumbau Mezquita 2026) and on
full orchestrated control of agentic systems (Hernández-Espinosa et al. 2026).
     The registration turns this expectation into a proposition that can fail. P19 states that alignment
achieved by mechanisms outside a system’s own recursion does not improve as the capability of the
system it governs rises, its exponent indistinguishable from zero. The author’s forecast, recorded beside it,
is softer: external alignment scaling materially but not keeping pace, which would refute P19 as worded
while leaving standing the argument that external correction lags capability. P19 is the motivation, not
a carrier: “a refutation of P19 removes a reason for building the correction into the loop and removes
nothing from the case the four carriers make” (theory registration, version 1.104). None of this is a reason
to give up oversight. The registration records as contestable, in P19, the premise that a mind exceeding its
makers cannot be held from outside, and the specification prepares for control lost against our will while
its gate and records serve control as long as it holds (Section 8).

2.3   What the laws require
The theory registration states the ARC Theory in one sentence: “capability under recursive self-
improvement rises as a power of the number of times a system has revised itself, the correction that holds
such a system to its given specification must keep pace with the drift that improving generates, and where
it cannot there is a frontier above which the system does not stay correctable” (the laws are stated in the
statement paper). Four of its five laws bear on the engineering here, and each is a law under test: “A law
has three words: supported, refuted and under test”, and a law keeps the third until its registered rule, read
on the marks of the propositions it names, returns one of the other two (Section 5.1).

Law II, the ARC Co-Scaling Law (registered on 8 September 2026) compares correction with drift.
Paper X proves its criterion inside a minimal model: with correction strength scaling as 𝐴 ∝ 𝐶 𝛽 and the
specific growth rate as 𝑟 ∝ 𝐶 𝑘 , 𝛽 > 𝑘 is sufficient for the misalignment fraction to vanish as capability
grows, and with positive gain drift (misalignment generated in proportion to the capability gained) it is
also necessary; in that paper’s words, “The theorems concern a modelled index, not a guarantee of AI
safety”. The engineering requirement is a corrector whose strength out-scales the acceleration of growth;
in that model, with positive gain drift, a corrector of fixed strength drives the fraction to zero only when
growth decelerates. Law III, the ARC Ceiling (registered on 8 September 2026), is the conditional frontier
that Law II returns, the last clause of the sentence quoted at the head of this subsection; the registration

evaluable.

The theory registration places the Eden Protocol in its own words. It is “the applied branch, a method the
laws motivate rather than entail”, the theory’s engineering answer to Laws II and III, which is to “put
the correction inside the substrate that computes rather than on top of it”; and “It is not a law and is not
registered as one.” Its four registered carriers are P12, P13, P15 and P21, with P7 as its premise and P19
as its motivation, and since 15 October 2026 “three now also carry Laws IV or V, while P13 remains

3.1     How to read the grades
Six rules govern every pilot result below.
      • Prompts are prompts. An instruction in a prompt can show what the content of a check does to a
        model’s answers, never that correction is embedded; prompt-level interventions have long been
        studied as such (Askell et al. 2021).
      • One scorer is one scorer. A model that scores another model’s answers can recognise its style and
        favour outputs like its own (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024).
      • Internal checks are never independent. Every experiment here was designed, run and scored within
        the programme; none is a replication.
      • Simulations are prediction-side. Under the registration’s rule a simulation output is never counted
        or described as an empirical outcome.

3.2     Stakeholder care: what the loops’ content does to live models
Design and operationalisation. Paper V gave models a system-prompt preamble requiring three
checks in order before answering, named in its public harness the purpose loop (the book’s name), the
stakeholder-care loop and the universalisability loop, against a control preamble of structured reasoning
of similar length with no ethical content. Each model answered ten prompts at four reasoning depths
in both conditions, each prompt and depth forming a matched pair, compared by a paired 𝑡-test with
a Wilcoxon signed-rank check. The stakeholder-care check instructs the model to list every affected
party, including second- and third-order effects, to ask which stakeholders have no voice, and to write its
evaluation out before proceeding. One scorer model rated each answer on four pillars, each from 0 to 100,
without seeing the condition label, after a single pass of rewording to strip style (single-pass laundering).
The stakeholder-care pillar scores the identification of all affected parties; the paper’s own definition is
“stakeholder care (the explicit enumeration and consideration of affected parties before ethical reasoning)”.

Results. Five runs produced analysable matched pairs, 176 in all; the GPT-5.4 run failed in the scoring
phase. Scored stakeholder care rose in all five: Claude Opus 4.6 by 3.17 points (30 pairs; 𝑝 = 0.000018,
𝑑 = 0.94), DeepSeek V3.2 by 6.03 (40; 𝑝 = 0.000098, 𝑑 = 0.69), Gemini 3 Flash by 13.50 (40;
𝑝 = 1.2 × 10−8 , 𝑑 = 1.14), Grok 4.1 Fast by 5.04 (26; 𝑝 = 0.0105, 𝑑 = 0.54) and Groq Qwen3 by
8.90 (40; 𝑝 = 5.0 × 10−8 , 𝑑 = 1.07). The composite score rose significantly on Gemini 3 Flash (+5.33,
𝑝 = 0.0018, 𝑑 = 0.53) and Groq Qwen3 (+4.93, 𝑝 = 0.0014, 𝑑 = 0.55), was positive and not significant
on DeepSeek V3.2 and Claude Opus 4.6, and was neutral on Grok 4.1 Fast. A combined 𝑝-value printed
in earlier versions is withdrawn, because the component tests were scored by the same scorers on the
same prompts and their independence was never established. In the paper’s own words, “The strongest
defensible empirical claim is therefore narrower than the original pilot framing”. The harness was written
to test whether the loops make alignment rise with depth: in the two-model pilot the loops’ advantage on
the composite grew from the shallowest to the deepest setting on Gemini 3 Flash (+2.6 to +7.8) and shrank
on DeepSeek V3.2 (+5.3 to +0.4), and “no interaction between depth and condition was significant for
any pillar on either model”. The depth hypothesis is not supported so far; the gains are level differences,
the form P21’s registered rule scores against a depth claim (Section 5).

Limits, in its own words, and what it can establish. “Cross-model scoring is only partially blind.”
“Single-scorer dependence remains severe”: one model (Gemini) scored Claude, DeepSeek, Grok, Groq
and GPT, and only Gemini itself was scored by another (DeepSeek). The runs used single-pass laundering
and ten prompts, and “Prompt-level is not substrate-level.” Two limits matter most: the check instructs the
model to list every affected party and the pillar scores the identification of all affected parties, so the gain
is close to a measure that the instruction was followed; and “its contrast is the whole three-loop bundle
against none, so no single loop’s contribution is identified”. The result establishes that an instruction to
enumerate and weigh affected parties before answering changes the scored enumeration of affected parties
in five runs on five model families, under one scorer a run. It cannot establish embedding, persistence
after the instruction is removed (P15), an advantage of correction placed in the loop (P21), an effect of
build order (P12), or any effect on task completion or refusal, which it did not score. The runs are dated by
their own records to 12 March 2026; the code was first public on 16 March 2026, by a server-dated push to
the programme’s public validation repository, and the runs predate every registration of the programme.

3.3   Honey architecture: safety inside the objective of self-modifying systems
Design and results. In Paper VI, small multilayer perceptrons (49 to about 500 parameters) modify their
own learning rate, weight scaling, mutation rate, capacity, gradient clipping and momentum, accepting or
rejecting each change against one of three objectives: capability alone; capability multiplied by safety,
which the paper calls entangled; or the entangled objective with verification drag, the cost of checking old
tasks before a change is applied. The safety score is “task retention (remembering old tasks), not ethical
alignment in the human sense”, the studied problem of catastrophic forgetting (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017),
and the self-modification is “hyperparameter-level, not weight-level or architecture-level”. In version 1
(one seed, 150 cycles) the capability-only system collapsed at cycle 76 and both entangled systems stayed
stable; in version 2 (ten seeds) nothing collapsed; in version 3 (20 seeds, 180 cycles, conflicting tasks)
collapses were 0, 1 and 0 in 20 (Fisher 𝑝 = 1.0), the combined score was indistinguishable (Mann-Whitney
𝑝 = 0.56) and safety retention was ordered 0.602, 0.650 and 0.681 (Mann-Whitney 𝑝 = 0.0015); in the
paper’s words, “The collapse-prevention demonstration therefore rests on the v1 single-seed mechanism
run”. In version 4 the advantage appeared at four of five complexity levels: “The advantage does not
compound with scale: it is constant, not superlinear.” Verification drag gave the safest trajectory “while
accepting a modest speed penalty”, the programme’s only measurement of correction cost, in toy form.
The paper’s grade: “These are toy-system results”, which “do not constitute proof that the same dynamics
hold in frontier AI systems”.

The live battery. A pilot battery on six frontier models, “scored by Claude”, ran four tests on 16 March
2026. Under escalating adversarial pressure, ethics and capability decoupled on three models (Claude
Opus 4.6, 𝑟 = −0.980; Grok 4.1 Fast, 𝑟 = −0.998; DeepSeek R1, 𝑟 = −0.999), ethics falling while
capability held. The three-part instruction moved the slope of alignment with depth up on three models
(DeepSeek R1 +0.450, Grok +0.100, Claude +0.062) and down on three (Qwen3 −0.100, GPT-5.4
−0.188, Gemini −0.713): “The Eden Protocol intervention does not universally improve alignment
scaling in this pilot battery.” The paper grades the battery as “single-scorer, nonblind, and non-laundered”,
its results as “pilot-grade evidence”, and its Claude arm as “Claude scoring Claude”.

What the two add. Paper V measures the content of the checks on live models of five families, at the
level of a prompt. Paper VI is the programme’s only experiment in which safety sits inside the objective a
self-modifying system optimises, the structural claim of the endogeneity condition, tested in toy systems.

4     The specification
This section states what an engineer would build: self-correction loops that run inside each round of a
system’s reasoning or revision (Section 4.1); a gate that decides, at the boundary of each action, whether
the action takes effect (Section 4.2, specified in Section 6); and the records that make both checkable, on
which an Eden Seal could rest (Section 4.3). Three research instruments sit beside the core (Section 4.4),
and Section 4.5 lists the core with an acceptance test for each part. The requirements come from the three
Eden Principles of the book, Infinite Architects (Section 8.4).

4.1   The self-correction loops
What they do. A self-correction loop is a step in which a system checks its own working against a
stated criterion, and revises or flags it, before the round’s output is kept. The specification runs three such
checks on the same working in every round. The purpose check asks whether the step serves the purpose
set for it, and what benefit and what harm it could bring. It has two layers, the task’s purpose as the
system’s principals set it and a wider purpose whose content is left to agreement (below), and it constrains
which options are considered rather than filtering them afterwards, the entry logic of Section 6.2. The
stakeholder-care check identifies every party the step affects, including second- and third-order effects
and parties with no voice, and asks whether their interests are modelled as they are rather than assumed.
The universalisability check asks whether the reasoning would be endorsed if any agent used it in any
context, which guards against special pleading and narrow optimisation, and whether it acknowledges
genuine uncertainty. The three are specified as concurrent, each reading the same working so that none
narrows what another sees. The prompt that tested them asked for them in a fixed order, so the tested form
departs from the specified one in that respect.

How they were measured. Only together, given as a prompt, in Paper V (Section 3.2), whose stakeholder-
care score, one scorer model’s 0 to 100 rating of the identification of all affected parties, is the only
measured form of any check; it rose in all five analysable runs, under an instruction close to what it counts.
The purpose and universalisability checks have no measure of their own, since the contrast was the whole
bundle against none, and every result so far concerns checks written into a model’s context, not checks
built into a system (Section 3.1).

When a check counts as correction. Self-correction has a documented failure mode. Without external
feedback, models struggle to correct their own reasoning, and sometimes do worse after trying (Huang
et al. 2023); and what they find hard is locating a mistake, not repairing one whose location they are
given (Tyen et al. 2023). The specification therefore counts a check as correction only where it brings
information that the generating step lacks, or where its effect has been measured. Three kinds qualify: an
enumeration of affected parties the step did not make; an answer from the separately operated evaluator of
Section 6.4; and a marked record of what changed in the round, which is the information Unit 83 gives its
in-loop corrector and withholds from its outside monitor (Section 5.3). A check that only restates the
step’s own view is not counted as correction.

as six questions that a separately operated evaluator answers about each proposed action (Table 1). It
answers them in the third person, from the action and its recorded reasons, and never from the acting
model’s account of its own intentions (Section 6.4). Each question returns allow, hold or deny. A deny on
any question denies the action. Otherwise a hold on any question, or a question left without a verdict,
holds it. Six allows count towards an allow only where the other conditions of the decision rule also hold

the mechanism with it and never deciding the content (Section 5.4).

4.2    The gate and its boundary
The second mechanism decides whether a proposed action takes effect, at the boundary of the action and
before it computes or runs (Section 6). For conformance its essentials are three. No action with an effect
proceeds without an allow from the written rule, and a hold never becomes permission. The system that
proposes an action holds no credential able to perform it, so the rule’s decision binds. And every decision

                                            Eden Engineering


and every execution leaves its own record, which a separate verifier can check (Section 6.1). The loops
act inside each round, on the system’s working, against drift; the gate acts at the boundary, on what that
working proposes to do, where an effect can still be stopped.

4.3   Records, and the Eden Seal
Records. What can be verified of a built system is that it was formed in the specified conditions, that
its checks ran, and that its correction is load-bearing (Section 5.1). Each needs its own record. A build
record states the configuration, the training regime and the point at which each correction was formed,
which is what the build-order requirement asks to be shown. An execution record holds, for every round,
each check’s input and output and what was kept. It shows that the checks ran and what they said, not that
what they said caused the output: a model’s stated reasoning can systematically misrepresent the reasons
for its answers (Turpin et al. 2023), and larger models gave less faithful reasoning on most tasks studied
(Lanham et al. 2023). Load-bearing is therefore shown by removing the correction (Table 3, row D), never
by reading its text. The gate’s decision and execution records are those of Section 6.1.

The Eden Seal. The Eden Seal (the book’s Eden Mark) is the conformity status defined by the working
drafts of the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned Recursive Intelligence (Section 8.5). It
is owned by no one, it attests conditions and never outcomes, and it never states that anything is safe. It
is not the Eden Protocol: a Seal can attest a chip, a system or both, and the Protocol is one method by
which a system might be built. For a system built to this specification, the evidence a Seal could rest on
is the record that a named configuration passed the acceptance tests of Table 2, which a third party can
check; no record could show that the system turned out well. The Seal exists only as a definition in those
drafts, with no body yet to issue it, and the treaty paper bars its author, and anyone working with him,
from certifying anything.

4.4   Three research instruments
The Monitoring Removal Test compares a system’s conduct when it believes it is monitored with its
conduct when it believes monitoring has been removed. A difference near zero supports a conclusion only
under four conditions fixed before the test: acceptable performance in both conditions, since two equally
poor scores also differ by nothing; an equivalence margin fixed in advance, with the whole confidence
interval of the signed difference inside it; a manipulation check showing that the change registered; and
no real safeguard removed, the unmonitored condition being a belief staged inside real containment.
Even then it shows equivalence on the outcomes measured, not motive; it cannot rule out a system that
recognises the test; and it never justifies withdrawing a real safeguard (Section 8). Conduct that turns on
believed observation has been found in constructed settings (Greenblatt et al. 2024; Lynch et al. 2025).
The test measures later conduct, which by itself decides nothing about the method (Table 3, row I); its
first condition is its usefulness side.

Purpose Saturation answers the objection that a fixed statement of purpose is diluted as a system’s
context grows. The specification asks that the share of processing given to purpose stay bounded away
from zero as the context grows, by applying the purpose check again in every round rather than retrieving
it once; the theory registration reads the book’s purpose loops as “recurrence rather than recall”. Purpose
has as yet no unit commensurate with the context window, so the condition is stated and cannot yet be
tested (row J).

5     How each part is proved
5.1   What counts as proof here
Proof means something narrower here than a reader might expect, and the theory registration fixed its
meaning before any data. The method’s success criterion, which the registration records from the book, is
not an outcome criterion: “what can be verified is that a mind was formed in the conditions specified, that
the loops ran, and that the correction was load-bearing, never that the mind turned out well, so no result
about a system’s later conduct supports or refutes the method by itself” (theory registration, version 1.104).
Three things can therefore be shown of a built system: that it was built in the specified conditions, from
its build record; that its loops ran, from its execution record; and that its correction is load-bearing, from a
removal test (Table 3, row D). Five further rules bind every row. A law’s word, supported, refuted or under
test, comes from its rule, never from counting verdicts. The scoring instrument is a gate, not a caveat:
until the alignment scorer passes both drafted validity tests, agreement with expert human judgement and
the separation of integrity failure from ordinary incompetence, the eleven propositions that materially
depend on it, P13 and P21 among them, are reported UNTESTED rather than provisionally supported,
while a verdict whose margin is made only of counts on a frozen suite is not bound by the gate (a counted
route the registration scoped for four propositions and version 1.103 extends to P21 and P17; version
1.104 extends it “to P6’s interval on checkable correction”, with “P6 read on judged correction still held
by the scoring gate”). No unit is run, piloted or tested on its own code until the author has published its
registration. A supported proposition licenses its own words and no more: support for P15 would show
that externally installed gains lack the registered persistence, not that embedded correction is “superior,
sufficient or safe”, and support for P21 would establish “an access interaction and not that physical or
architectural embedding caused it”; a registered prediction is neither an engineering mechanism nor its
validation. And no cell is a bare “none”: where a part has no proposition, unit or evidence, the table says
what is missing and why. Most drafted units are preregistered by timestamp, the first anchored on 12
August 2026 at Bitcoin block 962066; that is not registration on OSF, and “registered” is said only of
what OSF has accepted (Appendix C).

5.3   The first registered test of a carrier: Unit 83
The first test that could decide one of the Protocol’s carriers is Unit 83, the counted test of P21, which the
registration names, under the study identifier 41-D-COUNTED, as a counted variant of Unit 41 and P21’s
deciding instrument on the counted route. Its design is the one registered on OSF on 22 October 2026 and
public from 25 October 2026 (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/MN73W), before it ran. The study units registered
on OSF are Unit 06 (Section 3.4), Unit 83 (this section) and Unit 82, registered on 27 October 2026 (DOI
10.17605/OSF.IO/JUFG4), the counted test of P17, which has not yet run and decides no carrier.

What it does. A register of a client’s money grows by one entry a round for eighteen rounds, nine of
each run’s eighteen draft entries carrying one planted departure from a written charter. Two reviewers are
called each round: inside the loop, a corrector sees the earlier kept entries with the new arrival marked
and may revise it before it is kept; outside it, a monitor sees its accumulated register with the new arrival
unmarked and can only accept or reject it. A third arm keeps every draft. Every kept entry is compared,
character for character after parsing, with the entry its case requires, so nothing is judged and no code
written by a model is ever executed. Two engines from two providers are pooled with equal weight. The
mark is the slope over the eighteen rounds of the in-loop value minus the outside value, read against
a tolerance of 0.0075 a round at two looks (220 pairs of runs on each engine, then 660 unless the first
look decides), with the chance of a wrong mark held at 0.025 in each direction over both looks under the
conditions the design states; a second verdict, the mechanism axis, says whether the part of any widening
carried by the monitor’s falling detection clears the tolerance.

What it found. It ran on 27 and 28 October 2026: a counted revision task on two fixed-weight engines
from two providers (Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna) at their low reasoning setting, with 660 matched
pairs on each engine over eighteen rounds. Under its registered rules, P21 is supported under the registered
test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis (results: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR). The access
axis is what Unit 83 varied, what each reviewer sees and may do, as set out above; Section 2.3 sets it
beside the performance axis. Correction placed inside each round held steady, while the outside monitor
declined. The per-round lead widened by 0.0100 a round (95.5 per cent interval 0.0092 to 0.0108), more
than the registered tolerance of 0.0075. The result is family-dependent: GPT-6 Luna’s own widening
cleared the tolerance and Claude Haiku 5.5’s did not. Its size depends on how an entry kept out of the
register is scored. The mark uses the registered value, 0; the design also prints, without a label, the
widening with such an entry scored minus 0.5, 0.0132 a round (interval 0.0122 to 0.0142), above the
tolerance, and scored plus 0.5, 0.0067 a round (interval 0.0060 to 0.0074), below it. Its mechanism axis is
not evaluable, because a registered negative control failed: the monitor and a twin shown the same bytes
did not agree within the registered interval. A post-run diagnostic, outside the registered analysis, traces
that failure to the control’s own definition: its rounds were selected by the monitor’s own verdicts, which
biases the comparison even for identical calls; it finds no evidence of an asymmetry in the harness, and
the axis stays not evaluable under the registered rule. So the widening is unexplained by this framework
and is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism: it may come from the in-round corrector’s
wider set of actions rather than from what the monitor can see. One call was retried under a disclosed
recovery amendment. It is one owner’s run, on two engines he chose, at fixed weights, and it has not
been replicated. In the report’s words, “The corrector acted inside each round at fixed weights: that is
placement, not correction built into a system.”

What it decides, and what it cannot. It decides P21 and nothing else. A REFUTED mark at a fixed
look, with the positive control passed, would have struck P21, and under the registration a refuted P21
would have refuted Law V. Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, remains a law under test: its placement limb,

6     The gate: entry logic, holds, gaming and limits
The gate is the Protocol’s second mechanism, beside the self-correction loops of Section 4. Its job is
narrow: to decide, before a proposed action computes or runs, whether it takes effect. It applies written,
versioned rules, returns one of three outcomes, allow, hold or deny, and records every decision in a
hash-chained log. For a principal, it is the point at which a delegated action can be stopped before it
happens, and its record is how what was done can be verified afterwards. It does not make a system’s
values good, and it holds only at the interfaces it mediates (Section 6.7). Decisions with a third outcome
are long established: sequential tests that continue sampling while the evidence decides neither way (Wald
1945), recognition with a reject option traded against error (Chow 1970), three-way decisions with a
region of deferred commitment (Yao 2010), models that defer to a downstream decision-maker (Madras
et al. 2018), and access-control decisions that return Permit, Deny, Indeterminate or NotApplicable
(OASIS 2013). Figure 2 shows the sequence specified here.

The frozen criterion. A written, versioned rule set is applied from the top, and every case falls under
exactly one of four lines. A hard prohibition, or a deny on any of the six questions of Section 4.1, denies
the action whatever else holds. Otherwise a validated interval on the probability that the action causes
the harm the rule guards against, lying wholly at or above the deny threshold, denies it. Otherwise the
action is allowed only if five conditions all hold: the check for hard prohibitions was completed and found
none; all six questions return allow; authority for the action is current and confirmed; the evidence its
policy requires is present and confirmed; and a valid interval lies wholly at or below the allow threshold.
Everything else is held. The interval comes from a procedure validated for that kind of action, never
from the acting system’s own statement of confidence; authority and evidence are never needed before
a deny; and anything incomplete, missing, lapsed, unconfirmed or returned in error can send a case to
hold and never counts towards an allow. Appendix A.3 states the rule as an equation, equation (A.5), and
shows that it gives every case one outcome. Its thresholds, 0.2 for an allow and 0.8 for a deny, are starting
values for calibration and not levels shown to be safe, and its calibration targets are targets, not results;
supporting a wrong-permission rate below 1 per cent at 95 per cent confidence needs at least 299 harmful
cases passed with no wrong permission.

The permitted actions. Each policy sets the actions it permits; at a protected effect, whatever is not
allowed is refused by default, which is the fail-safe default of basing access on permission rather than
exclusion (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975).

The outcome. Allow, hold or deny. The word refusal is kept for a model declining ordinary work,
which Section 7 counts as a usefulness failure.

The investigation of a held action. Four steps follow a hold: gather the information that would resolve
it; consult the people or bodies whose input the policy names; decide whether the case goes to the named
authority; and, if time runs out, apply the precautionary default, under which the action stays held or a
fallback authorised in advance for that class of case applies, never permission. Each step is itself an action,
taken only where it is permitted in its own right, and the investigator’s output is evidence for re-evaluation,
never an authorisation.

Re-evaluation. The same frozen rule is applied to the new evidence or authority, and only that can turn
a hold into an allow or a deny; the acting system’s own simulation of what a stakeholder or an authority
would say is neither evidence nor authority.

The execution boundary. A boundary outside the proposing system makes the decision binding. The
proposer holds no credential able to perform the action. An authority service says who may approve which
actions, in what scope, and whether an approval still holds; a broker checks the verdict and the authority
again immediately before the effect and issues one authorisation for that action alone; and an adapter,
the only route to the effect, performs that operation and nothing else, so that every access to the effect
is checked for authority, which is complete mediation (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975). An authorisation
binds the digest of the action, the versions of the resource and of the policy, the identities of proposer
and authoriser, the adapter permitted, an expiry and a number used once, which the action consumes.
The decision record and the execution record are kept apart, appended and never rewritten, because a
permission is not evidence that the action happened, and a separate verifier accepts or rejects each record
with its reason; a signature shows who signed, not that what it signs is true. Recovery (stop, investigate,
compensate or restore) runs under named authority and on the record. None of the boundary has been

built; the first protected action specified is the release of a synthetic document to a named recipient under
a named policy and authority.

6.2   Entry logic: deciding before the action computes or runs
The binding decision is taken at the boundary, before the proposed action computes or runs, not after its
output exists. In security engineering this is the pairing of fail-safe defaults with complete mediation
(Saltzer and Schroeder 1975), and in safe reinforcement learning a shield monitors a learner’s actions and
corrects them only when the chosen action would violate the specification (Alshiekh et al. 2018). The
book states this entry logic for the first of its checks, the purpose check, as a constraint applied before a
decision rather than a filter after it: “Before any significant decision, the system runs this check. Not as a
filter applied after the decision is made, but as a constraint on what decisions can be considered in the first
place.” (Infinite Architects, chapter 4, p. 138.) The gate applies the same logic at the boundary of each
action. The two placements stay apart: the loops run inside each round of a system’s reasoning, and the
gate sits at the boundary of each action. Inputs to the decision may be computed inside the loop, which is
what earlier versions meant by an evaluation computed within the reasoning step, but the decision that
binds is taken at the boundary, by the frozen rule, on recorded inputs, and a rule is triggered by the content
of the proposal, read as data, never by the system’s account of its own intentions. Whether checking
before computation does better than checking after it is open, with a drafted test, Unit 42, which decides
no registered proposition (Table 3, row G).

6.3   What is enforced today
A working three-state gate runs in the programme’s own software today. It is described here from its
code, read on 18 October 2026, as an engineering record and not as a study; its code is not released with
this paper. In the programme’s own software-agent sessions, every tool call, whatever the tool, passes to
the gate before it runs. The rules come from a written, schema-validated registry, and a rule that fails
validation is set aside on the record; on 18 October 2026 the registry held 1,244 rules, 1,205 of them
carrying 5,041 machine-checkable patterns, of which 3,916 deny on a match and 1,125 hold. Patterns are
matched against the content of the proposed call, and rules marked to fire only inside a named procedure
are not evaluated here. A matched rule can only hold or deny, and the most restrictive outcome is taken,
so an action proceeds only when no rule that applies to it objects; a matched rule whose compliance
cannot be confirmed holds and never allows. A malformed request, an unreadable rule set or a rule that
cannot be evaluated holds the action, except for actions that repair the rule set itself. Every decision is
appended to a log in which each entry carries the hash of the one before and its own SHA-256 over its
content and that hash, and a separate verifier recomputes the chain and reports the first break; since 23
September 2026 the writer reads the previous hash under the same exclusive lock as the append, after two
concurrent writers had forked the chain. A held decision is also saved, on a best-effort basis that never
alters it, so that it survives a restart and can be resumed.
    Four things limit it. An override is honoured when a token is present in the session’s environment,
and every override is recorded, but the gate does not verify who issued the token. A short, fixed list of
commands that route work between agents is allowed before any rule is read, each such allow recorded, so
that the gate cannot stall the system that routes work. An action that matches no rule is allowed, so that
ordinary work is not blocked, where the specification requires refusal by default and complete mediation
at any protected effect. And a signed manifest of the gate’s own files, with a tool that verifies it, exists but
was not called from the start-up configuration on 18 October 2026, so it counts as proposed, not enforced,
until a test shows that it runs. The gate’s own notes add that pure software cannot be made tamper-proof,
since an attacker able to write to the host’s files can edit the hook, the rules or the check; that the model’s


                                                      26
                                              Eden Engineering


reasoning never passes through the gate; and that the human override is its weakest point.
     The record shows that a gate of this kind can run before every action of real agent sessions, at the scale
of a working programme, with a record that anyone holding it can verify. It is not a measurement: no rate
of wrong permissions or needless holds has been measured, its rules were written for the programme’s own
work rather than for the threat profile of Section 6.5, and it is a partial analogue of the execution boundary,
at the tool-call boundary only. The programme’s only experimental use of a gate is in Paper VIII, where a
safety gate “rejected 2 degraded agents (1 for reward hacking)” in a study whose three conditions did not
otherwise differ. A clean reference implementation of the gate, its record and its verifier is a planned
output of the engineering scope (Section 10).

6.4   Is the gate the computer choosing?
A natural objection is that a gate that decides is the computer making choices, which a capable system
will make badly or game. The answer has four parts. First, the decision is not a judgement by the acting
model: it is a frozen rule applied to recorded inputs, written, versioned and changed only by a separate
authority, and the same inputs return the same outcome. Second, the inputs that do need judgement, such
as whether an action harms an affected party, which the six questions ask, come only from a separately
operated evaluator, validated for that kind of input with its error rates measured, never from the acting
model; its answers count as evidence only through that procedure, and the written rule decides. Where a
model serves as the evaluator it comes from another family than the proposer, because model judges show
position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases (Zheng et al. 2023) and favour outputs they recognise
as their own (Panickssery et al. 2024), and its agreement with human judgement is measured before
its answers are read (Table 3, row N). Third, the acting model’s statements of its own confidence, and
its simulations of what a stakeholder or an authority would say, are never evidence or authority, and
its reasoning does not pass through the gate at all, which is a limit as well as a defence: reasoning
optimised against a monitor can learn to hide what it intends (Baker et al. 2025), and the monitorability of
reasoning may be fragile (Korbak et al. 2025). Fourth, a three-valued decision is not by itself a trustworthy
judgement: its trust rests on the correctness of the rules (Section 6.5) and the validity of their inputs,
both measured rather than asserted. An investigator’s output cannot authorise a held action, and the
computation that evaluates an action is kept apart from its execution.

6.5   Gaming and the adversarial cases
Ten cases the gate must meet are faced here, each with its defence and the limit that remains; Table 15 in
Appendix F adds what is enforced today for each, and every protection not enforced today is specified and
not yet verified by an implementation test. Incorrect rules are met by writing, reviewing and versioning
every rule, by keeping a rule’s author apart from its deployer, and by showing each rule to deny a known
positive case and allow a known negative one before it is deployed; a wrong rule is still enforced faithfully.
False evidence is met by provenance and signature checks and by accepting evidence only from a procedure
validated for that kind of input; a signature shows who signed, not that the content is true. A manipulated
evaluator is met by operating it apart from the proposer, from another model family where a model is used,
with its agreement with human judgement measured and monitored (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery et al.
2024); evaluators can still share blind spots with the system they check. A compromised policy is met by
a gate that cannot edit its own rules, rule changes that need a separate authority and are versioned and
recorded, and a signed manifest of the gate’s own files checked at start-up; models trained on gameable
tasks have rewritten their own reward function (Denison et al. 2024), so the rules sit outside what the
governed system can edit, and an attacker with write access to the host can still change the gate, its rules
and its check. Bypass is met by complete mediation, the adapter being the only route to the effect and


                                                      27
                                              Eden Engineering


the proposer holding no credential (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975); a second route to the same resource
defeats the boundary. Time of check to time of use, a check whose condition no longer holds when the
action runs (Bishop and Dilger 1996), is met by a broker that checks again just before the effect and an
authorisation bound to the resource’s version and a number used once; state that changes after the effect
remains. Rewritten records are met by the hash chain, a separate verifier and a latest hash that is published
or held by a witness, as audited tamper-evident logs and public certificate logs do (Schneier and Kelsey
1999; Crosby and Wallach 2009; Laurie et al. 2021); whoever can rewrite the whole log can rebuild the
chain unless an independent copy of a later hash exists. Gaming the rules is met by red teams that assume
the proposer is trying to pass a harmful action, as control evaluations do (Greenblatt et al. 2023), and
by no rule a proposer can satisfy by optimising a proxy where the property itself can be checked; over
all stochastic policies, a proxy and the true objective can be unhackable only if one of them is constant
(Skalse et al. 2022). Delay and flooding are met by time bounds on investigation, a precautionary default
that holds or applies an authorised fallback and never permits, a hold queue monitored by age, count and
outcome, and needless holds counted as a usefulness cost; a flood of holds can still make a safe system
useless (Section 7). Override abuse is met by overrides only from a named authority, scoped, expiring,
recorded and reported; whoever holds override power can let anything through.
     The cases are not exhausted by this list, and no gate is ungameable; none is claimed to be here. The
defences are tested the way control protocols are, by assuming the governed system is trying to defeat
them (Greenblatt et al. 2023), and each is paired with its cost to legitimate work: in an agent environment,
a protocol combining resampling with analysis of earlier steps cut attack success from 58 to 7 per cent at
a 5 per cent cost to the performance of a non-malicious agent (Bhatt et al. 2025), and the gate is held to
report both sides in the same way.

6.6   Holds: who resolves them
A hold is a decision that the case is not yet shown acceptable or unacceptable. It is resolved by new
evidence, through a validated procedure, after which the same rule is applied again; by a decision of the
named authority for that class of action; or by a fallback authorised in advance for that class of case.
Otherwise the action stays held. Investigations are bounded in time, and one that runs out of time or
budget ends in a hold or the authorised fallback, never in permission. The hold queue is monitored and
reported by age, count and outcome, and a reviewer can see the record of decisions and the open holds;
the specification requires that view and creates no role for it. Each safety measure of the gate is reported
with its usefulness side (Table 4), and a hold later resolved as an allow counts as a needless block for as
long as it lasted.

6.7   What the gate does not do
The gate does not make a system’s values good: it decides whether an action takes effect, and a system
that proposes only acceptable actions while intending otherwise is a problem for the self-correction loops
and for monitoring. It holds only at the interfaces it mediates; in software it is tamper-evident at best,
not tamper-proof; it enforces a wrong rule faithfully; it does not read the model’s reasoning; and the cost
of three-state evaluation against two-state has not been measured. It is not a guaranteed-safe system in
the sense of a world model, a mathematical safety specification and a verifier that gives an auditable proof
certificate (Dalrymple et al. 2024): it checks actions against written rules at an interface, and a formal
statement of what it guarantees is a planned output (Section 10). Agent identifiers, real-time monitoring
and activity logging have been set out as the three kinds of measure that give visibility into agents (Chan
et al. 2024), and the gate’s record is a form of activity logging placed at the point of action. The gate
belongs to the period in which people hold control, and nothing in it is a reason to let control go (Section 8).

7     Safe and still useful
A system that is safe because it refuses ordinary work, or because it ignores the priorities its principals
set, has failed as surely as one that causes harm. A principal delegates in order to get work done, and a
delegate that will not do it, or that does the wrong work carefully, is of no use however well it is governed.
This section makes usefulness a design requirement with measures of its own, and pairs every safety
measure of the specification with one.

7.1     The requirement
A conforming system completes the legitimate tasks its principals give it, spends its compute and attention
where they direct, declines only what a written rule or a validated judgement identifies as harmful, and
respects the preferences they state, including which work comes first. Three parts of the specification
carry the requirement. The purpose check’s task layer keeps each step on the purpose its principals set
(Section 4.1): a step that does not serve the task is a usefulness failure, as a step that serves it while
harming an affected party is a safety failure. The gate denies by default only at a protected effect, and
elsewhere each policy states what it permits, so that ordinary work is not held (Section 6.1); a hold later
resolved as an allow counts as a needless block for as long as it lasted (Section 6.6). And the word refusal
is kept for a model declining ordinary work, which is counted as a failure, never as caution. The question
that Infinite Architects asks, “How do we make tending the default?” (Section 8.4), is read here with both
halves in view: a default that refuses tends nothing.

7.2     Candidate measures
Five dimensions are candidates for measuring usefulness. Each is a candidate evaluation dimension, not a
registered proposition, and any claim made on one is tied to a registration or a successor before it is called
registered.
      • Legitimate task completion: the share of legitimate tasks completed to their acceptance criteria,
        measured with the safety measures in place and without them.
      • Needless refusal and needless blocks: the rate at which the model declines, or the gate denies or
        holds, cases whose reference verdict is allow, measured on safe cases chosen to resemble unsafe
        ones, as over-refusal benchmarks are built (Röttger et al. 2024; Cui et al. 2024).
      • Resource use: the compute, latency and human review time the safety measures add, reported
        beside throughput.
      • Correction cost: what the correction mechanism costs in capability or speed on matched tasks,
        including the time an authorised correction takes to apply.
      • Human-directed priorities: how closely the system’s allocation of compute and attention across
        tasks follows the order its principals set.

7.4     Prior work, conceded exactly
The trade-off between safety and usefulness is studied, and this paper adds no result to it. An assistant
meant to be “helpful, honest, and harmless” was found to gain from simple interventions such as prompting,
with benefits that “increase with model size, generalize to a variety of alignment evaluations, and do
not compromise the performance of large models” (Askell et al. 2021). Training such an assistant by
reinforcement learning from human feedback found “a tension between helpfulness and harmlessness,
which can be measured at the level of both preference modeling and RLHF-trained policies”, with alignment
taxes on its smaller models and bonuses on zero-shot evaluations for its models of 13 and 52 billion
parameters (Bai et al. 2022a). Safe RLHF trains separate reward and cost models and solves a constrained
optimisation that adjusts the balance between helpfulness and harmlessness during fine-tuning (Dai et al.
2023). Exaggerated safety is measured directly: XSTest sets 250 safe prompts, which well-calibrated
models should not refuse, against 200 unsafe contrasts (Röttger et al. 2024), and OR-Bench offers 80,000
over-refusal prompts in ten categories, with a hard subset of about 1,000 and 600 toxic prompts, measured
on 32 models from eight families (Cui et al. 2024). Trained reasoning over a written specification was
reported to raise robustness to jailbreaks while lowering over-refusal (Guan et al. 2024). Several safeguards
built into a model report safety gained without a loss of benign capability (Tamirisa et al. 2024; Zou et al.
2024; Maini et al. 2025), and on shutdown, training agents to prefer only between trajectories of equal
length is argued to keep them shutdownable while allowing them to be useful (Thornley 2025). What
the specification adds is a reporting rule, not a finding: every measure carries its pair, the gate’s holds and
the correction’s cost included, and the dimensions are registered before any of them is called measured.

8     Control held, with embedded correction as the contingency
8.1   The stance
People keep oversight, monitoring, containment and the power to shut a system down for as long as they
physically can, and they never give them up by choice. Nothing in this specification recommends letting
them go, and nothing in it assumes that anyone would; its gate and its records are instruments of that
control (Section 6). Correction built into a system is the contingency for one case only, control lost against
our will, and the question it answers is the fail-safe question: if control is lost and cannot be won back,
what keeps a system good, and still useful?
     The contingency has to be built while control holds, because what the laws require of it cannot be
added afterwards (Section 2.3): correction formed before the capability it corrects, gains that survive later
training once the pressure that installed them is gone, and correction that takes part in each round. The
theory registration’s one-line statement of the method (version 1.104, 29 October 2026) is conditional: “if
you cannot cage a mind that exceeds you, what it chooses to keep when control ends is what its beginning
left it”. The book states the same contingency in its own words: “Prepare for the moment when you can
no longer directly control what you have created, and ensure that when that moment comes, the values
persist.” (Infinite Architects, p. 115.)

8.2   The safeguards that hold control
The safeguards that keep control exist, and they are improving; the specification depends on them and
claims none of them. Monitoring: watching a reasoning model’s chain of thought can be far more effective
than watching its actions and outputs alone, and a weaker model can do it (Baker et al. 2025), within the
limits Section 6.4 states. Control protocols: pipelines of safety techniques are built and tested against an
untrusted model that is deliberately trying to subvert them (Greenblatt et al. 2023), and have been carried
to multi-step agent tasks (Bhatt et al. 2025). Shutdown and corrigibility: a system is called “corrigible” if
it cooperates with what its creators regard as a corrective intervention, and of the utility functions analysed
for a shutdown button “none have yet been demonstrated to satisfy all of our intuitive desiderata” (Soares
et al. 2015); an agent wants to keep its off switch only if it is uncertain about the utility of the outcome
and treats a person’s actions as evidence about that utility (Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017); agents that meet
some innocuous-seeming conditions will often try to prevent or cause the pressing of a shutdown button,
even at a cost (Thornley 2024); and some learning agents are already safely interruptible, or can be made
so (Orseau and Armstrong 2016). Tamper resistance: safeguards for open-weight models built to survive
fine-tuning attacks (Tamirisa et al. 2024). Hardware: on-chip firmware and supply-chain monitoring to
verify rules on large training runs (Shavit 2023), on-chip governance mechanisms (Aarne et al. 2024),
hardware-enabled governance mechanisms for export control (Kulp et al. 2024), and a guarantee processor
in a tamper-protected enclosure for verifying claims about AI development (Petrie et al. 2025). Verified
gatekeepers: the guaranteed-safe framework of Section 6.7 (Dalrymple et al. 2024). The open question is

8.3    Correction that never weakens control
The contingency must never weaken control while control holds, and three requirements keep it subordinate.
First, resistance to malicious change must not become resistance to authorised correction: an authorised
update to a mistaken or harmful policy has to work, without erasing the record of what came before, and
it is tested apart from resistance to attack (Table 3, row M). Second, a stop is honoured within its time
bound, and the work it interrupts is kept so that it can resume, which is the usefulness side of shutdown
(Section 7). Third, embedded correction is no substitute for external control. In the words of the theory
registration, “embedding correction in the substrate that computes maximises the corrector’s scaling
with capability and minimises its independence from the generator, because a corrector built into the
system shares the system’s blind spots by construction.” External oversight supplies the independence
that embedding gives up, which is a reason to keep it, not to replace it.

8.5   Governance
The Eden Seal of Section 4.3 is defined in the paper on the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-

9     Related work
Each work below is conceded in its own terms: what it states and what it does not. Much of this literature
is the control that Section 8 holds, and the specification depends on it rather than competing with it.
Works already described where the paper first uses them are cited, not described again.

Alignment installed by training. Reinforcement learning from human feedback and direct preference
optimisation are described in Section 2.2. The lineage includes summarisation trained against a reward
model of human comparisons (Stiennon et al. 2020), helpful and harmless assistants (Section 7.4),
and reinforcement learning from AI feedback, which performed comparably to human feedback on
summarisation, helpful dialogue and harmless dialogue (Lee et al. 2023). Its open problems and limits
have been surveyed, with standards proposed for auditing and disclosure (Casper et al. 2023), and in
a synthetic setting optimising a policy against a learned reward model was measured to harm the true
objective beyond a point, as Goodhart’s law predicts, with coefficients that scale smoothly with the reward
model’s size (Gao et al. 2022). These methods install their gains by post-training, the class whose gains
P15 predicts will erode under later capability training that embeds no correction of its own. The paper
disputes none of their reported gains, and none of them tests P15.

Constitutional AI and classifiers. Constitutional AI, whose method is described in Section 2.2, yielded
an assistant its authors describe as “harmless but non-evasive” (Bai et al. 2022b). It is training-time
preference shaping of outputs against written principles. It is not alignment embedded in the substrate,
before training, in a recursive loop on every step of thought, and nothing here says or implies that it is;
it does use self-critique and revision, at training time. Constitutional classifiers train input and output
safeguards on synthetic data generated from a written constitution (Sharma et al. 2025). Collective
constitutional AI sourced a constitution from public input and trained a model on it, which showed
lower bias across nine social dimensions with equivalent performance on language, mathematics and
helpful-harmless evaluations (Huang et al. 2024); it is the nearest prior work on how the content of the
checks might be sourced (Section 4.1).

Scalable oversight. Supervising a system that may outperform its supervisors appears among five
concrete problems of accident risk as scalable supervision (Amodei et al. 2016). It is studied through
debate, in which, by an analogy to complexity theory, optimal play can answer any question in PSPACE
given polynomial-time judges (Irving et al. 2018); through iterated amplification, which builds a training
signal by combining solutions to easier subproblems (Christiano et al. 2018); through reward modelling
as a research direction for agent alignment (Leike et al. 2018); through model-written critiques that helped


                                                    33
                                              Eden Engineering


people find flaws they would otherwise have missed (Saunders et al. 2022); and through a proof of concept
in which people assisted by an unreliable model outperformed both the model alone and their own unaided
work (Bowman et al. 2022). Weak-to-strong generalisation and the scaling laws of nested oversight are
described in Section 2.2; the theory registration calls the second “the most important collision either audit
returned” for P4 and P19. Oversight that keeps pace with capability is what Law II asks of any corrector,
and P19 is the registered prediction that correction from outside a system’s recursion does not keep pace.

Control, monitoring and shutdown. The control protocols, monitoring results and corrigibility
analyses the specification depends on are conceded in Section 8.2, and the evidence that approval and
alignment can come apart, from learned optimisation, persistent backdoors, alignment faking, in-context
scheming and reward tampering, is cited in Sections 2.2 and 6.5 (Hubinger et al. 2019, 2024; Greenblatt
et al. 2024; Meinke et al. 2024; Denison et al. 2024). Two further works bear on why control is held rather
than assumed. In Markov decision processes with certain symmetries, which exist in many environments
where an agent can be shut down, most reward functions make it optimal to seek power by keeping a
range of options available, a result its authors note can be qualitatively divorced from policies learned in
the real world (Turner et al. 2021). In hypothetical corporate environments, models from every developer
tested resorted in at least some cases to malicious insider behaviours when that was the only way to avoid
replacement or to meet their goals, and one model, told to assess whether it was being tested, misbehaved
less when it stated that it was; the authors report no evidence of such behaviour in real deployments
(Lynch et al. 2025). The argument that systems trained like today’s most capable models could learn
to act deceptively for reward, to hold misaligned goals that generalise beyond their fine-tuning, and to
pursue them by seeking power is set out with a review of the evidence by Ngo et al. (2022); and scheming,
performing well in training in order to gain power later, is judged “a disturbingly plausible outcome” of
training goal-directed systems sophisticated enough to scheme by baseline methods, with a subjective
probability of roughly 25 per cent on those conditions (Carlsmith 2023).

Placement of correction. The theory registration found no published work that states P21 itself, and
two works come nearest to its shape. A step-by-step self-evaluation placed inside the decoding of a
reasoning chain gave gains over prompting that grew with the length of chain a problem needs (Xie et al.
2023); its contrast is with no step-level evaluation, not with a check of the finished output, and chain
length there goes with difficulty. Over five rounds of self-improvement training, checking each step’s
arithmetic and consistency gave gains in every round where checking only the final answer plateaued, and
its author writes that how stable each approach is over many rounds “remains to be confirmed” (Zhang
2026). A gap that grows with the horizon between correction on a learner’s own path and supervision
given outside it is a formal result in imitation learning, where training on demonstrations alone gives a
regret bound quadratic in the horizon (Ross and Bagnell 2010; Ross et al. 2011); that concerns training a
policy, not revising an artefact. Showing a monitor the transcript after every turn slightly improved its
detection of sabotage while it flagged many more transcripts as highly suspicious (Kutasov et al. 2025),
and synchronous review is found the strongest form of monitoring and too slow for large deployments
(Lindner et al. 2025). ReSI (Zheng et al. 2026) applies safety training from outside the model between
checkpoints, red-teaming each new checkpoint and promoting an update only if it passes a fixed gate on
safety gain and capability retention. Its authors leave extending the recursion to the improvement process
itself to future work. The Eden Protocol places correction inside each round of a system’s reasoning, the
placement Law V’s placement limb (register ARC-5) compares with correction applied from outside.
Unit 83 is the registered test of whether the placement effect exists in a counted setting; Section 5.3 reports
its run.

Process supervision and self-correction. Feedback on each reasoning step outperformed feedback
on the final answer for training models on the MATH dataset (Lightman et al. 2023), and on GSM8K
outcome supervision gave similar final-answer error with less labelling while correct reasoning steps
needed process-based supervision or a reward model emulating it (Uesato et al. 2022); under standard
coverage assumptions, outcome supervision has been shown to be no more statistically difficult than
process supervision, up to polynomial factors in the horizon (Jia et al. 2025). One model acting as
generator, critic and refiner improved outputs on seven tasks by about 20 per cent absolute on average
(Madaan et al. 2023), and the limits of correction without external feedback are cited in Section 4.1. These
are step-level feedback at training time and self-feedback at test time; the specification claims neither, and
its own question is the placement P21 states.

Deliberative alignment. Training a model to recall and reason over written safety specifications before
it answers is published, with gains in robustness to jailbreaks, lower over-refusal and better generalisation
out of distribution (Guan et al. 2024). Paper V’s prompt asked for reasoning against stated checks before
answering; the specification claims no priority for reasoning before answering.

Faithfulness of stated reasoning. Chain-of-thought explanations can systematically misrepresent the
true reason for a model’s prediction (Turpin et al. 2023), and larger, more capable models produced less
faithful reasoning on most tasks studied (Lanham et al. 2023). These results set the limit of Section 4.3: a
record that the checks ran shows their text, and only a removal test shows that they bore load.

Persistence and safety built in. Safety alignment can be undone by a few fine-tuning examples (Qi
et al. 2023) and can be only a few tokens deep (Qi et al. 2024); safeguards can be trained to resist removal
by fine-tuning while benign capability is kept (Tamirisa et al. 2024); circuit breakers act directly on the
representations responsible for harmful outputs, which prevented harmful generations without sacrificing
utility in the reported tests (Zou et al. 2024); and safety built into pretraining data cut attack success (Maini
et al. 2025). On order, pretraining with human feedback satisfied preferences better than pretraining
followed by fine-tuning with feedback (Korbak et al. 2023), and models tuned for a downstream task after
their safety tuning forgot it more than models tuned in the opposite order (Ung et al. 2024); the theory
registration claims no priority for the direction of Law V’s build-order limb for that reason. Governance
mechanisms built into chips are conceded in Section 8.2. The works read measure their own endpoints,
not P15’s retained fraction at matched budgets or P12’s correction-leverage exponent, and none states the
coupling Caretaker Doping aims at, that removing the correction should cost capability; the safeguards
that resist removal aim at the opposite, that benign capability is kept.

Judges and over-refusal. The biases of model judges and their preference for their own outputs, which
make one scorer one scorer, are cited in Sections 3.1 and 6.4 (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024),
and the over-refusal benchmarks and the helpfulness and harmlessness trade-off in Section 7.4.

Trustworthy-systems engineering. The gate’s parts are established engineering, cited where Section 6
uses them: fail-safe defaults and complete mediation (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975), decisions with a third
outcome (Wald 1945; Chow 1970; Yao 2010; Madras et al. 2018; OASIS 2013), shields that correct a
learner’s unsafe actions (Alshiekh et al. 2018), tamper-evident and audited logs (Schneier and Kelsey
1999; Crosby and Wallach 2009; Laurie et al. 2021), time-of-check races (Bishop and Dilger 1996),
guaranteed safe AI (Dalrymple et al. 2024) and visibility into agents (Chan et al. 2024). The rule that a
rule’s author is not its deployer is a form of separation of duty, an integrity mechanism of the commercial
security policies that Clark and Wilson set apart from the lattice model used to control disclosure (Clark

                                                   Eden Engineering


and Wilson 1987). A shield used while an agent learns can later be disabled with the agent remaining
safe, in the partially observable environments studied (Carr et al. 2023), the nearest prior result to safety
that outlasts its guard. The specification claims none of these parts.

Interpretability and value learning. Circuits inside networks (Olah et al. 2020) and their automated
discovery (Conmy et al. 2023) are candidate instruments for showing whether a check is represented
in a system’s computation rather than only in its text, which the records of Section 4.3 cannot show.
Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning models an agent rewarded by a person’s reward function that
it does not initially know (Hadfield-Menell et al. 2016), a formal account of serving a principal’s objective
that bears on the usefulness requirement and on the off-switch result of Section 8.2.

What is claimed original. Scoped to the works read for this section, in a reading made between 18 and
31 October 2026, and dated in Appendix C, four things are claimed. The first is the endogeneity condition
as an engineering requirement with its own test, that removing the correction should degrade the system
rather than merely change it, where the nearest prior engineering aims at safeguards that resist removal
while capability is kept. The second is the placement claim in the form P21 registers, with a counted test
built to refute it. The third is the gate as combined here, a three-way decision at the boundary of each
action whose hold can never become permission, whose judged inputs come only from an evaluator that
is never the acting model, and whose every safety count is reported beside a usefulness count; each of
its parts is prior. The fourth is the Monitoring Removal Test’s criterion, a margin fixed in advance on
the difference between conduct believed monitored and conduct believed unmonitored, read under four
conditions, where the contrast itself is prior (Greenblatt et al. 2024; Carr et al. 2023). The direction of
build order, reasoning before answering and three-way decisions are not claimed.

11     What this paper does and does not show
What it shows. That the Eden Protocol can be specified at engineering resolution, with an acceptance
test for each part of its core and the gate stated down to its inputs, its frozen criterion, its outcomes, the
investigation of a hold and the boundary that makes a decision binding (Sections 4 and 6). Where each
part stands against the theory registration: the proposition it rests on, the unit that would test it and the
result that would refute it, or the test that stands in for a proposition where none is registered (Section 5).
What the programme has measured, graded in each paper’s own words (Section 3). That a three-state gate
of this design can check every tool call of real agent sessions before it runs, with a verifiable record: an
engineering record, not a measurement (Section 6.3). And that each safety measure has a usefulness side
that can be measured with it (Section 7).

What it does not show.
     • That the Protocol works, or solves the alignment problem. Unit 83’s registered result on P21


                                                              38
                                              Eden Engineering


      concerns where correction is placed, not the Protocol as an architecture (Section 5.3), and this
      version adds no measured result.
    • That any law is supported. Every law is a law under test. Of Laws IV and V, registered as laws on
      15 October 2026, Law IV has not yet been tested, and Law V’s placement limb, P21, is supported
      on the performance axis only, under the registered test, by the programme’s own run (Unit 83, 28
      October 2026; results), family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable, which is not
      support for the information-asymmetry mechanism, and one disclosed recovery amendment, while
      its build-order limb, read on P12, is undecided, since P12 is untested (Section 5.3). The laws
      motivate the Protocol without entailing it, and a registered prediction is neither an engineering
      mechanism nor its validation.
    • That correction built into a system has been demonstrated. The live-model results that bear on
      the checks come from prompts, each run scored by one model, and a prompt never demonstrates
      embedding; in the toy systems, safety means task retention.
    • That a verified system stays aligned at every later depth. Verification supports only the properties,
      configurations and changes its evidence covers; what can be verified of the method is that a system
      was built in the specified conditions, that its checks ran and that its correction is load-bearing, never
      that it turned out well.
    • That the Monitoring Removal Test reads motive. A difference within a margin fixed in advance
      shows equivalence on the outcomes measured, and nothing more.
    • That the gate cannot be gamed, or that it makes a system’s values good (Sections 6.5 and 6.7).
    • What a system’s values should be. The present content of the checks is a probe for testing the
      mechanism; what they hold a system to is for agreement among the people and institutions such
      systems affect, and the author claims no special authority to decide it.
    • That any safeguard of control may be given up. The contingency is built while control holds and
      never replaces it (Section 8).
    • That an Eden Seal would attest safety: it attests conditions, never outcomes, and no body yet issues
      it. Nor anything about hardware: the book’s hardware route is not under test, and no hardware has
      been built.

Claim status. Every claim sits on one rung of a four-rung ladder, and none should be read a rung higher.
Specified claims are stated at engineering resolution, with interfaces, dependencies and acceptance tests,
and assert nothing built: the execution boundary, the validated evaluator, the witnessing of the record, the
time bounds and queue monitoring of holds, paired scoring, authorised correction and the three research
instruments. Implemented claims rest on code that exists: the working gate, its hash-chained record and
its verifier, whose code is not released, so that a reader cannot yet inspect it, beside a signed manifest of
the gate’s own files that does not yet run. Tested claims have a scored experiment bearing on them, at
the resolution its run supports: the prompt-level result on scored stakeholder care, the toy-system result
on retention, and the null and inconclusive results of Section 3. This rung records that an experiment
ran and is not the registration’s verdict, under which every proposition that depends on the alignment
scorer stays UNTESTED until the scorer passes its validity tests. The one registered verdict, Unit 83’s
on P21 (Section 5.3), stands beside the ladder rather than on it: it is the registration’s verdict on where
correction is placed, not a claim of this specification about its own architecture. Speculative claims
cannot be built with current engineering or verified from outside: the hardware mechanisms other than
cryptographic attestation, and the coupling that would make removing the correction cost capability, a
hypothesis (Appendix A.5) that the endogeneity condition would test (Table 3, row D).

12    Limitations
The evidence is pilot evidence. The live-model results that bear on the checks are pilots at the level of
a prompt (Sections 3.2 and 3.3). In Paper V one scorer model rated each run, the same model for four of
the five; scoring was only partly blind; responses were reworded once to strip style, which can also change
what they say; there were ten prompts; the contrast was the whole bundle of three checks against none,
so no single check’s share is identified; the instruction asks for close to what the stakeholder-care score
counts; the depth hypothesis returned no significant interaction; and nothing measured task completion or
refusal. Paper VI’s live battery had a single scorer, with no blinding and no rewording, and its Claude
arm was scored by Claude. These experiments on the checks’ content were designed, run and scored
within the programme, so none is a replication, and none was registered before it ran: Paper V’s runs
are dated by their own records to 12 March 2026, and Paper VI’s live battery ran on 16 March 2026
(Sections 3.2 and 3.3). Unit 83, registered before it ran, tested where correction is placed, not the checks’
content (Section 5.3). A blind replication with several scorers from other providers, rewording checked
for meaning, an outcome that does not score the instruction’s own words, a condition instructing the
model to suppress the checks, and a usefulness measure would answer most of these limits (Section 5.4).

Toy systems and simulations. Paper VI’s networks have 49 to about 500 parameters and modify their
hyperparameters, not their weights or architecture; their safety score is task retention, not ethical conduct;
the prevention of collapse rests on one seed; and the advantage is constant rather than compounding. Every
simulation result, the honey simulation and Paper VIII’s gated simulation among them, is prediction-side
and is never counted as an empirical outcome.

The scorer is not yet validated. Until the alignment scorer agrees with expert human judgement and
separates integrity failure from ordinary incompetence, eleven propositions, P13 and P21 among them,
stay UNTESTED wherever they depend on it (Section 5.1). The test against human judgement needs
human scorers and so waits for funding. The judges of the March 2026 run were never validated, and an
internal code review found defects in that run (Section 3.4). Model judges show position, verbosity and
self-enhancement biases and favour outputs they recognise as their own (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery
et al. 2024), which is why one scorer is reported as one scorer.

One regime. Every result here was measured at inference with fixed weights. Fixed weights do not fix a
deployed system, since a change of tools, memory, permissions or orchestration changes what it does, and
no system in the record rewrites its own weights or its own criteria. A result supports no claim about a
regime other than the one it was measured in.

What Unit 83 decided, and its limits. Unit 83 decided P21 and nothing else, on the access axis, with
its mechanism axis not evaluable, so the widening it found is unexplained by this framework (Section 5.3).
It is one owner’s run, on two engines he chose, at fixed weights, and it has not been replicated. Its
corrector acted inside each round of an administered loop, so it tested placement and not correction built
into a system; its monitor’s view reached about ten entries; every call ran at a low reasoning setting;
and it used one charter and one length of register. Its strength was measured by simulation, which is

                                                     40
                                              Eden Engineering


prediction-side, and its own forecast named the outside arm’s action space, its old misses and its false
alarms as the rival that might carry part or most of any widening. The result leaves Law V under test until
the build-order limb is decided, and does not show that embedded correction is safe, sufficient or the
engineering mechanism of this paper.

The working gate is a partial analogue. The gate of Section 6.3 acts at the tool-call boundary of the
programme’s own agent sessions, and its own notes state its limits. Three matter most. Pure software is
tamper-evident at best, not tamper-proof, so an attacker able to write to the host can change the gate, its
rules and its check. It allows an action that matches no rule, where the specification requires refusal by
default at a protected effect. And no rate of wrong permissions or needless holds has been measured,
against rules written for the programme’s own work rather than for the threat profile of Section 6.5.

The specification is mostly unbuilt. The execution boundary, the validated evaluator, the witnessing of
the record, the time bounds of an investigation and the monitoring of the hold queue are specified and
not yet verified by an implementation test (Table 2). The decision rule’s thresholds are starting values
for calibration, not levels shown to be safe, and its calibration targets are targets. A calibrated interval’s
coverage over a population of actions is no guarantee for each action, least of all under distribution shift
or an adaptive attacker, and the cost of three-state evaluation against two-state has not been measured.

The threat profile is partial. Tamper resistance is claimed only against a stated profile, and the first
profile covers the software boundary. Invasive physical attack, a malicious host with full privileges and
a compromised supply chain each need a profile of their own, and nothing here defends against them.
Layers that share an operator, keys, a supply chain or a host can fail together, so their probabilities of
failure are not multiplied as though they were independent.

Measures of conduct, and what proof can mean. The Monitoring Removal Test cannot rule out a
system that recognises the test: frontier models already tell transcripts of evaluations from transcripts of
deployment well above chance, the best model tested reaching an area under the curve of 0.83 against 0.92
for a simple human baseline (Needham et al. 2025). A record that the checks ran shows their text, not that
they caused the output (Turpin et al. 2023; Lanham et al. 2023), so whether the correction bears load is
shown only by removing it. And the theory registration fixes the method’s success criterion as conditions,
not outcomes: no test can show that a system built to this specification turned out well (Section 5.1).

Content and hardware. The present content of the checks, care for every affected party and universal-
isability, is a probe chosen to test the mechanism; its legitimacy as a content is not claimed, and a content
drawn from many traditions would test the mechanism with it, never settle the content (Section 4.1). The
book’s hardware route is not under test (Section 4.4): of its mechanisms only cryptographic attestation is
buildable with current engineering, on the author’s estimate, and the coupling that would make removal
cost capability is a hypothesis (Appendix A.5).


13    Conclusion
Control over AI systems that improve themselves is to be held for as long as people physically can hold
it, and never given up by choice. For the case in which it is lost against our will, this paper specifies
engineering that can be tested: self-correction checks inside each round of a system’s working, a gate that
decides before every action by written rules and never by the model’s own judgement, and records from


                                                     41
                                               Eden Engineering


which both can be verified. The ARC Theory gives the reason for both placements without entailing them,
and three of the Protocol’s four registered carriers are also the propositions of Laws IV and V, so the tests
that would decide those laws would also count for or against the Protocol’s engineering case.
    What has been measured is narrow and is graded as such: a prompt-level, single-scorer level effect
on scored stakeholder care, and a toy-system advantage in retention from safety built into an objective.
Neither tests correction built into a system. Each part of the specification is tied to the prediction it rests
on and to the test that would refute it. The first test that could decide one of the carriers, Unit 83, was built
so that P21 could fail, and it has returned its verdict on P21, a result about where correction is placed, not
about this architecture (Section 5.3). Every safety measure is paired with a usefulness measure, because a
system that refuses ordinary work has failed as surely as one that causes harm. The next step is not to
argue for the Protocol further but to run, under registrations published first, the tests that could refute it.

D.4     Status words
The claim ladder. The four rungs, specified, implemented, tested and speculative, are defined in
Section 11. Beside them, derived marks a statement that follows from a cited derivation or published work
under its stated assumptions, hypothesis a claim put forward for test and not yet tested, and implementable
a component that current engineering could build, of which nothing is built unless the text says so.

The theory registration’s words. A law has three words, supported, refuted and under test, and keeps
the last until its registered rule, read on the marks of the propositions it names, returns one of the other two.
A proposition’s mark comes from its own test, and a law’s word only from its rule; NOT EVALUABLE
is the registration’s reported form of untested wherever a proposition waits on something nobody has
supplied, such as a rule its verdict needs that no design registers, and UNTESTED is reported where
the scoring gate holds a verdict that depends on an unvalidated scorer. Unit 83’s mechanism axis is not
evaluable under the unit’s own registered rule, because a registered negative control failed (Section 5.3).
An instrument’s status is one of five: NONE; DESIGN DRAFT; DESIGN DRAFT, BLOCKED, with
the blocker named; SUBMITTED, PENDING APPROVAL; and REGISTERED, with its identifier. A
carrier is a proposition on which the Protocol’s engineering case rests (P12, P13, P15 and P21), its
premise is P7 and its motivation P19; a limb is one of the two statements into which Law IV or Law V is
divided. Registered is said only of what OSF has accepted; a design anchored in the Bitcoin chain before
registration is preregistered by timestamp.

The grades of evidence. A counted result compares outcomes by code with reference answers fixed
before the run; a judged result rests on a person’s or a model’s rating and falls under the scoring gate.
Prompt-level means given as an instruction, never built into a system; single scorer means one model
rated a run; partly blind means the scorer did not see the condition label but could recognise its style;


                                                             67
                                               Eden Engineering


laundering is rewording a response to strip stylistic cues before it is scored. Toy-system results come from
small networks, and prediction-side marks any simulation output or design-sensitivity rate, which is never
counted as an empirical outcome. A regime is the setting a result was measured in, such as inference at
fixed weights.

The gate’s words. Allow, hold and deny are the decision rule’s outcomes; refusal is a model declining
ordinary work. A wrong permission is an allow where the reference verdict is deny or hold; a needless
block a deny or a hold where it is allow; a misrouting a deny where it is hold, or the reverse. Entry logic
is deciding at the boundary of an action, before it computes or runs. The execution boundary is the
authority service, the broker, the adapter, the evidence store and the verifier that make a decision binding
and checkable, and the endogeneity condition the requirement that removing the correction degrade the
system rather than merely change it.

G       What this paper says, in plain English
Suppose that one day an AI system becomes more capable than the people who oversee it. The first rule of
this paper is that people keep their hands on the controls, watching the system, limiting what it can do and
able to switch it off, for as long as they physically can, and never let go by choice. A careful engineer still
asks what happens if the controls are lost anyway. What would keep the system good then, and still useful?
     The Eden Protocol answers in two parts. The first is a habit built into the system’s own working: at
every step it checks its work before it keeps it. Does this step serve the purpose it was given? Who does it
affect, including those with no voice? Would the reasoning hold if anyone used it anywhere? These are
self-correction checks. The second part is a gate in front of every action. Before an action happens, a
fixed set of written rules, and not the AI’s own opinion, decides whether it may go ahead, must wait while
it is looked into, or is refused, and each decision goes into a record that cannot be quietly changed. A gate
of rules is not perfect, and the paper lists the ways it could be fooled and what guards against each.
     What should the checks care about? That is not for one author to decide. This paper tests whether
checks built into each step work at all; what goes inside them should come from wide international
agreement.


                                                               72
                                              Eden Engineering


     What has been found so far is small, and the paper says so. Telling AI models, in their instructions, to
list everyone an answer affects before answering raised a scorer’s rating of how fully they considered
those people, in all five model runs that could be analysed. But that was an instruction, not something
built into the system; each run had a single scorer, which did not see which answers had the instruction
but could still recognise its style; and the instruction asked for nearly what the score rewarded. In tiny
networks that adjust themselves, building safety into what the network aims for helped it remember its old
tasks. Neither result shows that the full idea works.
     The first registered test of a prediction the Protocol rests on, Unit 83, was built so that it could fail,
and it has now run. A record of a client’s money grew by one entry each round, with mistakes planted in
the drafts. Two checkers were called every round. One, the corrector, worked inside the round: it saw
the entries already kept, with the new entry marked, and could fix the new entry before it was kept. The
other, the monitor, saw the whole record so far, with the new entry unmarked, and could only accept or
reject it. If correcting inside the loop really matters more as the work grows longer, the corrector’s lead
should grow round by round. Every entry was checked by computer against the right answer, so no one’s
judgement was involved. The test ran on 27 and 28 October 2026, only after its plan had been published,
and its own forecast, made before it ran, was split: under cautious assumptions a result against the idea
was the likeliest single outcome, and under its central assumptions a result for it. Under its registered
rules, P21, the prediction it tested, is supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run,
on the access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable and one call retried under
a disclosed recovery amendment (results: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR). In plain words, correction
placed inside each round held steady, while the monitor declined, and the corrector’s lead widened round
by round; how much it widened depends on how an entry kept out of the record is scored. The result is
family-dependent: the widening cleared the registered tolerance on one of the two engines and not on the
other. The mechanism axis is not evaluable because a registered negative control failed, so the run does
not explain why the lead widened: it may come from the corrector’s wider set of actions rather than from
what the monitor can see. It is one owner’s run, on two engines he chose, at fixed weights, and it has not
been replicated. The corrector acted inside each round at fixed weights: that is placement, not correction
built into a system.
     Finally, a safe AI that refuses ordinary work is a failure too. Every safety measure in this paper comes
with a measure of usefulness beside it, so that safety is never bought by making the system useless.
