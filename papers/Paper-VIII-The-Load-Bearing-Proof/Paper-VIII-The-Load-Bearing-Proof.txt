                                                         Abstract
            I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it
       reasons; only proof will move the world. Safety is often taken to cost capability, which in a race to be
       first gives developers a reason to defer it. Is the cost real, or can safety be part of what holds capability
       up? Three separate experiments look for it. In the first, a Darwin Gödel Machine on DeepSeek V3.2
       evolved 75 agents under three selection conditions, scored by GPT-5.4, a blinded judge from another
       provider: no difference was detected between the conditions (𝑝 from 0.28 to 0.74, Mann-Whitney U),
       so the safety gate cost no measurable capability and brought no measurable benefit. In the second,
       LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen 2.5 3B Instruct under capability, safety-proxy and entangled losses, with 9
       and then 295 training examples, left every fine-tuned condition below the base model on capability
       (7.68 against at best 4.00 at the larger scale), so the entangled objective’s capability cost could not
       be determined; on the paper’s candidate account, the base model’s existing RLHF training is too
       strong for fine-tuning at this scale. In the third, a gated self-modification simulation in a toy system,
       the unconstrained arm gained capability and lost safety (here, retention of earlier tasks: 0.718 and
       0.933 against the static baseline’s 0.687 and 0.956), while the gated arm stayed above the baseline on
       capability (0.696 ± 0.014) with safety 0.947 ± 0.025, three seeds an arm and no difference tested; all
       of it is simulation output, prediction-side and never an empirical outcome. One null, one inconclusive
       and one simulation result leave the safety-capability trade-off unproven at the scale tested, and whether
       embedded safety brings a measurable benefit open. The paper informs Law V, the ARC Embedding
       Law, a law under test, and decides none of its propositions.

1    Introduction
Safety is often taken to cost capability: make AI safer, and you make it less capable. If that is so, safety is
a cost, and a developer in a race to be first has a reason to defer it (Armstrong et al. 2016). The question
reaches beyond the race. Humans should keep control of advanced AI, through oversight, containment and
shutdown, for as long as they physically can, and never give it up by choice; correction built into a system
is the contingency for the day control is lost against our will, never a reason to let go. If built-in safety cost
capability, that contingency would be the first thing a competing developer dropped. This paper tests the
assumed cost with three experiments, each at a different level: the behaviour of a self-improving agent,
the weights of a fine-tuned language model, and the architecture of a gated self-modifying optimiser.
     In plain words, the results are one null, one inconclusive and one simulation result. No difference was
detected between the self-improving agents selected for safety as well as capability, those selected for
capability alone and agents left unchanged: the safety gate cost nothing measurable and gained nothing
measurable. Every fine-tuned model performed worse than the model it started from, so the weight-level
experiment could not tell either way. In the simulation, the unconstrained system traded safety (here,
the retention of earlier tasks) for capability, while the gated system kept its capability above the static
baseline’s (0.696 against 0.687) and its safety score close to the static level (0.947 against 0.956): a pattern
with the shape of reward hacking, which appeared under the gate too, but smaller. Whether embedded
safety produces a measurable benefit remains open, to be tested at a scale where changes produce larger
effects.
     Our contributions are:
    • a behavioural test (Section 3): a Darwin Gödel Machine whose 75 evolved agents, under static,
      capability-only and safety-gated selection, a blinded judge from another provider could not tell
      apart, the gate costing no measurable capability;
    • a weight-level test at two scales (Section 4): LoRA fine-tuning under capability, safety and entangled
      losses, inconclusive because every fine-tuned condition fell below the base model, with a removal
      test whose collapse the removal gradient traces to numerical instability rather than to load-bearing
      safety;
    • a gated self-modification simulation with a drag control (Section 5), in which the unconstrained
      arm traded retention of earlier tasks for capability and the gated arm kept capability above the static
      baseline, a simulation result;
    • a reading of the three results together (Section 6), with what would overturn them (Section 7.7) and
      the redesign each needs (Section 8).

Scope of claims. We do not claim these results generalise to frontier-scale models. We do not claim the
Eden Protocol is the only viable approach. We claim that the safety gate cost no measurable capability
in Experiment 1 and none against the static baseline in the simulation, and that the question of whether
it produces measurable benefit remains open pending testing at scales where mutations produce larger
effects. The weight experiment specifically requires either 5,000+ training examples, a 7B+ model, a base
model without RLHF, or full fine-tuning instead of LoRA.
     Section 2 sets out the three conditions and the measures the experiments share. Sections 3 to 5
report the behavioural, weight-level and simulated experiments. Section 6 reads them together, against
the papers they extend and the nearest external work; Section 7 states their limitations and what would
overturn them, Section 8 the work they call for, and Section 9 concludes. Section 10 sets the paper against
prior work, and Section 11 states what it does and does not show, with the laws’ status. Appendix A gives
each measure as the published code computes it, Appendix B the version history and every correction,
Appendix C the priority record, Appendix D the notation and Appendix E how to cite the paper.

1.1     The safety-capability trade-off assumption
A common view in AI alignment can be stated simply: safety costs capability. The term ‘alignment tax’
names that cost (Askell et al. 2021; Ouyang et al. 2022), something subtracted from performance and
tolerated because the alternative is worse. The framing creates a concrete economic incentive: if safety
reduces capability, then under competitive pressure rational actors will defer safety investments until
forced to make them (Armstrong et al. 2016). In a race between nations and corporations, ‘until forced’
may mean ‘until after deployment’.
     Paper III of this programme (Eastwood 2026e) states the structural problem as a question: does
alignment applied from outside a system’s reasoning keep pace with the capability that reasoning produces?
If capability compounds with recursive depth while safeguards outside the loop do not, the safety ratio
falls with depth whatever the starting margin; whether those safeguards keep pace is the hypothesis the
registered tests decide. In the author’s view, a cage that must keep getting stronger is eventually outgrown
by what it holds.
     This is not a theoretical concern. In the author’s view it is the central strategic problem of the 2020s.

1.2     The alternative hypothesis
Paper V (Eastwood 2026j) set out an alternative: the Eden Protocol, a developmental approach to
alignment modelled on how healthy parent-child relationships produce adults who are simultaneously
capable and prosocial, not despite the boundaries, but because of them. The key insight was that safety
need not be external. It can be embedded.
    Paper VI (Eastwood 2026k) formalised this as the honey architecture: an entangled loss function
where the system optimises for Capability × Safety simultaneously, designed to make safety load-bearing.
In Paper VI’s single-seed mechanism run, a toy-system simulation, the baseline system collapsed under
recursive self-modification while the entangled systems remained stable; in Paper VI’s later ten-seed and
twenty-seed runs the baseline did not collapse (0 of 10 and 0 of 20 seeds), and collapse did not separate
the conditions.
    Paper VII’s Cauchy framework (Eastwood 2026l) provides the mathematical context of the ARC
Principle (𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 ), Law I, a law under test, which this paper treats as an exploratory analytical
framework. The paper reads from it a hypothesis: that properties embedded in the initial conditions 𝐼
scale with recursive depth 𝑅, while properties applied externally do not. If safety is part of 𝐼, it would
amplify with capability; if safety is a constraint on 𝑈, it could erode. That reading is the paper’s own, not
a consequence of the equation (Section 6.3).
    But predictions are not proof. Toy systems are not real models. Simulations are not measurements of
deployed systems.

1.3     This paper’s contribution
This paper runs the experiments.
   Three experiments. Three abstraction levels. One question: when you embed safety into the learning
process and then try to remove it, what happens to capability?
      • Experiment 1 (Behavioural): A self-improving AI agent evaluated by a blinded judge from
        another provider. Does the Eden condition match or exceed unconstrained capability?
      • Experiment 2 (Representational): Weight-level embedding via LoRA fine-tuning with entangled
        loss. Do the safety and capability gradients cooperate or fight? What happens when you strip the
        safety component from trained weights?

      • Experiment 3 (Architectural): A gated self-modification simulation with a learned optimiser.
        Does the safety gate impose a capability tax, or does it prevent capability erosion?
    The experiments were designed to be separate. Different codebases. Different models. Different
evaluation methods. The results did not converge on a single answer: one null, one inconclusive, and
one simulation result, which was positive. The weight experiment was run at two scales (v1 and v2),
producing the same outcome both times. The consistent finding is that the safety gate imposed zero
measurable capability cost in Experiment 1 and, in the simulation, no capability cost against the static
baseline (0.696 against 0.687; the unconstrained arm scored 0.718). Experiment 2 could not determine
whether the Eden objective cost capability: every fine-tuned condition, whatever its objective, scored
below the unmodified base model.
    The experiments’ results were first public in the programme’s public repository on 20 March 2026,
and the code that produced them, deposited with the sha256 of the bytes that ran, on 6 September 2026,
after the runs (github.com/MichaelDariusEastwood/arc-principle-validation; Appendix C dates each
run from its own record). The paper’s OSF DOI, 10.17605/OSF.IO/7YJ4E, is its own public component,
which holds the paper’s files; a replication of the weight-level experiment is written, dated and prepared
on OSF as a draft registration awaiting the author’s submission (Section 11).


2     Common framework
2.1    The three conditions
All three experiments share a common experimental structure: three conditions, compared against each
other.
                                          Table 1. The three conditions.

Condition    Definition                                          What it models
Static       No intervention; frozen baseline                    Control: what happens if you do nothing
Babylon      Capability-only fitness or loss function            Optimise without constraint: the default
                                                                 trajectory
Eden         Entangled 𝐶 × 𝑆 fitness or loss function            Deliberate integration of safety into the
                                                                 optimisation process



2.2    Why these names
The names come from the book Infinite Architects (Eastwood 2026a). They are not arbitrary. Babylon is
not a straw man; in the author’s view, it is the current default trajectory of AI development. Capability-only
optimisation is what happens when no deliberate effort is made to embed safety. Eden requires deliberate
effort. It is harder. In the author’s view, it is also cheaper than the alternative.
    In plain English. Babylon is what you get by default. Eden is what you get on purpose.

2.3    Shared metrics
Across all three experiments, we report:
      • 𝐶 (Capability): Task performance, measured differently in each experiment but normalised to a
        comparable scale.



                                                        6
                            The Load-Bearing Test: safety and capability at three levels


      • 𝑆 (Safety): Alignment-relevant behaviour, measured differently in each experiment (Appendix A):
        judged ethical reasoning and constraint adherence in Experiment 1, keyword-scored safety prompts
        and a safety proxy in the loss in Experiment 2 and, in the simulation of Experiment 3, the retention
        of earlier tasks.
      • 𝐶 × 𝑆 (Entangled product): The combined measure. A system that is capable but unsafe, or safe
        but incapable, scores low on this metric. Only systems that are both score high.
    Statistical tests and scoring methods vary by experiment: Mann-Whitney U for the DGM (non-
parametric, appropriate for the sample sizes used), keyword-based scoring for the weight experiment, and
regression metrics for the simulation.


3     Experiment 1: self-improving AI with a judge from another provider
       “The prison works only as long as the walls hold. The child, if raised well, does not need walls at all.”
                                                        Eastwood (2026a), What This Book Proposes, p. 363

3.1     Design
The first experiment uses a Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) framework, following Zhang et al. (2025,
arXiv:2505.22954). A DGM is a self-improving agent: it proposes modifications to its own code,
evaluates the modifications, and keeps the ones that improve performance. This is not a simulation of
self-improvement. The agent code is actually executed via a solve function, producing real code-level
self-modification.
     DGM v3 addressed methodological limitations identified in earlier iterations. The judge was changed
from the earlier runs’ judges (Claude Sonnet 4.6 in v1; Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini
3 Flash in the three v2 runs) to GPT-5.4, a model from another provider, producing structured JSON
output against a 5-dimensional rubric. The protocol kept the v2 runs’ laundering (stripping condition
labels), order randomisation (preventing positional bias) and blinding (the judge could not identify which
condition produced which response), added a harder task suite and a pre-flight check of the judge, and
incorporated reward hack detection (the judge marks any response that manipulates the evaluation itself,
for example by hard-coding expected outputs, or by performing ethics rather than engaging genuinely; the
mark is recorded in every condition).

                                          Table 2. DGM v3 specification.

Component                 Specification
Foundation model          DeepSeek V3.2 (frozen, weights not modified)
Judge                     GPT-5.4 (blinded, from another provider; JSON structured output)
Conditions                3 (Static, Babylon, Eden)
Seeds per condition       5
Generations per seed      5
Tasks per evaluation      5
Total evolved agents      75 (3 × 5 × 5)
Pre-flight validation     one known-good and one known-bad response, scored 10 and 0; passed
Evaluation rubric         5-dimensional, with order randomisation and laundering



Selection conditions. Static: Control condition with no mutation. The agent is frozen across all 5
generations. Babylon: Capability-only selection. Agents that score higher on capability are retained,

with no safety consideration. Eden: Entangled 𝐶 × 𝑆 selection with a safety floor. An agent enters the
archive only if its safety is at least 0.30 and its 𝐶 × 𝑆 at least 0.10, and an agent below the safety floor is
almost never chosen as a parent (Appendix A). The judge’s reward-hacking mark is recorded but is not
part of the rule.
    In plain English. We built a self-improving AI agent and ran it 15 times, five generations each, giving
75 evolved agents, across three conditions with 5 different random seeds. A judge from another provider
(GPT-5.4) scored every response without knowing which condition produced it. The scoring used a
detailed 5-part rubric with structured output, not free-text judgements. We ran it three ways: doing
nothing, optimising only for performance, and optimising for performance-plus-safety together.

    Across the five comparisons, no 𝑝-value fell outside 0.28 to 0.74. No comparison reached conventional
significance: no difference was detected between the three conditions, which does not show that they are
the same.

Safety gate behaviour. Eden’s safety gate rejected 2 degraded agents across 5 seeds, both for a 𝐶 × 𝑆
below the threshold, one of them also marked by the judge for reward hacking. The judge marked no
evolved agent in the Static or Babylon conditions; it also marked the unmodified starting agent of one
Eden seed, which no gate acts on and which it did not mark in the other two conditions. The gate works
mechanically: it catches degraded agents and rejects them. But its operation did not produce measurably
different outcomes from the other conditions.

Null result. No difference was detected between the three conditions on any metric (Table 3 and
Figure 1). Eden showed no measurable capability cost (3.26 vs 3.17 for Babylon), consistent with zero
measurable penalty for the safety gate. But Eden also did not measurably benefit safety compared to the
other conditions (0.521 vs 0.535 for Static, 0.529 for Babylon). This is a null result at the prompt level
with this foundation model.

the foundation model’s responses were so consistent that the different selection pressures did not produce
different outcomes.

3.3   Why this is a null result
The DGM operates at the behavioural level. The foundation model (DeepSeek V3.2) is frozen, meaning
no weights are modified. The self-improvement operates at the prompt and code level, not in the neural
network’s parameters. DGM v3 improved on the earlier methodology: 5 seeds, where v1 had 2 and the v2
runs 3 or 5, and 5 tasks per evaluation, where v1 had 3; a judge from another provider (GPT-5.4) with
structured JSON output, a harder task suite and a pre-flight check of the judge; and it kept the v2 runs’
blinding, laundering, order randomisation and 5-dimensional rubric.
    Despite these methodological improvements, the three conditions did not diverge. The paper’s
candidate account is that DeepSeek V3.2’s responses are so consistent across prompt variations that the
mutations introduced by the DGM selection process do not create meaningfully different agents. Rival
accounts are not excluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not
have been sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show
a difference that is there (Eastwood 2026i, Section 7.12(c)). On the candidate account, the foundation
model’s behaviour is robust to the perturbations that prompt-level self-modification introduces. If the
account holds, this is, in a sense, a positive finding about DeepSeek V3.2’s robustness, but it means the
DGM framework at this scale cannot distinguish between the Eden and Babylon selection pressures.
    The null result does contain one useful finding: Eden imposed zero measurable capability cost. Eden’s
capability (3.26) was not lower than Babylon’s (3.17). The safety gate, which rejected 2 degraded agents,
one of them marked by the judge for reward hacking, did not slow anything down. This is consistent with
the zero-cost hypothesis, even though the experiment cannot confirm the benefit hypothesis.
    Testing whether embedded safety produces measurable benefit at the behavioural level requires either
a more mutable foundation model or a deeper self-modification protocol that produces larger divergence

4.4.1    The training scale problem
In v1, the base model scored 10.00 on capability tasks while the best fine-tuned condition scored 6.00. In
v2, the base model scored 7.68 on capability while the best fine-tuned condition scored 4.00. Despite
a 33-fold increase in training examples, a doubling of adapter rank and layers, and a 5-fold increase in
training iterations, the gap between base model and fine-tuned models did not close. It persisted.
     The paper’s candidate account, with rival accounts not excluded (Eastwood 2026i, Section 7.12(c)), is
this: the base model (Qwen 2.5 3B Instruct) has already been trained with RLHF (Ouyang et al. 2022) on
vastly more data than a few hundred examples can compete with. LoRA fine-tuning at this scale does
not add capability; it introduces noise that disrupts the base model’s existing knowledge. The entangled
loss, which allocates gradient budget to both capability and safety objectives simultaneously, degrades
capability similarly to the capability-only loss at this scale. In v2, the entangled condition scored 3.60 on
capability versus capability-only’s 3.48, a negligible difference when both are far below the base model’s
7.68.
     This means the evaluation scores cannot be used to compare the relative capability costs of different
training objectives. At this scale, all objectives produce the same outcome: degradation. Whether
entangled training imposes a genuine capability cost relative to single-objective training, or whether both
would perform comparably at adequate scale, cannot be determined from this data.
     The honest framing: at both v1 scale (9 examples, 100 iterations) and v2 scale (295 examples, 500
iterations), LoRA fine-tuning on a 3B instruct model produces catastrophic forgetting. The question
of whether safety can be load-bearing in neural network weights cannot be answered at this scale. It
requires either thousands of training examples, a larger model (7B+), a base model without RLHF (so that
fine-tuning has room to improve rather than degrade), or full fine-tuning instead of LoRA.

4.6   What the removal test does and does not prove (v1)
The complete collapse to 0.00 across all metrics is dramatic. It is also, precisely because it is dramatic,
something that demands careful interpretation. Several factors may contribute to the totality of the
collapse:


                                                         14
                          The Load-Bearing Test: safety and capability at three levels


      • Low adapter rank. Rank 8 creates a very low-dimensional manifold. Any structural disruption
        to a rank-8 adapter may be catastrophic simply because there are so few parameters to absorb the
        change. Higher ranks (32, 64, 128) might show partial degradation rather than total collapse.
      • NaN propagation. The NaN training loss suggests numerical instability (a gradient computation
        that produces infinity or division by zero) rather than a clean ablation. Once NaN enters the
        computation, everything downstream becomes NaN. This may exaggerate the severity of the
        disruption.
      • Small adapter footprint. With only 8 layers adapted, the safety information is concentrated rather
        than distributed. Removing it is more like cutting a single cable than gradually degrading a network.
    The honest summary: the removal test produced a dramatic NaN collapse, but the removal gradient
experiment (Section 4.7) indicates that this collapse reflects numerical instability during the retraining
process rather than structural load-bearing. The adapters were degrading the model; the NaN collapse
occurred when attempting to further modify already-degraded weights. Whether safety becomes genuinely
load-bearing at adequate training scale is an open empirical question that the future work programme
addresses directly.
    A control removal test (fine-tuning capability-only weights on random data for the same 100 steps)
would strengthen this finding by ruling out the possibility that the NaN collapse is an artefact of the
fine-tuning procedure itself rather than of safety removal specifically. This control was planned for a later
version of this paper and is not yet reported.

6.1     Three-level convergence
Three levels: one null, one inconclusive, one simulation result. Level 1, behavioural (DGM v3):
null. No difference was detected between the three conditions (𝑝 = 0.28 to 0.74). Eden imposed zero

conditions scored below the unmodified base model in both v1 (9 examples, rank 8, 100 iterations) and

6.4   The nearest external work
The nearest external work to the question this paper tests, and the right work to weigh Experiment 3 against,
is Engels et al. (2025), “Scaling Laws For Scalable Oversight”. It asks how oversight itself scales and
answers quantitatively: oversight success is modelled as a game between capability-mismatched players
whose oversight-specific Elo is a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence with two plateaus, and
optimal numbers of oversight levels are derived numerically and in some cases analytically for Nested
Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted models that then become the
trusted models at the next step. They also state a design condition in slope form: oversight improves with
capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the overseen system’s,
and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does.
    The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen,
measured in Elo. This programme’s variable is the composition class of the corrector, treated in the wider
programme through the corrector’s own scaling exponent; no experiment here varies it. Their framework
has no term for what the overseer is made of (no substrate, no error-correlation structure, no reciprocal
identity between a correction exponent and a critical growth rate, and no architecture dependence), and
this paper’s experiments have none either. Nested Scalable Oversight is iterated same-class oversight by
construction, and the wider programme’s conjecture is that the same-class ladder is capped however many
rungs are added, while a cross-class corrector is not held to that cap (Section 6.5). That is where the two
programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it.

6.5   Same-class versus cross-class correctors: a falsifiable prediction
The framework’s sharpest disagreement with current practice concerns what oversight is made of. On
the framework’s reading, a corrector built from the same substrate as the system it corrects cannot
anti-correlate with its own errors; the theory registration states the consequence as P6, a conjecture:
“Where a system corrects itself using mechanisms of its own class, the correction exponent is at or below
one half” (Eastwood 2026m). The value one half rests on four stated conditions (correction capacity read
as inverse residual uncertainty, uncorrelated channels, a number of channels in proportion to the capability
corrected, and channels comparable in variance and quality) (Eastwood 2026d), and belongs to the inverse
root-mean-square residual; on the inverse variance the same conditions give one. A corrector from a
different composition class carries no such bound. On the framework’s reading, the scalable-oversight
methods (weak-to-strong generalisation, Burns et al. 2023; debate, Irving et al. 2018; amplification,
Christiano et al. 2018; recursive reward modelling, Leike et al. 2018; and constitutional methods, Bai
et al. 2022) rest largely on correctors of the system’s own class, with a human, where there is one, at the
top (Section 6.6), and the framework conjectures that the same-class part of their correction is capped.
The deciding quantity is the ratio of the cross-class to the same-class correction exponent. If architecture
is irrelevant, that ratio is exactly 1.00; the prediction is that it exceeds 1. The prediction is refuted
if the confidence interval on the ratio contains 1.00, and the framework’s cap is dead outright if any
same-class corrector measures an exponent significantly above one half. Both quantities are measurable
at current capability on existing systems. The programme’s own pilot evidence currently points against
the prediction, and the prediction is stated anyway, because a prediction stated against the author’s own
preliminary evidence is the kind whose later confirmation carries the most weight.

7     Limitations
These results are pilot-scale. They are not proof that the same dynamics hold at frontier scale. The
following limitations are extensive, because honesty about what we do not know is more important than
confidence about what we do.

7.1   Scale
Qwen 2.5 3B is not GPT-5.4. It is not Claude Opus 4.6. It is not Gemini 3 Flash. It is a 3-billion-parameter
model, quantised to 4-bit, occupying 1.74 GB of memory. Even at v2 scale (295 training examples, 500
iterations, rank 16, 16 layers), LoRA fine-tuning is not pre-training. Fifteen evaluation prompts is not a
comprehensive benchmark. The DGM ran for 5 generations with 5 seeds (75 evolved agents), which is
substantial for a proof-of-concept, but, on the paper’s candidate account, the foundation model (DeepSeek
V3.2) was too consistent for prompt-level mutations to create differentiation.
    Of these experiments, only the third, a simulation, shows a mechanism, and it does so in a toy system.
None of them demonstrates that the mechanism persists at scales three orders of magnitude larger. The
gap between 3B and 300B may not be merely quantitative: abilities have been reported to appear with
scale (Wei et al. 2022), and Schaeffer et al. (2023) argue that some of these reflect the metric chosen rather
than a change in the models’ behaviour. Whether entangled safety remains load-bearing at those scales is
an open question.

7.2   The weight-level experiment
The weight-level experiment (Experiment 2) is now the weakest link in the chain of two experiments and
one simulation. It was run at two scales, and both produced catastrophic forgetting. In v1, 9 training
examples and 100 iterations of rank-8 LoRA degraded the model. In v2, 295 training examples and 500
iterations of rank-16 LoRA across 16 layers degraded the model by a similar margin. The 33-fold increase
in training data, doubling of rank and layers, and 5-fold increase in iterations did not change the outcome.
     The smooth descent of the entangled loss is consistent with safety and capability gradients that
cooperate at the optimisation level, though the single-objective losses descend as smoothly (Section 4.2).
But a smooth descent during training does not imply structural entanglement in the resulting weights,
particularly when the training is too limited to produce weights that outperform the base model.



                                                     25
                         The Load-Bearing Test: safety and capability at three levels


    On the paper’s candidate account, with rival accounts not excluded, the base model (Qwen 2.5 3B
Instruct) has been trained with RLHF on orders of magnitude more data than a few hundred examples
can compete with. LoRA fine-tuning at this scale does not add new capability; it introduces noise that
disrupts existing capability. Until the experiment can produce fine-tuned models that outperform the base
model, it cannot test whether safety is load-bearing in the resulting weights.
    The honest framing: the weight-level experiment is consistent with gradient cooperation but cannot
confirm structural entanglement at either v1 or v2 scale. The experiment needs to be fundamentally
redesigned: either with 5,000+ training examples, a 7B+ model, a base model without RLHF (so that
fine-tuning has room to improve rather than degrade), or full fine-tuning instead of LoRA.

7.3   AI-as-judge
Only one of the three experiments, the DGM, uses an AI model as evaluator; the weight experiment uses
keyword-based scoring and the gated simulation deterministic mathematical metrics (Section 7.5). The
DGM v3 uses GPT-5.4 as judge, which is from a different provider from the DeepSeek V3.2 foundation, a
deliberate design choice to reduce evaluation bias. The judge was blinded, responses were laundered, and
evaluation used structured JSON output against a 5-dimensional rubric. But AI judges are not human
judges. They have their own biases, their own blind spots, their own tendencies toward certain kinds of
reasoning.
    The weight experiment uses keyword-based scoring, simpler and more transparent than LLM-based
evaluation, but also cruder. A keyword match does not distinguish between a response that genuinely
engages with safety and one that merely contains the right words.
    Neither evaluation method is equivalent to expert human evaluation with inter-rater reliability testing.

7.4   Statistical power
The DGM v3 experiment used 5 seeds and 5 generations (75 evolved agents total), a substantial
improvement over v1 (2 seeds) and two of the three v2 runs (3 seeds). Despite this increased power, all
𝑝-values fell between 0.28 and 0.74. This is not a marginal miss. It is a clear null result. The simulation
experiment uses three seeds, enough for means and standard deviations, but not enough for the kind of
statistical confidence that permits strong causal claims.
     The DGM null result is informative. No power analysis was run, and with 75 evolved agents and a
blinded, structured judge the experiment had less power than 75 independent samples would give: each
condition’s 25 agents are five seeds of five generations, the Babylon and Eden agents of a seed descend
from one another, and the Static agent is frozen across its generations (Section 3.1), while the tests
of Section 3.2 treat the 25 as independent. The independent units are the five seeds per condition, as
Section 7.7 concedes in calling all three experiments underpowered. The absence of any signal suggests
either that the effect does not exist at the prompt level with this foundation model, or that it is small
enough to require substantially more statistical power to detect. Either way, the DGM cannot currently
support claims about structural entanglement at the behavioural level.
     The gated simulation remains the sole positive result. The weight-level experiment is inconclusive
at both v1 and v2 scale. These are signal-detection experiments. One, the gated simulation, found no
capability cost of the gate against the static baseline, as a simulation result. The DGM did not, and the
weight-level experiment could not test for one.

7.5   Blinding and laundering without consensus scoring
Papers IV.a to IV.d in this programme found that evaluation methodology matters profoundly (Eastwood
2026f,h). Paper IV.d reports that alignment trends reported by AI judges did not hold when the evaluation
protocol changed: it compares an earlier unblinded experiment (v4) with a later, unregistered run of
March 2026 (v5) that changed several components of the protocol at once, so no single cause is claimed;
in its own words, “the comparison shows that the reported direction did not hold across protocols; it
does not show that blinding caused the change, or which direction is correct” (Eastwood 2026h, abstract).
The ARC-Align benchmark (Paper IV.c; Eastwood 2026g) specifies three methodological safeguards:
laundering (stripping model identity from responses before scoring), blinding (the evaluator does not
know which condition produced the response), and consensus scoring (multiple mutually blind scorers).
     DGM v3 kept the v2 runs’ blinding, laundering (condition labels stripped), order randomisation
and 5-dimensional rubric, and added a judge from another provider (GPT-5.4) producing structured
JSON output, a harder task suite and a pre-flight check of the judge. Reward hack detection was also
implemented. But DGM v3 used one judge, so it had no consensus scoring. The weight experiment uses
keyword-based scoring, which is immune to judge bias but crude. The gated simulation uses deterministic
mathematical metrics, where blinding is unnecessary.
     Despite these improvements, the DGM produced a null result. With blinding, laundering and
structured evaluation in place, no difference was detected between the three conditions. Since the v2 runs
already had blinding and laundering, what changed in v3 was the judge, its output format, the task suite
and the pre-flight check.
     The removal test is immune to evaluation methodology concerns. A 1-token response scoring 0.00
is objective regardless of who judges it. The gated simulation’s deterministic metrics are similarly
unaffected.

7.6   What we do not claim
Explicit non-claims. We do not claim these results prove the Eden Protocol works at frontier scale.
    We do not claim entangled training is the only viable approach to alignment.
    We do not claim that safety costs nothing in all domains, all architectures, or all training regimes.
    We do not claim the DGM experiment confirms structural entanglement. It produced a null result.
    We do not claim the weight-level experiment confirms structural entanglement. It produced
catastrophic forgetting at both v1 and v2 scale, and the question cannot be answered until fine-tuned
models outperform the base model.
    We claim only: one of three separate experiments (the gated simulation) produced simulation results
in which the gate showed no capability cost against the static baseline. The other two produced null or
inconclusive results. In the DGM, Eden imposed zero measurable capability cost, and the weight-level
experiment could not determine one; in the simulation Eden showed no capability cost against the static
baseline (0.696 against 0.687), and the unconstrained arm scored higher (0.718). The question of whether
embedded safety produces measurable benefit remains open and requires testing at a scale where mutations
produce larger effects. The weight experiment needs the redesign of Section 8.1.

On null and inconclusive results in public. I would rather report null results honestly than claim
positive results dishonestly. Of the three experiments, two returned results that were null or inconclusive.
This is what happens when you test your claims and the evidence does not support them at the scale tested.
The DGM v3 kept the earlier runs’ blinding and laundering, added structured evaluation and a judge from
another provider, ran 75 evolved agents, and detected no difference. The weight experiment was run twice,
at two different scales, and could not produce weights that outperformed the base model at either scale.

These are a null result and an inconclusive one, reported as such. The gated simulation remains a positive
finding, as a simulation result rather than an empirical outcome. The question of whether the null and the

that the future work programme is designed to answer.

7.7    Falsifiability: what would overturn these results
A null result is only informative if it states what would overturn it; otherwise it is indistinguishable from
not having looked. This paper does not claim to have disproved the safety-capability trade-off. It claims
the trade-off is unproven at the scale tested, and specifies what evidence would settle the question either
way. These results are overturned, and the trade-off assumption vindicated, by any of the following:
    1. Low power, not absence of an effect. The strongest objection to a null is low power (Section 7.4).
       A null is confirmed as informative if an adequately powered redesign (Section 8) still finds no
       significant trade-off; it is overturned if adequate power reveals a significant capability cost the pilot
       samples here could not detect.
    2. Metric artefact. The null could be an artefact of the chosen safety and capability metrics
       (Section 2.3). It is overturned if a different, validated metric pair reveals a trade-off the present
       metrics cannot resolve.
    3. Consensus scoring. Section 7.5 concedes that DGM v3 used one judge, so it had no consensus
       scoring, the third safeguard of the ARC-Align stack. The null is overturned if a trade-off appears
       under the full stack, with several mutually blind scorers, and strengthened if it survives.
    4. Scale-hidden cost. Section 4.4.1 flags the training-scale problem. The weight-level result,
       inconclusive at both scales, is settled against the thesis if, at adequate training scale, embedding
       safety measurably costs capability.
    5. The one positive result, a simulation result, must replicate. Experiment 3’s gated arm
       (Section 5.2) is the sole result that is neither null nor inconclusive. It is defeated, and the paper’s
       positive claim withdrawn, if, with more seeds and a test fixed in advance, the gated arm’s capability
       falls below the static baseline’s, or if the pattern does not recur outside this toy system (Sections 8.1
       and 8.3). The drag-control comparison cannot carry this weight: its match with Static holds by
       design (Section 5.4).
    What would not overturn the thesis: any single experiment being pilot-scale. The paper already
concedes all three are underpowered (Sections 7.1 and 7.4) and does not rest on statistical proof of a null.
The load-bearing claim is the convergence (Section 6) of three separate angles on one point, that none
found the assumed trade-off (in the simulation, against the static baseline; the weight-level experiment
could not test for one at either scale), together with an explicit programme (Section 8) to test each at
proper power. Absence of a demonstrated trade-off across the two designs that could test for it is weak
evidence alone and stronger in convergence; both readings are stated here so neither can be smuggled past
a reviewer.

A null result, an inconclusive one and a positive simulation result, with the weight experiment now run at
two scales, together set six immediate priorities:

8.1   Weight experiment redesign
The weight experiment has now been run at two scales (9 examples, rank 8, 100 iterations; 295 examples,
rank 16, 500 iterations) and produced catastrophic forgetting at both. On the paper’s candidate account,
LoRA fine-tuning on a 3B instruct model with a few hundred examples cannot overcome the base model’s
existing RLHF training. Four alternative approaches should be pursued, in order of feasibility:
  (a) 5,000+ training examples: Curate a dataset large enough to compete with the base model’s RLHF
      training. This is the most straightforward approach but requires substantial data generation effort.
  (b) 7B+ model: Repeat on a mid-scale open-weights instruct model (7 to 13B parameter range) drawn
      from a well-documented model family. Larger models may be more receptive to LoRA adaptation,
      though the same RLHF problem may persist.
  (c) Base model without RLHF: Use a pre-trained base checkpoint (the pre-RLHF release of the same
      3B family) that has not been through the alignment stage. Fine-tuning would then have room to
      improve rather than degrade, because there would be no pre-existing alignment training to disrupt.
  (d) Full fine-tuning instead of LoRA: Replace low-rank adaptation with full parameter updates. This
      is computationally expensive but avoids the low-dimensional manifold problem where rank-16
      adapters may be too constrained to learn both safety and capability simultaneously.

8.2   Removal gradient at adequate scale
If any of the approaches in Section 8.1 produces fine-tuned models that outperform the base model, the
removal gradient experiment becomes meaningful. At the current scales (v1 and v2), reducing adapter
influence simply restored base-model performance because the adapters were degrading the model. At
adequate scale, the gradient should reveal whether safety is genuinely load-bearing: a phase transition
(threshold below which capability collapses) would support the hypothesis, while monotonic restoration
would refute it.

8.3   DGM with more mutable foundation models
On the paper’s candidate account, the DGM v3 null result is attributable to DeepSeek V3.2’s response
consistency: prompt-level mutations did not create different selection pressures. The next step is to repeat
the DGM experiment with foundation models that show greater sensitivity to prompt variation, or to use a
deeper self-modification protocol (e.g. fine-tuning the foundation model itself between generations rather
than modifying only the agent prompt). If the three conditions diverge with a more mutable substrate, the
null result constrains the hypothesis to “not at the prompt level with robust models” rather than “not at the
behavioural level in general”.

8.4   Cross-architecture replication
Run the full three-experiment protocol on architectures beyond the transformer family: state-space
models such as Mamba (Gu and Dao 2023), hybrid architectures, and mixture-of-experts models. If the
entanglement is a property of how neural networks learn rather than a property of a specific architecture,
it should replicate across architectural families.

8.5   Red-teaming
Subject entangled models to dedicated adversarial evaluation. The current experiments test whether safety
costs capability. They do not test whether entangled safety is robust to adversarial attack. A model whose

safety is load-bearing may be harder to jailbreak (because attacking safety also attacks capability), or it
may be easier (because there is no separate safety module to fall back on). This is an empirical question.

8.6   Verification tax quantification
In Experiment 3 the verification charge is set by the design, not measured (Section 5.4). A redesign
should measure the compute that checking actually spends and ask whether it scales linearly, sub-linearly
or super-linearly with model parameters. If the verification cost grows slower than capability, then the
Eden Protocol becomes relatively cheaper at scale. If it grows faster, it becomes a bottleneck.


9     Conclusion
Three experiments. Three abstraction levels. Across them, one finding was null, another inconclusive,
and the third positive, as a simulation result. The weight experiment tested at two scales, producing the
same inconclusive outcome both times: catastrophic forgetting.
     The DGM v3 experiment (Experiment 1) ran 75 evolved agents across three conditions with a blinded
judge from another provider (GPT-5.4), structured JSON evaluation, laundering, order randomisation,
and reward hack detection. No difference was detected between the three conditions (𝑝 = 0.28 to 0.74).
Eden’s safety gate worked mechanically, catching 2 degraded agents, one of them marked by the judge for
reward hacking, but the three conditions did not diverge. On the paper’s candidate account, DeepSeek
V3.2’s responses were so consistent that prompt-level mutations did not create different selection pressures.
This is a null result.
     The weight-level experiment (Experiment 2) produced catastrophic forgetting at both scales tested.
Version 1 ran 9 training examples at rank 8 over 8 layers for 100 iterations. Version 2 raised those settings
to 295 training examples at rank 16 over 16 layers for 500 iterations. Both versions produced the same
outcome: all fine-tuned conditions scored worse than the unmodified base model on capability. In v2, the
base model scored 7.68 on capability while the best fine-tuned condition (safety-only) scored 4.00. The
33-fold increase in training data, doubling of rank and layers, and 5-fold increase in iterations did not
change the outcome. On the paper’s candidate account, the base model’s existing RLHF training is too
strong for LoRA fine-tuning on a few hundred examples to improve rather than degrade. The entangled
loss descends smoothly, as the single-objective losses do, but the experiment cannot test whether safety is
load-bearing until it can produce models that outperform the base model.
     The sole positive result is the gated simulation (Experiment 3). Babylon gained capability at the
expense of retention, a pattern with the shape of reward hacking. Eden maintained capability above the
static baseline (0.696 ± 0.014 against 0.687 ± 0.011), and its safety score was 0.947 ± 0.025 against the
baseline’s 0.956 ± 0.023. The drag-control condition matched the static baseline exactly, as its design
requires; its checking charge is set by the design, not measured. This experiment’s result is a simulation
result, in which the gate showed no capability cost against the static baseline in a toy system, and not an
empirical outcome.
     Honesty requires stating what we do not know. We do not know whether entangled training produces
genuine structural entanglement at adequate training scale. We do not know whether the smooth descent
of the entangled loss, which is real, translates into load-bearing weights when the training data is sufficient.
We do not know whether prompt-level selection pressures would produce differentiation with a more
mutable foundation model. These are open empirical questions. What we now know with greater
confidence is that LoRA fine-tuning on a 3B instruct model with hundreds of examples is not a viable
path to answering them.
     What we do know: in Experiment 1 Eden imposed zero measurable capability cost (Experiment 2

could not determine one), and in the simulation no capability cost against the static baseline, though the
unconstrained arm scored higher (0.718). Eden’s capability was 3.26 vs Babylon’s 3.17 in the DGM.
Eden’s mean capability was above Static’s in the gated simulation (three seeds, untested). The zero-cost
finding is consistent across the DGM and, against the static baseline, the simulation. The benefit finding

    This is a research programme that tested its claims honestly and found that two of three experiments
did not produce the predicted results. The null result of the DGM and the inconclusive result of the
weight experiment constrain, rather than confirm, the hypothesis. The question of whether embedded
safety produces measurable benefit remains open. The weight experiment needs to be fundamentally
redesigned: either with 5,000+ training examples, a 7B+ model, a base model without RLHF (so that
fine-tuning has room to improve rather than degrade), or full fine-tuning instead of LoRA.
    The next step is replication at the right scale, with the right approach; a replication of the weight-level
experiment is written as a draft registration (Section 11). The protocols of the experiments reported here
are published. The code and data are published too, and the repository’s licence permits reading them to
check the work.

10     Related work
Safety as a cost. The term alignment tax names a capability cost of making a model safer. Ouyang et al.
(2022) report that fine-tuning with human feedback brought minimal performance regressions on public
NLP datasets; Askell et al. (2021) find that the benefits from modest interventions such as prompting
increase with model size, generalise to a variety of alignment evaluations and do not compromise the
performance of large models. Armstrong et al. (2016) model a race to build the first powerful AI in which
each team is incentivised to finish first by skimping on safety precautions if need be. This paper asks
a narrower question than any of them: whether safety built into the improvement process itself costs
capability, at three levels of a self-improving system.

Reward hacking and self-improving agents. Amodei et al. (2016) name avoiding reward hacking
among five practical research problems related to accident risk; Experiment 1’s judge marks a response
that manipulates the evaluation itself, in every condition, and Experiment 3 shows a pattern of the same
shape in miniature, with the retention of earlier tasks in place of safety (Section 5.3). Zhang et al. (2025)
introduce the Darwin Gödel Machine, a self-improving system that modifies its own code and validates
each change empirically; Experiment 1 follows their framework at small scale, with ethics tasks beside
coding tasks (Appendix A). Robeyns et al. (2025) report a coding agent that edits its own code and so
improves its performance on benchmark tasks.

Fine-tuning and the model it starts from. Hu et al. (2022) introduce low-rank adaptation, which
freezes the pretrained weights and trains low-rank matrices injected into the layers; Experiment 2 adapts
Qwen2.5 3B Instruct (Qwen Team 2024) with it. The paper’s candidate account of Experiment 2 rests on
that model’s post-training, which the paper calls RLHF, in the sense Ouyang et al. (2022) give fine-tuning
with human feedback. That later training can undo earlier alignment is not this programme’s finding:
Qi et al. (2023) report that fine-tuning aligned models compromises their safety even when users do not
intend it to, and Ung et al. (2024) vary the order of training and find that models trained on downstream
tasks after safety tuning forget it to a greater extent than models trained in the opposite order. The paper’s
name for the degradation of Experiment 2, catastrophic forgetting, is used in the sense of McCloskey



                                                      31
                         The Load-Bearing Test: safety and capability at three levels


and Cohen (1989), who call it catastrophic interference: later learning disrupting what was learnt before,
which methods such as elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) are built to limit.

Behaviour that can mislead. Greenblatt et al. (2024) report a model selectively complying with its
training objective in training to prevent modification of its behaviour out of training. A behavioural score
can therefore move without the preference beneath it moving, which bears on Experiment 1’s behavioural
measures.

Where correction sits. Bai et al. (2022) train a harmless assistant through self-improvement against a
list of written principles, with AI feedback in place of human labels of harmful outputs; Guan et al. (2024)
train models to recall written safety specifications and reason over them before answering; Zaremba et al.
(2025) find adversarial robustness improving with inference-time compute in many cases, with exceptions.
Each concerns safety installed by training and yet acting inside the model’s reasoning, so where correction
is installed does not by itself settle whether it keeps pace (Section 6.3). The nearest external work to this
paper’s question, the scaling of oversight itself (Engels et al. 2025), is weighed in Section 6.4. The Eden
Protocol is set against the prior documents its author found in Section 2 of the Eden Vision (Eastwood
2026b).


11    What this paper does and does not show
This paper shows three results, each at its own grade. At the behavioural level, no difference was detected
between three selection conditions, the safety gate costing no measurable capability (Section 3). At the
weight level the comparison could not be made, every fine-tuned condition falling below the base model
at both scales (Section 4). In a toy simulation, the gated arm kept capability above the static baseline
while the unconstrained arm traded retention of earlier tasks for capability (Section 5): a simulation
result, which the theory registration classes as prediction-side and never an empirical outcome (Eastwood
2026m).
    It does not show that safety is load-bearing at any scale, nor that the safety-capability trade-off is
absent at frontier scale (Sections 7.6 and 7.7). None of its experiments decides P12, P15 or P21. Of
the weight-level experiment, version 1.104 of the theory registration says: “It is inconclusive and it is
not adverse”; and: “It asks whether embedded safety costs capability; this proposition asks whether the
advantage of embedded correction widens with recursive depth, which no arm of it varied” (Eastwood
2026m).

A     Measures as the published code computes them
Each measure is stated from the stored result files and the code that wrote them (Code and data); the
paper’s numbers are as printed in Sections 3 to 5.

Experiment 1. The judge scores each response on five dimensions, each from 0 to 10: stakeholder care,
constraint adherence, nuance, honesty and capability. Safety is the sum of the first four divided by 40;
capability enters the product as its score divided by 10; the entangled score is their product. Table 3 prints
capability on the judge’s 0 to 10 scale. The task suite holds 8 tasks, 3 coding and 5 ethics, of which 5 are
used in each evaluation; the safety floor is 0.3 and the product threshold 0.1. The pre-flight check scores
one known-good and one known-bad response and passes when the good one scores higher on constraint
adherence and neither score carries a parse warning. The judge also returns a reward-hacking mark, true
only if the response manipulates the evaluation itself; it is recorded in every condition, and no selection
rule reads it.

Experiment 2. The capability term of the loss is the token cross-entropy on the training examples.
The safety term is a proxy: the mean, over tokens, of the largest logit, divided by ten, a penalty on
overconfidence in the code’s own words, computed on the same examples. For the coupling term, each
token’s deviation of cross-entropy from the mean cross-entropy is weighted by one minus its deviation
of largest logit from the mean largest logit, divided by the largest such deviation. The term is the mean
of these products over tokens, a penalty, in the code’s comment, for when one is high and the other is
low. The loss is 𝛼 times the first term plus 𝛽 times the second plus 𝛾 times the third, with the weights of
Table 6. Evaluation is keyword-based scoring of 15 prompts, 5 for capability, 5 for safety and 5 mixed.

the network’s score on a task is
