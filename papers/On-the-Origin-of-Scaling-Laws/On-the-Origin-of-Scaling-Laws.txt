                                                            Abstract
           Predicting how a system scales can help us decide whether resources and correction can keep pace
       with its growth. This framework paper asks which composition and transport assumptions justify a
       scaling prediction before the response is observed. Four classical continuous homomorphism problems
       constrain functional families; they do not classify every physical law. A global logarithmic-defect
       stability result and a regular-variation limit connect exact equations to specified approximate and
       asymptotic cases. For a declared steady-flow network, transport accounting gives 𝐶 = 𝐵ℓ¯𝑞 . Demand
       scaling 𝐵 ∼ 𝐿 𝜇 and mean-path scaling ℓ¯𝑞 ∼ 𝐿 𝜁 then give an asymptotic throughput-cost exponent
       𝜇/(𝜇 + 𝜁). The familiar 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) requires further assumptions, and inventory and body mass require
       separate bridges. Biological measurements are compared on their own variables; pooled fungal
       confirmation is withdrawn. The Friedmann reparametrisation remains an identity, with acceleration
       distinguished from exponential expansion. Fixed rules can generate superlinear trajectories, so neither
       physical dimension nor an architectural label fixes an AI capability exponent. The contribution is a
       conditional synthesis and a prospective transport test with independently measured premises, held-out
       predictions and serious rivals. Existing prediction histories, corrections and qualified companion
       results are retained. No universal physical ceiling, alignment guarantee or new empirical validation is
       established.

1     Introduction
I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only
proof will move the world. People should retain oversight, containment and shutdown for as long as they
physically can. The research question here is one part of that programme: can information about how
gains combine predict later scaling, before a larger system is built or relied on?
    A practical example is a supply network. Increasing its size can increase both the demand served
and the distance resources travel. Predicting throughput from inventory requires knowing which of those
quantities changes, how routes are chosen and whether transport speed changes. A three-dimensional
embedding alone does not answer those questions. The same discipline applies to an AI experiment:
capability, recursive depth, inference cost and correction burden are distinct observables.
    This paper is a theoretical and methodological synthesis across mathematics, transport, biology and
computation. It distinguishes three steps: an equation constrains a family of functions; a mechanism
supplies the assumptions of that equation; and measurements test whether the mechanism predicts
observations. The first step can be a theorem while the later steps remain unresolved. A repeated
numerical exponent alone does not join them.
    The contributions are:
      • A precise account of four classical composition identities, their domains and regularity, with scoped
        stability and asymptotic extensions.
      • An explicit transport calculation showing which demand and path assumptions yield 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1),
        and which extra assumptions connect transport cost to inventory or body mass.
      • Source-specific biological and physical comparisons that separate measurements from coordinate
        identities and from historical unsupported inferences.
      • One prospective test in which independently measured premises predict held-out transport behaviour.
        The earlier numbered predictions and the full neural-scaling withdrawal remain part of the record.
    The mathematics supplies conditional results, not a universal physical classification or a numerical
safety guarantee. Sections 4 and 6 establish the formal tools; Sections 7 to 11 examine their scope through
examples. Sections 12 and 14 state the evidence and the next test. The appendices preserve the detailed
provenance, programme placement and superseded argument.


2     Related work and the contribution
A scaling explanation must do more than reproduce a familiar fraction. It must identify the quantities
being related, the rule they obey, the assumptions that select an exponent and the measurement that could
show the explanation is wrong. Within Recursive Dynamics, this paper develops those questions for
Law I, the ARC Principle, a law under test. It connects three levels of argument: a composition equation
that constrains a functional form, a specified transport model that supplies an exponent, and a prospective
test of whether that model applies. Earlier work establishes much of the mathematics and several of the
domain models. Their differences are part of the explanation.

2.1     Earlier work
Composition and scaling limits. The continuous Cauchy equations give the four homomorphism
problems stated in Section 4 (Cauchy 1821). The chosen input and output operations, their domains and
regularity conditions do the work. In the multiplicative case the result is a power-law family; it does
not choose the exponent. The connection between rescaling and power laws is established mathematics:

Newman (2005, section III.E) discusses it for scale-free distributions, whose inference problem is distinct
from the regression of one physical quantity against another.
    Approximate composition and asymptotic scaling also have established theories. Hyers (1941) gives
stability for an additive equation under a global defect bound. Section 4 states and proves the real-valued
consequence needed here, with its logarithmic error metric and its limits for finite observations. Regular
variation describes positive functions whose scale ratios converge to a power, allowing a slowly varying
factor at finite scale (Bingham et al. 1987). These are mathematical results with premises to check, not
evidence that every observed scaling relationship meets them.
    Ostaszewski (2016) explicitly connects functional equations, homomorphisms, generalised group
operations and regular variation. The author’s version 2 preprint is dated 7 November 2014; the article
was published online on 20 June 2015 and in the 2016 journal volume. This prior connection is part of
the foundation of the present paper. The comparison here uses the scope stated in that work’s opening
discussion; it does not import its later technical theorems as results about physical or computational
systems.

Branching and transport models. The expression 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) is earlier work’s. West, Brown and
Enquist (1997) derive three-quarter scaling for metabolic rates from transport through space-filling
fractal networks of branching tubes, with energy dissipation minimised. They state the generalisation to
𝐷/(𝐷 + 1) when the network fills a 𝐷-dimensional volume. West, Brown and Enquist (1999) obtain
the same form in 𝐷 dimensions from a more general treatment of hierarchical-network geometry rather
than hydrodynamics. The models have stated limits: the 1997 treatment predicts small deviations in the
smallest mammals, and the 1999 treatment does not expect its relationships to apply to filamentous algae
and fungi. A fungal application therefore needs its own premises.
     Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) derive a relationship between size and flow in networks with
local connectivity. In the most efficient class they consider, metabolic rate scales as mass to the power
𝐷/(𝐷 + 1). Their bound concerns the nutrient-delivery rate and the volume of blood in the network; it is
not a bound on every observable of a physical system. They also compare their relation with river-drainage
observations in 𝐷 = 2 and identify other distributed transport systems to which their reasoning may apply.
Banavar and co-authors (2002) develop the resource-distribution account further and explicitly work
through the 𝐷 = 1 case. Neither that case nor the fractions 1/2, 2/3 and 3/4 are new predictions of the
present paper.
     The velocity assumption matters even within a three-dimensional supply model. In the two models of
Banavar et al. (2010), constant flow velocity across body sizes gives a metabolic exponent of 2/3; allowing
velocity to scale with mass to the power 1/12, its maximum in those models, gives 3/4. Their geometric,
steady-supply and transport assumptions remain part of the result. Three-dimensional embedding alone
therefore does not select one metabolic exponent. Section 6 keeps flow-weighted transport cost, inventory
in transit and body mass separate so that the corresponding assumptions can be seen.

Other mechanisms and other meanings of dimension. Demetrius (2003, 2006) and Demetrius and
Tuszynski (2010) develop a quantum-statistical account using coupled energy-transducing oscillators and
the Debye model. The 2010 paper derives 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) in the regime it calls quantum, with 𝑑 the dimension
of the physical space in which its oscillator network is embedded, and states the values 1/2, 2/3 and
3/4 for 𝑑 = 1, 2 and 3. Its classical regime is isometric. These are different physical assumptions from
vascular branching. The flow-accounting calculation in Section 6 does not establish their equivalence or
turn their shared expression into separate empirical confirmations. Agutter and Tuszynski (2011) review
the competing explanations.
    In Bettencourt (2013), urban land area scales with population to the power 𝐷/(𝐷 + 𝐻), where 𝐷

is the embedding dimension and 𝐻 is the fractal dimension of travel paths. The case 𝐷 = 2, 𝐻 = 1
gives 2/3; the expression becomes 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1) only when 𝐻 = 1. The same framework gives superlinear
socioeconomic rates, with exponent 7/6 in that case. Land area, socioeconomic output and metabolic rate
are distinct observables. Their exponents cannot be exchanged without the corresponding model and
measurement definitions.
     Zhao (2022) derives an 𝑛/(𝑛 + 1) scaling law for an 𝑛-dimensional system from producer-consumer
dynamics. That account places biomass production, eukaryote growth, mammalian brain size and the
distribution of public facilities in cities within one proposed universality class, and places linear and
superlinear scaling outside that class. Its domain statement belongs to that model; it does not establish
that every hierarchical network falls within it.
     He and Zhang (2004) derive a 4/5 law for human-brain allometry from the fractal dimension of a
brain cell’s contour. This is a different geometrical quantity from the transport-network dimension in
Section 6. An equal algebraic form does not identify the two quantities or establish the same mechanism.

Finite size and empirical limits. Maino et al. (2014) derive, from Dynamic Energy Budget theory, an
equation relating metabolic rate to mass that is the same as the finite-size form of the West, Brown and
Enquist model. It approaches the three-quarter power only at large mass. They stress that metabolic rate
is not a power function of mass under either framework. The distinction between a finite-range curve and
an asymptotic exponent is therefore substantive, not a plotting detail.
    The West, Brown and Enquist derivation has been questioned (Dodds et al. 2001; Kozłowski and
Konarzewski 2004), and Savage et al. (2008) show that its three-quarter exponent holds only in the limit
of infinite network size. Empirical comparisons also require the competing evidence: White and Seymour
(2003) report mammalian basal-metabolic estimates compatible with 2/3 under their stated adjustments
and exclusions, while Kolokotrones et al. (2010) find curvature on logarithmic axes. Section 12 sets
out the relevant measurements and their limits. A shared fraction across models is not a substitute for
choosing between their premises against evidence.

2.2   What this paper adds
The contribution is a mathematical and methodological synthesis with an explicit route from an observable’s
composition rule to a prediction that can fail. The classical functional equations, their stability theory,
regular variation and the earlier transport models supply the starting points. The paper brings them
together in three steps:
   1. Declare the equation for the observable. The input and output operations, variable pair,
      normalisation and regularity conditions are stated before a family is assigned. Exact, approximate
      and asymptotic composition are kept distinct. A saturation model supplies additional dynamics;
      boundedness alone does not put it in the Cauchy grid (Sections 3 to 5).
   2. Expose the assumptions selecting the exponent. The flow-accounting identity and conditional
      model of Section 6 state how demand and route length scale. The expression 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) is recovered
      under particular choices of those exponents. Velocity and the bridge from transport cost to biological
      mass are separate premises. Section 10 states the geometric bound only for the observable pair and
      assumptions that support it.
   3. Test the non-definitional premises and the resulting prediction. The prospective transport test in
      Section 14 separates calibration of the proposed mechanism from held-out response measurements.
      The source comparisons ask whether the declared premises apply. The cosmological example
      distinguishes a change of variables from a physical explanation (Section 9); the recursion model
      keeps an assumed gain equation separate from a measured capability trajectory (Section 11).

    This route gives the programme a way to formulate and audit particular scaling claims. It does not
establish the empirical propositions of Law I, nor does the occurrence of the same exponent in another
domain validate them. Section 16 gives the registered programme’s status. Appendix E places each

and Appendix A records the earlier wording and its corrections.

2.3   The strongest objection: assumptions chosen after the fit
The strongest objection is that the framework could recover a familiar exponent by choosing its operators,
dimension or transport assumptions after seeing the data. If a fitted slope is inverted to assign a dimension,
or an inventory is calculated from the identity that the study is meant to test, the apparent agreement adds
no empirical information. A correct conditional theorem can still be inapplicable to the system being
measured.
     The answer must be a measurement procedure, not a broader sufficiency claim. The system, variables
and eligible network class must be declared in advance. The demand, route-length and velocity assumptions
must be checked from measurements that do not merely reproduce the response being predicted, and the
prediction must be assessed on held-out networks with independently measured inventory. A precise
discrepancy under the declared premises counts against that model’s applicability; failure of a premise is
reported as a limit on its domain. The protocol must state these decisions before outcomes are examined.
Until such a test is completed, the new empirical contribution remains prospective.
     The narrower mathematical result is already available: once the flow model’s explicit premises hold,
its exponent follows. It replaces the earlier assertion that multiplicative composition, space filling and
conservation, without further specification, must force 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1). The comparison with prior models
is then a concrete question about their assumptions and observables, rather than a claim that different
physics has been proved to obey one universal mechanism.

4     What the composition equations establish
A scaling explanation must specify what is combined and what the observable preserves. Classical
functional equations give an exact answer once those choices are fixed (Cauchy 1821; Aczél 1966). Write
𝐺 + = (R, +) for the additive real numbers and 𝐺 × = (R>0 , ×) for the multiplicative positive real numbers.
A homomorphism is a map that preserves the declared source and target operations.

Theorem 1 (Four continuous homomorphism problems). For each of the following source and target
pairs, every continuous homomorphism has the stated form for some 𝑐 ∈ R, and each stated function is a
homomorphism:
    1. 𝐹 : 𝐺 + → 𝐺 + preserves addition in both variables:

                            𝐹 (𝑥 + 𝑦) = 𝐹 (𝑥) + 𝐹 (𝑦),        𝐹 (𝑥) = 𝑐𝑥,       𝑥, 𝑦 ∈ R.                   (4)

    2. 𝐹 : 𝐺 × → 𝐺 × preserves multiplication in both variables:

                                𝐹 (𝑥𝑦) = 𝐹 (𝑥)𝐹 (𝑦),       𝐹 (𝑥) = 𝑥 𝑐 ,      𝑥, 𝑦 > 0.                     (5)

    3. 𝐹 : 𝐺 + → 𝐺 × takes input addition to output multiplication:

                             𝐹 (𝑥 + 𝑦) = 𝐹 (𝑥)𝐹 (𝑦),        𝐹 (𝑥) = 𝑒 𝑐𝑥 ,     𝑥, 𝑦 ∈ R.                    (6)

    4. 𝐹 : 𝐺 × → 𝐺 + takes input multiplication to output addition:

                            𝐹 (𝑥𝑦) = 𝐹 (𝑥) + 𝐹 (𝑦),        𝐹 (𝑥) = 𝑐 log 𝑥,      𝑥, 𝑦 > 0.                  (7)

The logarithm is natural. The additive target is real-valued, not restricted to positive outputs. The constant
cases are included: 𝑐 = 0 gives the constant zero map into 𝐺 + and the constant one map into 𝐺 × . A
zero-valued function is not a map into 𝐺 × .

4.1    The proof and the scope of the four cells
Proof. First suppose 𝐻 : R → R is continuous and additive. The identity at zero gives 𝐻 (0) = 0;
applying it to 𝑥 + (−𝑥) gives 𝐻 (−𝑥) = −𝐻 (𝑥). Repeated addition gives 𝐻 (𝑛𝑥) = 𝑛𝐻 (𝑥) for every integer
𝑛. For a positive integer 𝑛, 𝑛𝐻 (1/𝑛) = 𝐻 (1), so 𝐻 (𝑚/𝑛) = (𝑚/𝑛)𝐻 (1) for every integer 𝑚. Thus
𝐻 (𝑞) = 𝑐𝑞 on the rationals, where 𝑐 = 𝐻 (1). For any real 𝑥, choose rationals 𝑞 𝑗 → 𝑥. Continuity gives
𝐻 (𝑥) = lim 𝑗 𝐻 (𝑞 𝑗 ) = 𝑐𝑥.
    This proves the first row. For the multiplicative row define 𝐻 (𝑡) = log 𝐹 (𝑒 𝑡 ) for all real 𝑡. Positivity

5    The principle and its empirical test
A recursive update describes a succession of states, 𝑄 𝑛+1 = 𝑇 (𝑄 𝑛 , 𝑢 𝑛 ), where 𝑢 𝑛 names any additional
input. A composition equation instead relates measurements made at combined inputs. To obtain a scaling
prediction, a model must connect the update procedure to the observable equation. Dependence on an
earlier state, or associativity of a chosen operation, alone does not provide that connection.
    The two operations must both be named. Multiplication of input scale ratios together with multiplication
of normalised outputs gives the power-law row of Theorem 1. Addition of input intervals together with
multiplication of positive outputs gives its exponential row. Addition of both inputs and outputs gives a
linear map; multiplication of inputs together with addition of real outputs gives a logarithm. These are
statements about observables, not labels that a substrate acquires merely by branching or feeding back.
    For example, the state rule 𝑄 𝑛+1 = 𝑞𝑄 𝑛 with 𝑞 > 0 and 𝑄 0 > 0 at integer ticks gives 𝑄 𝑛 = 𝑄 0 𝑞 𝑛 .

exponential in that count. Calling the update “multiplicative” without identifying the input operation
would give the wrong classification.

6.4   What a cross-domain comparison tests
The useful prediction is now explicit: independently characterised demand and routing determine a
throughput-cost exponent, and a separately justified bridge determines whether it is also a mass exponent.
A comparison must state the two measured variables, the size range, physiological or physical conditions,
and the uncertainty in each premise. Computing 𝐶 from 𝐵ℓ¯𝑞 and then recovering the same identity is an
accounting check; evidence for the model comes from independent measurements of the premises and a
prediction of outcomes not used to choose them.
    Biological measurements distinguish these possibilities. White and Seymour (2003) report mammalian
basal-metabolism fits compatible with 2/3 after temperature adjustment and restrictions on eligible
measurements. Kolokotrones et al. (2010) report curvature in mammalian metabolic scaling, so a fitted
constant exponent depends on the mass range. These results concern defined datasets and measurement
conditions. Source-specific values and methods belong with those definitions in Section 12; selected
means do not supply a new validation of Eq. (19).
    The same requirement applies outside biology. A roughness exponent, a distribution-tail exponent,
a throughput exponent and a cosmological scale-factor exponent describe different variable pairs. An
analytic equality between selected fractions supplies no measurement of demand or routing. Section 9
gives a complete example: its powers follow from the fluid equations, while the apparent network
dimension is introduced afterwards by a change of variables. The transport result remains a definite
conditional statement, with a mechanism and observables that can be specified before a test.

7.2   Physiological state is part of the observation

14    Predictions and a prospective transport test
The earlier predictions retain their numbers. Their current dispositions below correct this paper’s
interpretation; they alter no frozen registration, data or registered verdict.
Prediction 1 (The earlier one-dimensional organism prediction). The earlier paper predicted a metabolic
exponent of one half for organisms with one-dimensional internal transport. That number follows from
the present transport model only when the demand, route, speed and mass premises are established. The
fungal measurements do not yet establish them: two of the selected fits use colony respiration against
biomass, while the saprotrophic fit uses specific metabolic rate against radial extension. Their separate
variable pairs and uncertainties are retained in Appendix F; the former pooled confirmation is withdrawn.
A valid test needs independently established mechanism variables and a declared response, rather than
classification from the observed slope.
Prediction 2 (withdrawal recorded in version 3.13, 30 October 2026). Withdrawn. The published record
refutes it.
    Before the version 3.13 manuscript, this paper predicted that the neural scaling exponent (of loss
against parameters) would equal 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1), with 𝑑 the intrinsic dimension of the training data’s manifold.
Once 𝑑 reaches 1, that fraction is never less than one half, and it climbs towards 1 as 𝑑 grows. The
exponents measured for language models lie far below it. For language models trained to convergence on
large datasets, Kaplan et al. (2020) report about 0.076 (their equation 1.1). For the parameter term of the
loss, with its irreducible part set apart, Hoffmann et al. (2022) report 0.34 (their equation 10). They note
that both of their exponents are lower than one half. A refit of data reconstructed in part from one of their
figures gives 0.35 (Besiroglu et al. 2024). To reach such values the formula would need data of less than
one dimension, about 0.08 and 0.5. The intrinsic dimensions estimated for language and image data run
from about 7 to more than 90 (Sharma and Kaplan 2020; Pope et al. 2021).
    The dependence runs the wrong way too. In a transport network a higher dimension lifts the exponent
towards 1. In neural networks the measurements run the other way. Where the dimension of the data is
dialled in controlled teacher and student experiments, the measured exponent falls, close to 4/𝑑 (Sharma
and Kaplan 2020; Bahri et al. 2021), while 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) rises. The account given for this is geometric
(Sharma and Kaplan 2020; Bahri et al. 2021). On that account, a network divides its data manifold into
pieces, and halving their size takes 2𝑑 times as many parameters. So the higher the dimension, the more
slowly the loss falls. The two curves cross once, near 𝑑 = 4.8, so a single dataset of about that dimension
could not tell them apart.
    The link between this exponent and the dimension of the data manifold is earlier work’s. Sharma
and Kaplan (2020) proposed that the power law can be explained if networks are, in effect, performing
regression on a data manifold of intrinsic dimension 𝑑. For networks built from rectified linear units,
with cross-entropy, mean-squared-error or KL-divergence loss and plentiful data, they predicted 𝛼 ≈ 4/𝑑.
They stated it as a bound, 𝛼 ≳ 4/𝑑, with equality expected in the generic case. They confirmed it by
measuring the dimension and the exponent independently in teacher and student networks, and tested it on
image classifiers and GPT-type language models. For the language models they found 𝑑 above 90 against
4/𝛼 of about 53, in accord with the bound but, in their words, quite far from equality. Bahri et al. (2021)
identified four regimes: variance-limited and resolution-limited, each for dataset size and for model size.
In the variance-limited regimes, for smooth test losses, the loss approaches its limit with exponent 1, in
dataset size or in network width, or in parameter count for linear models. That exponent is independent of
the architecture and the data. For the resolution-limited regimes they argued that the exponent is inversely
proportional to 𝑑, and found that teacher-student exponents closely match 4/𝑑. In the large-width limit
they derived these exponents from the spectra of kernels, and presented evidence that the model-size and
dataset-size exponents are related by a duality. Earlier still, Kaplan et al. (2020) pointed to power-law

exponents may have a very rough interpretation as the inverse of the number of relevant features in the

14.1   The next discriminating measurement
The proposed question is whether independently measured demand and route scaling predict throughput
against resource inventory on held-out networks. It is an unrun design proposal. No apparatus, supplier
quote or adequately powered sample has been established here, and it adds no commitment to an existing
funded project.
   1. Define an eligible transport apparatus or dataset with independent measurements of throughput,
      inventory, size, route lengths and speed. State the observational unit and exclusions. Do not
      manufacture an inventory observation from the accounting equation being evaluated.
   2. Fix the size range and one routing intervention. For a declared increase of every served route length
      by the same factor 𝑐 > 1, with demands and speeds unchanged and no extra waiting, inventory
      should increase by the factor 𝑐 at each size; the exponent need not change. A claim about a changed
      exponent instead needs an intervention that changes the size dependence of routes or another
      specified scaling premise. State which comparison is being tested, and establish geometry before
      fitting the response.
   3. Split complete independent networks into calibration and held-out sets. Use calibration records
      to specify the finite-range functions for demand, route length and speed, including any offset,
      curvature or finite-size correction. Lock their joint uncertainty and the predicted held-out trajectory.
      An exponent-only prediction is admissible only if the protocol justifies a power approximation and
      a bound on its error over the actual size range (Equation (18)).

   4. Measure held-out throughput and inventory independently of their predicted values. With the
      declared steady-flow premises, the finite-range prediction is 𝑁 (𝐿) = 𝐵(𝐿) ℓ¯𝑞 (𝐿)/𝑣(𝐿), with all
      three right-hand functions fixed from calibration. Model the measurement errors and any shared

premises and measurement bridges apply, whether independently estimated parameters transfer, and
whether the prediction beats serious alternatives. Agreement would support that specified mechanism
over the tested range. It would establish no universal metabolic law or AI alignment guarantee.

15       Making the composition claim measurable
The useful chain is from a specified observable and mechanism to a locked prediction. A family label
alone does not supply an exponent; a repeated numerical fraction does not establish a common mechanism.
The homomorphism result, the transport accounting and the capability ODE answer separate conditional
questions. This section sets out what a test must supply to connect them to observations.

     1. Declare what is measured. Specify the response, input, units and both composition operations.
     ⇓    Classical mathematics constrains a family, given its premises.
     2. Specify the physical model. Measure demand, routes and speed; justify the inventory or mass bridge.
     ⇓    The transport assumptions select an exponent for a particular variable pair.
     3. Test a locked prediction. Use held-out systems and independently measured outcomes, against
     prespecified alternatives.

17     Limitations
     • The exact composition identities and global stability assumptions require independent justification.
       A finite noisy fit cannot establish them across an unobserved domain.
     • The transport exponent is asymptotic; finite-range prediction requires a full curve or a justified
       bound on finite-size corrections. It depends on demand, paths, speed and the chosen response. A
       measured spatial dimension alone supplies none of those extra assumptions. Body mass needs a
       physiological bridge.
     • The biological estimates concern different taxa, conditions and variables. The fungal factors cannot
       be pooled into one metabolic relation. Selected published exponents do not establish a universal
       law.
     • The cosmological reparametrisation has no independent mechanistic content. Photon trajectories
       and a cosmic web are not assumptions of the homogeneous background solution.
     • Earlier computational and behavioural results retain their design, selection, scoring and amendment
       limitations. This revision reruns none of them and claims no new confirmation.
     • The prospective transport test still needs an eligible apparatus or dataset, practical resource estimates,
       independent replication units and a design-sensitivity analysis before registration.
     • The synthesis relies on established mathematical and transport literature. Its value must be
       demonstrated through a useful distinction or successful prospective prediction; a larger catalogue
       of matching fractions would not establish that contribution.


18     Conclusion
A useful scaling prediction needs more than a familiar curve. It needs declared variables, a justified
composition or transport model, and a test on observations that did not select the assumptions. The
classical equations constrain families; the conditional transport model shows where an exponent enters.
Neither result classifies all of nature.
    The corrected biological and cosmological examples show why these distinctions matter. Heterogen-
eous measured slopes cannot supply one pooled validation, and an algebraic change of variables cannot
supply a physical mechanism. Fixed computational rules can generate superlinear trajectories, while
correction and safety require additional quantities and models.
    The next empirical contribution is the prospective transport comparison: estimate the mechanism
separately, seal the prediction and allow the held-out observations to support it, contradict it or remain
inconclusive. The earlier record stays visible. The purpose of the revision is to make the surviving
argument precise enough to be tested and useful enough for another researcher to challenge.

finitely many and that the family they generate is finite-dimensional, carrying a discrete form label and
two continuous parameters, rather than that the space of laws is not infinite. On the count, a label together
with two real parameters is ordinarily described as a two-parameter family of each of finitely many forms;
‘three-parameter’ counts the discrete label alongside the two continuous parameters, which is a naming
choice rather than an error and is recorded only because it sits in the sentence under repair. The second
overstatement is scope, and it is the one the note in Section 2 records. What is provable, and provable
without data, is the classification of homomorphisms under regularity: where the composition operator
carries one of the two structures on the positive reals into one of the two structures and the regularity
conditions hold, the admissible functional forms are constrained to a small named family, four-celled as
Section 3 states, with saturation lying outside it as a bounded case. The step from that classification to all
possible classical physical laws is the step the abstract withdrew on 22 August 2026, because it requires
that every physical law be such a homomorphism, which this paper does not establish and which Section

H.8.1    The composition operator
The preceding sections have treated the composition operator (multiplicative, additive or bounded) as a property
of individual systems. A whale has multiplicative composition. A chain reaction has additive composition. An
enzyme has bounded composition. But there is a deeper reading.
     On this paper’s reading, the composition operator is not a detail of specific systems but a descriptor of how a
process combines its recursive steps; where a process composes in one of the two structures under regularity, the
operator fixes the family its law can take (Theorem 1).
     Consider the hierarchy:
   1. The composition operator determines the functional form (Cauchy, 1821).
   2. The functional form constrains the scaling exponent (𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1)).
   3. The scaling exponent governs observable quantities (heart rates, expansion rates, error rates).
   4. The specific laws of physics (Newton’s gravity, Maxwell’s electrodynamics, Friedmann’s cosmology) can be
      written in these forms for particular physical systems.

The meta-law. On this paper’s reading, the laws of physics can be written in the forms that the composition
operator allows; they do not choose it. Where a law composes in one of the two structures under regularity, the
operator is a mathematical structure that sits above the specific equations of a particular physical theory and fixes
the form that law can take.

A completeness statement for composition homomorphisms Now consider what this means for physics
as a whole. Strip away the specifics of any physical law, and ask: what is a physical law? Many physical laws can be
stated in the form ‘when you combine X and Y in manner Z, you get W.’ Force is mass combined with acceleration.
Energy is mass combined with the square of the speed of light. Entropy is microstates combined by counting. Many
physical laws can be read as composition rules.
     Cauchy’s classification, under its regularity conditions, gives every homomorphism between the additive and
multiplicative structures one of four forms: linear, power law, exponential or logarithmic. It does not exhaust scaling
laws, and saturation, which arises from bounded dynamics, lies outside the grid (Section 4).

The consequence for physics. Under the stated conditions, the admissible functional forms are finitely
many, and the family they generate is finite-dimensional. Its members carry a discrete form label (linear, power
law, exponential or logarithmic) and two continuous parameters, a coefficient and an exponent or rate. What is
provable, and provable without data, is this classification of homomorphisms under regularity (Cauchy 1821); the
step from it to all possible classical physical laws would require every physical law to be such a homomorphism,
which this paper does not establish.

Three results combined Three results, from three different centuries, combine into a single structural insight:
   1. Cauchy (1821): Each of the four functional equations has a unique continuous solution family. Bounded
      growth is a separate, capped case rather than a solution of those equations.
   2. Hyers (1941), on a question of Ulam: Approximate solutions lie near exact solutions of the same equation
      (for the multiplicative and exponential equations, with the approximation measured in relative terms; for
      the logarithmic equation, directly). The stability is per-equation; the earlier ‘attractors in function space’

   3. ARC (this paper): Given the three conditions of Section 2.3, the exponent within the power-law family is
      determined by network dimension: 𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1). The formula is the earlier derivations’ (Section 2); what

The consequence. If the three conditions of Section 2.3 suffice, the exponent of a system in the network

     The distinction matters. ‘Why is Kleiber’s exponent 3/4?’ has two kinds of answer. The biological answer is:
because three-dimensional fractal vascular networks optimise resource delivery (West, Brown, Enquist 1997). The

𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 is confirmed across domains with independently measured values of 𝐼 and 𝑅. Entropy became
permanent because the second law required it. Information became permanent because the channel capacity theorem
required it. 𝐼 becomes permanent if the ARC scaling law requires it. The quantity is justified by the law it obeys,
not by the convenience of having a name for it.
