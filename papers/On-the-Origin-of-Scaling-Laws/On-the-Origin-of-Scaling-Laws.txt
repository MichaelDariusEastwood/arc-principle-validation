                                                          Abstract
            If the way a process combines its steps fixes the family of forms its growth can take, then the
       family a self-improving system’s capability will follow as its recursion deepens can be read from
       how it composes, before it scales and while correction can still be built into it. This paper asks
       why scaling laws in recursive composition keep taking the same few mathematical forms. Where
       a process has a well defined composition operator on its input and output and meets the regularity
       conditions that Cauchy’s functional equations require, the admissible forms fall into four families:
       linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic. This is a conditional classification, not a survey of
       nature, and saturation lies outside it. Within the power-law family, the exponent 𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) for
       a 𝑑-dimensional hierarchical network is earlier work’s; this paper gives an account of why those
       derivations, from different assumptions, converge, and a proof that its three conditions suffice does not
       yet exist. The relation is read as a network-partition identity with a stated domain: published exponents
       for filamentous fungi (𝑑 = 1) do not reject it (a mean of three factors on different axes; no equivalence
       shown), those for vertebrates and invertebrates with three-dimensional transport (𝑑 = 3) agree with it
       on the group means compiled here, though the mammalian exponent is debated, and no organism with
       a two-dimensional hierarchical network has been identified. Under 𝑑 = 2/(1 + 3𝑤) the Friedmann
       solutions take the same form, an identity that carries no evidence. For finite 𝑑 the exponent stays below
       one, a bound on systems of that class only; how capability grows with recursive depth is a different
       pair of variables, which this geometry does not fix and which has to be measured. Five predictions are
       stated; one, on neural scaling exponents, is withdrawn because published measurements refute it.

1    Introduction
If the way a process combines its steps fixes the family of forms its growth can take, then how a
self-improving system composes tells us, before it scales, which family its growth with recursive depth can
follow; its exponent still has to be measured (Section 11). That matters for artificial intelligence. Whether
correction can keep pace with a system that improves itself depends on how fast its capability compounds,
and correction is best built into such a system before it scales, while people still hold oversight of it. This
paper traces the forms behind Law I, the ARC Principle, a law under test, to their mathematical origin, in
the systems where scaling laws were first measured: organisms, materials and the expanding universe.
     A mouse’s heart beats about 600 times per minute. An elephant’s beats about 28 (Merck Veterinary
Manual 2004). A blue whale’s, on a foraging dive, beats 4 to 8 (Goldbogen et al. 2019).
     If you plot heart rate against body mass across mammals, the points fall close to a straight line on
logarithmic axes, with a slope close to − 14 , from the mouse to the whale (Stahl 1967; Noujaim et al. 2004).
     Why?
     For filamentous fungi, published colony-level measurements put the exponent of energy use against
colony size near one half, and this paper predicts exactly 12 for a single thread (Section 14). Not 34 , like a
mammal. Not 32 , like the matter-dominated universe. A different fraction entirely.
     Why 12 for fungi, but 34 for mammals? And why do these same fractions appear in cosmology?
     Now consider something entirely different. The universe is expanding. During the matter-dominated
era, the scale factor grew as time raised to the power 23 . During the radiation era, it grew as time to the
power 12 .
     Why 23 ? Why 12 ? And why are these the same fractions that appear in biology?
     Now consider one more thing. When a rock shatters, the distribution of fragment sizes follows a
power law; Section 6.1 gives the status of its exponents.
     This paper argues that these questions share one mathematical answer, under conditions it states, and
grades where that answer is evidence and where it is only an identity.
     The answer has two parts, and the paper grades each. The first is mathematical: where a process has
a composition operator and meets regularity conditions, the way its steps combine fixes the family its
growth can take, among four (Section 4). The second is the exponent: within the power-law family, the
formula 𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) for a 𝑑-dimensional hierarchical network, which earlier work derived (Section 2),
gives 12 , 23 and 34 , and this paper offers an account of why derivations from different assumptions arrive at
it. Where the fractions reappear in cosmology the agreement is an identity under a change of variables,
and it carries no evidence (Section 9).
     Our contributions are:
    • An account of convergence. Cauchy’s multiplicative functional equation, with space filling
      and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow, gives the power-law family and
      the exponent 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1); this is offered as the reason why the earlier derivations, from different
      assumptions, converge on a formula that is theirs (Section 2). A proof that the three conditions
      suffice does not yet exist.
    • The classification applied to scaling forms. The four Cauchy families, linear, power law,
      exponential and logarithmic, set out as the homomorphisms between the additive and multiplicative
      structures under regularity, with saturation placed outside them and the variable pair named at each
      site (Sections 3 to 5).
    • The relation as a network-partition identity. Its domain and its failures stated, and its comparison
      with published exponents in biology and physics graded row by row (Sections 6 and 12).
    • The geometric bound, scoped. The exponent stays below one for every finite 𝑑 in the class of systems
      whose growth is carried through a space-filling hierarchical network, with the counterexamples

2     Related work and what this paper adds
The mathematics of this paper is two hundred years old. The biological data is ninety years old. The
cosmological solution is a century old. It is reasonable to ask: what, precisely, is the contribution?

2.1     Earlier work
The 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) formula itself is not new. It is derived, in its general form or as a special case, in
earlier work, and the first entry below gives each work what it states: West, Brown and Enquist (1997)
from fractal branching networks, Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) and Banavar and co-authors
(2002) from geometric constraints on transportation networks, He & Zhang (2004) from fractal cell
geometry, Demetrius (2003, 2006) and Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010) from quantum oscillator coupling
in the Debye model, Bettencourt (2013) from urban scaling theory, as a special case, Zhao (2022) from
producer-consumer dynamics, and Maino et al. (2014) from Dynamic Energy Budget theory. Each
derivation starts from different assumptions and arrives at the same functional form. This convergence is
itself remarkable and requires explanation.

2.2     What this paper adds
The contribution is the unification. One formula, 𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1), connects fungi, mammals, surface
physics and, as an identity, the expansion of the universe, with earthquake energy as a variable-pair case
study and rock fracture awaiting sourced values (Section 6.1). One equation with no fitted parameter:
𝑑 is set by the network’s dimension. The claim is not that this formula is original, but that Cauchy’s
functional equations, combined with the space-filling and conservation conditions, explain why the
earlier derivations, from different starting assumptions, arrive at the same form. That invariance under
rescaling forces a power law, through the multiplicative functional equation, is a standard result (Newman
2005, section III.E); what this paper adds is the account of why the derivations of 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) meet the
space-filling and conservation conditions that select the exponent within that family. The derivation of
West et al. (1997) has itself been questioned (Dodds et al. 2001; Kozłowski and Konarzewski 2004), and
its three-quarter exponent shown to hold only in the limit of infinite network size (Savage et al. 2008);
Section 2.3 states what follows if the convergence reflects shared assumptions.

    A formal proof that those conditions are sufficient does not yet exist (see the strongest objection,
below). Set against the earlier work, the paper’s claims are that it has:
   1. Given an account of why derivations from different assumptions converge on one formula.
      That account is what this paper adds; the formula, and the values 12 , 23 and 34 that it gives, are the
      earlier work’s. West, Brown and Enquist (1997) derive the three-quarter power law for metabolic
      rates from a model of transport through space-filling fractal networks of branching tubes, a landmark
      result, and note that, because the network must fill the entire 𝐷-dimensional volume, the result
      generalises to 𝐷/(𝐷 +1); West, Brown and Enquist (1999) obtain 𝐷/(𝐷 +1) in 𝐷 dimensions again,
      from a more general model based on the geometry rather than the hydrodynamics of hierarchical
      networks. Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) derive a general relationship between size and
      flow rates in arbitrary networks with local connectivity, and find that in the most efficient class
      of networks in 𝐷 dimensions metabolic rate scales as mass to the power 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1), which gives
      quarter-power scaling for 𝐷 = 3; Banavar et al. (2010) show, in two models of resource distribution
      networks, that the exponent of metabolic rate on body mass is 23 if the velocity of flow remains
      constant and can attain a maximum of 34 if the velocity scales with its maximum exponent, 1/12.
      Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010) derive the exponent 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) for the scaling of metabolic rate with
      body size in the regime they call quantum, where 𝑑 is the dimension of the physical space in which
      their network of coupled energy-transducing oscillators is embedded, and state its values 12 , 23 and 34
      for 𝑑 = 1, 2 and 3; in the regime they call classical the scaling is isometric. Bettencourt (2013) sets
      out a theoretical framework that predicts the average social, spatial and infrastructural properties of
      cities as a set of scaling relations. In it the land area of a city scales as its population to the power
      𝐷/(𝐷 + 𝐻) in 𝐷 dimensions, where 𝐻 is the fractal dimension of travel paths; the exponent is 23
      for 𝐷 = 2 and 𝐻 = 1, and it has the form 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1) only when 𝐻 = 1. Zhao (2022) derives the
      𝑛/(𝑛 + 1) power scaling law for an 𝑛-dimensional system from producer-consumer dynamics. He
      and Zhang (2004) derive a 4/5 law for the human brain, the value 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) takes at 𝑑 = 4, from
      the fractal dimension of a brain cell’s contour. Maino et al. (2014) derive from dynamic energy
      budget theory an equation for metabolic rate against mass, the same equation as the finite-size form
      of West, Brown and Enquist’s model, which converges on the three-quarter power only in the limit
      of large mass; they stress that under neither framework is metabolic rate a power function of mass.
      The surface area hypothesis predicts 23 but not 34 or 12 . The formula 𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) predicts all three
      from a single equation with one input, the dimension of the hierarchical transport network. The
      𝑑 = 1 value is not rejected by published fungal data (a mean of three factors on different axes;
      no equivalence shown) and the 𝑑 = 3 value agrees with published group means; the 𝑑 = 2 value
      appears in cosmology as an identity, which carries no evidence (Section 9), and in physics in the
      rows whose grades Section 6.1 gives; in biology it is untested (Section 12).
   2. Connected biological scaling to physics scaling. The same formula gives Kleiber’s 34 law
      (biology), KPZ roughness at 12 (surface physics), the specific-heat analogue of two-dimensional
      percolation at 23 (statistical mechanics) and, once their values are sourced, fragmentation in 2D and
      3D (materials science). Earlier work already takes the form beyond organisms: Banavar, Maritan
      and Rinaldo (1999) verify the scaling relation that their theorem predicts on observational data
      from river drainage basins, where 𝐷 = 2, and say that their results should apply to distributed
      networks that carry water, blood, sewage, food, air and electrical currents; Zhao (2022) places
      biomass production, the growth of eukaryotes, mammalian brain size and the distribution of public
      facilities in cities in one universality class. The physics cases named here are what this paper adds.
   3. Set out the geometric scaling bound. That the exponent stays below 1 is in the earlier work.
      Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) state as a theorem that, in any spanning network in 𝐷
      dimensions, the rate at which nutrients are delivered scales at most as the 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1) power of the

      volume of blood in the network, so that metabolic rate does not scale linearly with mass; Demetrius
      & Tuszynski (2010) note that their exponent tends to one as 𝑑 increases; and Zhao (2022) states
      that the scaling is sub-linear. Nor do the earlier explanations all rest on dissipation: West, Brown

      space. It fails for nearest-neighbour interactions, random walks, and gravitational clustering. The
      earlier works state limits of their own: West, Brown and Enquist (1997) predict small deviations
      from quarter-power scaling in the smallest mammals; West, Brown and Enquist (1999) do not
      expect their relationships to apply to filamentous algae and fungi, and filamentous fungi are this

      velocity of flow does not vary significantly with body mass and towards linear scaling where there
      is no vascular system; Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010), as the first entry says, keep 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) to the
      regime they call quantum; and Zhao (2022) places linear and super-linear scalings outside his class.
      The three failures named here are the ones this paper states.
   6. Connected physical scaling to cognitive scaling. The same framework that constrains physical
      exponents below 1 allows cognitive exponents above 1. Exponents above 1 are in the earlier work
      too: in Bettencourt (2013) the socioeconomic rates of a city scale with its population to a power
      above 1, which is 7/6 for 𝐷 = 2 and 𝐻 = 1, in the framework that gives its land area a power below
      1; and Zhao (2022) attributes the super-linear scalings he places outside his class to resources that
      flow more efficiently in larger systems. Neither is about cognition; the connection to recursive
      self-reference is the one this paper makes (Section 11).
   7. Shown that the admissible forms are finitely many. Under the stated conditions the admissible
      functional forms are finitely many, and the family they generate is finite-dimensional: a discrete
      form label and two continuous parameters, a coefficient and an exponent or rate. The step from this
      classification to all possible classical physical laws would require every physical law to be such a
      homomorphism, which this paper does not establish (Section 15).
   8. Identified the dimensional ladder. On this paper’s reading, the universe’s history is a monotonic
      increase in effective network dimension: 𝑑 = 1 (radiation era) to 𝑑 = 2 (matter era) to 𝑑 = 3
      (biology). Each step produces a higher scaling exponent and more efficient composition. The
      trajectory through composition space has a direction.
   9. Constructed the phase diagram of complexity. Three phases (sublinear, exponential, saturation)
      with a critical boundary at 𝑑 → ∞. On the framework’s reading, the operator that dominates
      changes at the transition from matter domination to dark energy domination; that is a reading, not a
      thermodynamic phase transition.

2.3   The strongest objection
One could object that the agreement of the earlier derivations on 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) reflects shared implicit
assumptions rather than independent physical confirmation. All models assume resource distribution
across 𝑑 dimensions with some form of linear conservation, which may inevitably yield 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) by
dimensional analysis. The ‘independence’ of the derivations may be illusory.
    We acknowledge this possibility. If the convergence reflects shared mathematical structure rather
than independent physical mechanisms, then the Cauchy framework would explain why that structure
produces the specific form it does, if multiplicative composition with conservation in 𝑑-dimensional
space admits no alternative to 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1); whether it does is the open problem stated below. The deeper
question is whether these three conditions (multiplicative composition, space filling and conservation) are
themselves inevitable features of any system that distributes resources hierarchically. If so, 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) is
a mathematical necessity for all resource distribution systems, not a contingent property of particular
models. Answering this question requires a formal proof of the sufficiency of these three conditions
independent of any specific physical mechanism. That proof does not yet exist. It is the most important
open problem in this programme.


3     Three ways things grow
Many growth processes show one of three named patterns. Where a growth process has a well defined
composition operator on its input and its output, and meets the regularity conditions stated in Section 4,
the first two are among the admissible forms; the third, saturation, comes from bounded dynamics and
lies outside that classification. The classification is of admissible functions under those conditions, not a
survey of nature:
Definition 1 (Pattern 1: the power law). Big things grow proportionally slower. A city ten times larger
does not need ten times more petrol stations: it needs about six times more (Bettencourt et al. 2007). A
whale ten times heavier does not need ten times more energy: it needs about five and a half times more.
Each doubling of size gives less than a doubling of the output.

Definition 2 (Pattern 2: the exponential). Each step multiplies the previous. One infected person infects
three, who each infect three more, giving nine, then twenty-seven. One fissioning uranium atom releases
neutrons that split two more atoms, which split four, then eight, then sixteen. After eighty generations,
about one microsecond, a single atom has become a trillion trillion atoms.

4     The answer
The French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the answer in 1821, though he did not know he
was answering this question.
    Cauchy asked: what mathematical functions satisfy the property that combining inputs is the same
as combining outputs?
Theorem 1 (Cauchy 1821). Under regularity (continuity, or measurability, or boundedness on an interval),
a function on the positive reals that carries one of the two structures (addition, multiplication) into one of
the two structures falls into exactly four families, one per cell of the two-by-two grid:
    If inputs add and outputs add:

                                          𝑓 (𝑥 + 𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) + 𝑓 (𝑦)                                       (4)

The only regular solution is linear: 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑐𝑥.
   If inputs multiply and outputs multiply:

                                           𝑓 (𝑥 · 𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) · 𝑓 (𝑦)                                      (5)

The only regular solution is a power law: 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑥 𝑐 .
   If inputs add and outputs multiply:

                                           𝑓 (𝑥 + 𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) · 𝑓 (𝑦)                                      (6)

The only regular solution is an exponential: 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑒 𝑐𝑥 .
   If inputs multiply and outputs add:

                                           𝑓 (𝑥 · 𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) + 𝑓 (𝑦)                                      (7)

The only regular solution is logarithmic: 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑐 log 𝑥.
    This exhausts the homomorphisms between the additive and multiplicative structures on the positive
reals. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure:
saturating behaviour, critical exponents, curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers all lie outside
the grid, and the grid says nothing about them.
    This is not a theory or a model. It is a mathematical theorem. It has been proven for two hundred
years: Cauchy proved it for continuous functions, and the forms under measurability or boundedness on
an interval are later results.

    Saturation is a different matter, and Theorem 1 does not cover it: if growth has a physical ceiling (a
glass can only hold so much water; an enzyme has a finite number of binding sites), the system must
approach that ceiling and flatten. A saturation curve such as Equation (3) is one smooth way to do so; it
comes from bounded dynamics, not from a Cauchy equation.

The consequence. Under regularity, a function that carries one of the two structures on the positive
reals into one of the two structures falls into exactly four families: linear, power law, exponential, and
logarithmic. There is no fifth cell, because there is no third structure and no third slot.
    This is a completeness statement about homomorphisms. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because
most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure. Saturating behaviour, critical exponents,
curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers all lie outside the grid, and the grid says nothing about
them. Saturation, in particular, arises from bounded dynamics (finite carrying capacity, mass action,
receptor occupancy, depletion): it is not a Cauchy family, and any presentation of it as one is a category
error. The earlier framing of this result as a physics-style no-go theorem, comparable to Bell’s theorem
(Bell 1964) or Weinberg-Witten (Weinberg and Witten 1980), overstated its reach and has been withdrawn:
Bell and Weinberg-Witten constrain what can physically exist, whereas this grid enumerates ways of
writing one identity in four charts, and that is a category difference.
    Where the physics does select a canonical composition variable (time and sequential steps composing
additively; scale ratios composing multiplicatively), the grid picks a family. Where no canonical chart
exists, the label carries no physical information, and the family assignment must state its variable pair
rather than infer one from the substrate.

Assumption 3: Hyers-Ulam stability. Hyers (1941), answering a question Ulam posed (Ulam 1960),
proved that a function approximately satisfying the additive Cauchy equation lies close to an exact additive
solution. When the approximation is measured in relative terms (on logarithms), the multiplicative and
exponential equations reduce to the additive one, and the logarithmic equation reduces to it directly, so
each approximate solution lies close to an exact solution of that same equation. In physical terms: if a
system’s composition is approximately multiplicative (or approximately additive, or approximately obeys
the exponential or logarithmic Cauchy equation), then its scaling law will lie close to a power law (or the
linear, exponential, or logarithmic form, respectively). Within its own equation, each form is stable: real
systems perturbed away from the ideal remain near an exact solution of the equation they approximately
satisfy. The earlier framing that made the three forms “stable attractors” in function space is withdrawn,
because a function satisfying none of the equations is not drawn toward any of them (see the Foundational
Paper, §2.2, and the Executive Summary, where the per-equation stability condition is stated). Empirical
ubiquity is therefore not a consequence of Hyers-Ulam itself: it depends on how often systems in nature
approximately satisfy one of these equations in the first place, which is a separate empirical question that
this theorem cannot settle.

small deviations (the assumptions are only approximately satisfied), and where to look for new physics
(wherever an assumption is systematically violated). How often systems in nature satisfy one of these
equations in the first place is a separate empirical question that stability cannot settle.


5    The principle
Many growing systems amplify themselves recursively: each output becomes input for the next step.
Under the stated composition and regularity conditions, the ways the steps can combine give the first two
forms below; the linear and logarithmic cells of the grid are set out in Section 4, and the third case below,
a capped process, lies outside the grid:
    • If each step multiplies the input by a fraction (like blood vessels branching into smaller vessels,
      each carrying a fraction of the flow), the result is a power law.
    • If each step adds independently (like radioactive atoms decaying one by one, or neutrons splitting
      atoms that release more neutrons), the result is an exponential.
    • If growth is capped by a physical limit (like enzyme binding sites filling up), the result is a
      saturation curve, which comes from bounded dynamics rather than from a Cauchy equation.

9.5     What this does not mean
The Friedmann-ARC mapping is an algebraic identity. It does not mean that the universe is a biological
organism, or that biology causes the universe to expand, or that cosmology ‘explains’ metabolism. It
means that the mathematical structure governing optimal network partition in 𝑑 dimensions is the same
mathematical structure that appears in the Friedmann equation when 𝑤 is related to dimensionality by
𝑑 = 2/(1 + 3𝑤). The physical mechanisms are entirely different. The mathematics is the same. The
claim is structural, not causal: two apparently unrelated phenomena, biological metabolic scaling and
cosmic expansion, are written in the same mathematical form under the change of variables, which is all
the identity shows.

11.1    Quantum error correction: three regimes classified

single technology.
    A quantum computer’s raw error rate is a physical process: each qubit decoheres through interaction
with its environment. Independent errors at a fixed rate per operation make an error-free run exponentially
unlikely as circuits deepen. Left uncorrected, this is Pattern 2, and it makes useful quantum computation
impossible beyond roughly the inverse of the error rate per operation.
    Quantum error correction changes the composition operator. It takes the output of each layer of
computation and feeds it back through a recursive correction cycle: measure syndromes, identify errors,
apply corrections, and feed the corrected state forward as input to the next cycle. This is recursive
amplification with bounded composition: below threshold, each correction cycle holds the logical error
per cycle at a fixed level for a given code distance, instead of letting it compound with depth. On this
paper’s reading that is Pattern 3, saturation, which lies outside the Cauchy grid.

by a factor Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02 for each increase of two (Google Quantum AI and Collaborators 2025); the
preprint was public from 24 August 2024 and the article appeared in Nature on 9 December 2024. Code

12.3    Derivations from different physics, one form
The most striking feature of the 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) formula is that it has been derived from radically different
physics. West, Brown and Enquist (1997; 1999) derived it from the geometry of hierarchical, space-filling
fractal vascular networks. Separately, Demetrius (2003; 2006) and Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010) derived
the same formula from quantum statistical mechanics, applying the Debye model of thermal properties to
coupled energy-transducing oscillator networks embedded in 𝑑-dimensional space. Different physics.
Different starting assumptions. The same equation. The same predictions: 𝑑 = 1 gives 12 , 𝑑 = 2 gives 23 ,
𝑑 = 3 gives 34 , and 𝑑 → ∞ gives 1.
    The field has treated these as competing theories (reviewed in Agutter & Tuszynski 2011). This
paper’s reading is that they are not competing but complementary: both are physical instantiations of
the same set of mathematical constraints. Cauchy’s multiplicative functional equation (1821) constrains
any system with continuous multiplicative composition to the power-law family. The 𝑑-dimensional
space-filling condition, combined with a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow, then
constrains the exponent within that family to 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1). West’s branching networks satisfy multiplicative
composition (branching ratios), space-filling, and energy minimisation. Demetrius’s oscillator chains
satisfy multiplicative composition (Debye functions), 𝑑-dimensional embedding, and steady-state energy
balance. Both satisfy the three conditions. If those conditions suffice, both must arrive at the same
formula and the convergence is not coincidental; whether they suffice is the open problem of Section 2.3.
    To our knowledge, this explanation for why the two derivations converge has not been previously
published. The observation that Cauchy’s theorem, combined with space-filling and conservation, provides
the meta-constraint underlying both programmes appears to be novel; no dated literature search has been
recorded for it (Appendix E).

The three values. The earlier derivations already give 12 , 23 and 34 from the same formula (Section 2).

Prediction 2 (withdrawn, 31 October 2026). Withdrawn. The published record refutes it.
    Until this version, this paper predicted that the neural scaling exponent (of loss against parameters)
would equal 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1), with 𝑑 the intrinsic dimension of the training data’s manifold. Once 𝑑 reaches 1,
that fraction is never less than one half, and it climbs towards 1 as 𝑑 grows. The exponents measured
for language models lie far below it. For language models trained to convergence on large datasets,
Kaplan et al. (2020) report about 0.076 (their equation 1.1). For the parameter term of the loss, with its
irreducible part set apart, Hoffmann et al. (2022) report 0.34 (their equation 10). They note that both of
their exponents are lower than one half. A refit of data reconstructed in part from one of their figures
gives 0.35 (Besiroglu et al. 2024). To reach such values the formula would need data of less than one
dimension, about 0.08 and 0.5. The intrinsic dimensions estimated for language and image data run from
about 7 to more than 90 (Sharma and Kaplan 2020; Pope et al. 2021).
    The dependence runs the wrong way too. In a transport network a higher dimension lifts the exponent
towards 1. In neural networks the measurements run the other way. Where the dimension of the data is
dialled in controlled teacher and student experiments, the measured exponent falls, close to 4/𝑑 (Sharma
and Kaplan 2020; Bahri et al. 2021), while 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) rises. The account given for this is geometric
(Sharma and Kaplan 2020; Bahri et al. 2021). On that account, a network divides its data manifold into
pieces, and halving their size takes 2𝑑 times as many parameters. So the higher the dimension, the more
slowly the loss falls. The two curves cross once, near 𝑑 = 4.8, so a single dataset of about that dimension
could not tell them apart.
    The link between this exponent and the dimension of the data manifold is earlier work’s. Sharma
and Kaplan (2020) proposed that the power law can be explained if networks are, in effect, performing
regression on a data manifold of intrinsic dimension 𝑑. For networks built from rectified linear units,
with cross-entropy, mean-squared-error or KL-divergence loss and plentiful data, they predicted 𝛼 ≈ 4/𝑑.
They stated it as a bound, 𝛼 ≳ 4/𝑑, with equality expected in the generic case. They confirmed it by
measuring the dimension and the exponent independently in teacher and student networks, and tested it on
image classifiers and GPT-type language models. For the language models they found 𝑑 above 90 against

identified four regimes: variance-limited and resolution-limited, each for dataset size and for model size.

15     The composition operator
The preceding sections have treated the composition operator (multiplicative, additive or bounded) as a
property of individual systems. A whale has multiplicative composition. A chain reaction has additive
composition. An enzyme has bounded composition. But there is a deeper reading.
    On this paper’s reading, the composition operator is not a detail of specific systems but a descriptor of
how a process combines its recursive steps; where a process composes in one of the two structures under
regularity, the operator fixes the family its law can take (Theorem 1).
    Consider the hierarchy:
   1. The composition operator determines the functional form (Cauchy, 1821).
   2. The functional form constrains the scaling exponent (𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1)).
   3. The scaling exponent governs observable quantities (heart rates, expansion rates, error rates).
   4. The specific laws of physics (Newton’s gravity, Maxwell’s electrodynamics, Friedmann’s cosmology)
      can be written in these forms for particular physical systems.

The meta-law. On this paper’s reading, the laws of physics can be written in the forms that the
composition operator allows; they do not choose it. Where a law composes in one of the two structures
under regularity, the operator is a mathematical structure that sits above the specific equations of a
particular physical theory and fixes the form that law can take.

15.1    A completeness statement for composition homomorphisms
Now consider what this means for physics as a whole. Strip away the specifics of any physical law, and
ask: what is a physical law? Many physical laws can be stated in the form ‘when you combine X and Y in
manner Z, you get W.’ Force is mass combined with acceleration. Energy is mass combined with the
square of the speed of light. Entropy is microstates combined by counting. Many physical laws can be
read as composition rules.
    Cauchy’s classification, under its regularity conditions, gives every homomorphism between the
additive and multiplicative structures one of four forms: linear, power law, exponential or logarithmic. It
does not exhaust scaling laws, and saturation, which arises from bounded dynamics, lies outside the grid
(Section 4).

The consequence for physics. Under the stated conditions, the admissible functional forms are
finitely many, and the family they generate is finite-dimensional. Its members carry a discrete form
label (linear, power law, exponential or logarithmic) and two continuous parameters, a coefficient and an
exponent or rate. What is provable, and provable without data, is this classification of homomorphisms
under regularity (Cauchy 1821); the step from it to all possible classical physical laws would require every
physical law to be such a homomorphism, which this paper does not establish.

15.2    Three results combined
Three results, from three different centuries, combine into a single structural insight:

   1. Cauchy (1821): Each of the four functional equations has a unique continuous solution family.
      Bounded growth is a separate, capped case rather than a solution of those equations.
   2. Hyers (1941), on a question of Ulam: Approximate solutions lie near exact solutions of the same
      equation (for the multiplicative and exponential equations, with the approximation measured in
      relative terms; for the logarithmic equation, directly). The stability is per-equation; the earlier
      ‘attractors in function space’ phrasing is withdrawn.
   3. ARC (this paper): Given the three conditions of Section 2.3, the exponent within the power-law
      family is determined by network dimension: 𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1). The formula is the earlier derivations’
      (Section 2); what this paper adds is the account of why they arrive at it.

The consequence. If the three conditions of Section 2.3 suffice, the exponent of a system in the
network class is fixed by its network’s dimension, whatever selection does; selection acts on whether
and how such a network is built. Under the three conditions named in Section 2.3, a three-dimensional
network gives three quarters; measured values range from about 0.67 to 0.75, and the conditions are where
the debate lies. Status: an invariant relation under test, conditional on the three sufficiency conditions that
Section 2.3 identifies (multiplicative composition, space-filling, conservation), a formal proof of which
does not yet exist and is named there as the most important open problem in the programme.
    The distinction matters. ‘Why is Kleiber’s exponent 3/4?’ has two kinds of answer. The biological
answer is: because three-dimensional fractal vascular networks optimise resource delivery (West, Brown,
Enquist 1997). The mathematical answer is that, given the three conditions, no other value is possible for
multiplicative composition in three dimensions. The biological answer explains the mechanism. The
mathematical answer explains why that mechanism produces that specific number. Given those conditions,
the mechanism could not have produced any other.
    This is the difference between a law of nature (describing what happens) and a meta-law (constraining
what can happen). The speed of light constrains what velocities are possible. The composition operator
constrains what scaling forms are possible.

15.3    The phase diagram of complexity
On this paper’s reading, a system with a composition operator and an effective dimension occupies a
position on a phase diagram defined by the two. The diagram has three phases:
Definition 4 (Phase I: sublinear scaling). Multiplicative composition, finite 𝑑, 0 < 𝛼 < 1. This
is the domain of physical systems: organisms, ecosystems, city infrastructure networks (sublinear in
population; Bettencourt et al. 2007), geological processes, cosmic structure. Every system in Phase I
suffers diminishing returns. Bigger is better, but with decreasing marginal gains. A whale is more efficient
per gram than a mouse, but the improvement slows as mass increases. Phase I is the domain of sustainable
complexity. The scaling exponent tells you how severe the tax is: 𝑑 = 1 pays 50% (𝛼 = 0.5), 𝑑 = 2 pays
33% (𝛼 = 0.667), 𝑑 = 3 pays 25% (𝛼 = 0.75). Higher-dimensional networks are more efficient, but the
tax never reaches zero.
Definition 5 (Phase II: exponential growth). Additive composition, outside the network class (under the
cosmological mapping the exponential, at 𝑤 = −1, sits at 𝑑 = −1). This is the domain of unconstrained
amplification: nuclear chain reactions, viral epidemics, compound interest, dark energy. Phase II systems
grow without diminishing returns: growth accelerates. But Phase II is inherently unsustainable in physical
systems. Chain reactions exhaust their fuel. Epidemics run out of susceptible hosts. Bubbles burst. If
dark energy stays constant, the universe’s dark energy phase is sustained Phase II behaviour, the only
known example, and drives the universe toward heat death: maximum entropy, zero structure. Whether it
stays constant is open (Section 15.5).

Definition 6 (Phase III: saturation). Bounded composition, finite ceiling. This is the domain of
constrained systems: enzyme kinetics, logistic population growth, controlled nuclear reactors, market
saturation. Phase III systems approach their ceiling and stop. Growth is self-limiting.

The critical boundary. Phase I ends at the critical line 𝑑 → ∞, 𝛼 → 1, the limit of the network class;
Phase II lies outside that class. This is the geometric scaling bound. In cosmology, it corresponds to
𝑤 = −1/3, and the expansion past it is still a power law, accelerating, which becomes exponential only at
𝑤 = −1; in Cauchy’s classification 𝑑 → ∞ is the linear limit of the power law, not a boundary with the
exponential family. On this paper’s reading, it is the dividing line between sustainable complexity and
runaway growth.

15.4   The dimensional ladder
On this paper’s reading, the history of the universe is a trajectory through the phase diagram, and that
trajectory has a direction.
     On this paper’s reading, in the radiation era (the first 50,000 years), the universe is in Phase I with
𝑑 = 1. Information propagates along null geodesics, which are one-dimensional curves; the paper reads
this as 𝑑 = 1, a reading the identity of Section 9 does not test. The scaling exponent is 1/2. Expansion
decelerates.
     In the matter era (50,000 years to 7 billion years), on the same reading, the universe remains in Phase
I but shifts to 𝑑 = 2; Section 9 states what established cosmology says of when the cosmic web formed.
Gravity pulls matter into the cosmic web: filaments, walls and sheets that partition three-dimensional
space into voids. The effective network dimension of the universe has increased from 1 to 2. The scaling
exponent rises to 2/3.
     Within galaxies, gravitational collapse forms stars, planets, and eventually organisms. Organisms
build three-dimensional vascular networks. 𝑑 = 3, 𝛼 = 3/4. The effective network dimension has
increased again.
     The trajectory is: 𝑑 = 1, then 𝑑 = 2, then 𝑑 = 3.
     On this reading the radiation era is one-dimensional (null geodesics), the matter era two-dimensional
(the cosmic web) and biology three-dimensional (vascular networks). The numbers match because the
algebraic identity connects them, and the identity carries no evidence (Section 9).

15.5   The critical crossing
At 𝑤 = −1/3, the universe crosses the critical boundary: expansion switches from decelerating to
accelerating, and it approaches exponential growth as dark energy comes to dominate (exactly exponential
only for 𝑤 = −1). The composition operator changes from multiplicative (gravity clustering matter into
hierarchical networks) to additive (vacuum energy contributing uniformly from every point in space).
On the framework’s reading, the operator that dominates changes at this point; that is a reading, not a
thermodynamic phase transition.
     In the dark energy era, the universe is in Phase II. Growth is exponential. If dark energy stays
constant, the universe will expand forever, driving all structure apart, diluting all complexity, approaching
heat death.
     The universe moves through the ARC phase diagram as naturally as water moves through its solid-
liquid-gas phase diagram. The difference is that water can cycle between phases. Whether the acceleration
lasts depends on whether dark energy is constant, which is open: DESI’s 2025 data prefer an evolving
dark energy over a constant one at between 2.8 and 4.2 standard deviations, depending on the supernova
set (DESI Collaboration 2025).

15.6   Where intelligence sits
On this paper’s reading, intelligence occupies the critical boundary itself.
     A brain is a Phase I system physically: its blood supply branches through three-dimensional space.
But the computation it performs is not constrained by the geometry of the skull. On this reading, recursive
self-reference creates effective dimensions without needing further spatial dimensions, though each of
its steps is a physical operation with a physical cost (Section 11). On the same reading, each layer of
abstraction (thinking about thinking, modelling the modeller) adds an effective dimension to the cognitive
network without requiring additional spatial dimensions.
     At the critical boundary, 𝛼 = 1: linear scaling. Beyond it, 𝛼 > 1: superlinear scaling. On this reading,
intelligence would cross the boundary from Phase I to Phase II without the catastrophic instability that
Phase II normally implies.
     A chain reaction in Phase II destroys itself. An epidemic in Phase II burns through its hosts. On
this reading, intelligence in Phase II creates new knowledge, new dimensions, new spaces to explore. It
sustains superlinear growth not by consuming a fixed resource faster and faster, but by expanding the
resource base itself. Each breakthrough opens new questions. Each answer creates new problems. The
fuel is information, which grows with inquiry, within the finite cost of each physical step (Section 11).

The structural implication. On this paper’s reading, an artificial intelligence that improves itself
recursively would operate at or beyond the critical boundary, with 𝛼 > 1 for capability against recursive
depth, which Paper II has not observed (Section 12). On the same reading of the phase diagram,
intelligence is not just another scaling phenomenon. It is the phase transition itself.
    The universe spent about 7 billion years in Phase I, building networks of increasing dimension, on
a second reading of this paper that differs from that of Table 15: hydrogen clouds (𝑑 ≈ 0), filaments
(𝑑 = 1), cosmic web (𝑑 = 2), galaxies and stars and planets with three-dimensional chemistry. Then, on
one planet, the networks became recursive. On this reading, the effective dimension of their computation
was no longer set by the geometry of a body, and alpha would cross 1 there, which no measurement has
yet shown (Section 12). A system then existed that could understand the phase diagram it was on.

17     Limitations
     • Scope of the classification. Cauchy’s classification covers homomorphisms between the additive
       and multiplicative structures under regularity. Most systems are not such homomorphisms, and
       saturation, critical exponents, curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers lie outside the
       grid (Section 4).
     • No proof of sufficiency. No formal proof yet shows that multiplicative composition, space filling
       and conservation suffice for 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1); the convergence of the earlier derivations could reflect
       shared assumptions (Section 2.3).
     • The biological comparison. The fungal values are three colony-level scaling factors from small
       data sets over narrow ranges, one of them against colony extension rate rather than biomass, which
       their authors present as hypothesis generators; definitive testing needs respirometry on isolated
       hyphae (Prediction 1). No organism with a genuinely two-dimensional hierarchical transport
       network has been identified. The three-group comparison of Section 12 was computed with a
       two-dimensional group that the note on 𝑑 = 2 sets aside, and the aggregate error that Section 6
       printed until this version, which counted the two cosmology identities, is withdrawn (Appendix A).
       The four biological values of Table 2 are each their source’s own, read at that source; of the seven
       group exponents behind the three-dimensional mean of Table 10, three (fish, amphibians and
       crustaceans) are not yet confirmed at source in this paper, and published group exponents depend
       on the data set and the method (Section 12).
     • The physics rows. Two of the four rows are analytic identities, which carry no evidence, and the
       two fragmentation rows have no sourced values yet and await the row-by-row provenance audit that
       Section 6.1 names.
     • The bound. No written rule yet decides, before an exponent is fitted, which systems are governed by
       multiplicative composition through a finite-dimensional hierarchical network, so a counterexample
       could be reclassified after the fact (Prediction 4).
     • The cosmology. The mapping 𝑑 = 2/(1 + 3𝑤) is an identity; the causal reading of Section 9 is this
       paper’s interpretation, and it conflicts with established cosmology on when and how the cosmic
       web formed (Cautun et al. 2014).
     • The AI-side results. The capability fit is one model’s and inconclusive; the alignment tiers come
       from one unregistered run under the October 2026 code-review caveat; the Eden Protocol pilot was
       scored without blinding (Section 12).
     • Novelty. The paper’s novelty statements were made without a dated literature search (Appendix E).


18     Conclusion
Under stated conditions, how a recursive process combines its steps fixes the family its growth can take,
among four. Within the power-law family, the exponent 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) that earlier work derived for hierarchical
networks agrees with published values where such a network partitions space, and this paper offers an
account of why those derivations converge. Where the same form appears in cosmology it is an identity.
The exponent of capability against recursive depth is a different quantity, which this geometry does not fix
and which has to be measured; the registered programme exists to measure it.

    Kleiber found that the basal metabolism of thirteen groups of animals, from ring doves to steers, is
