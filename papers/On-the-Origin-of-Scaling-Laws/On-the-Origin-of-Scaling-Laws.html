Abstract

If the way a process combines its steps fixes the family of forms its growth can take, then the family a self-improving system’s capability will follow as its recursion deepens can be read from how it composes, before it scales and while correction can still be built into it. This paper asks why scaling laws in recursive composition keep taking the same few mathematical forms. Where a process has a well defined composition operator on its input and output and meets the regularity conditions that Cauchy’s functional equations require, the admissible forms fall into four families: linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic. This is a conditional classification, not a survey of nature, and saturation lies outside it. Within the power-law family, the exponent α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1) for a dd-dimensional hierarchical network is earlier work’s; this paper gives an account of why those derivations, from different assumptions, converge, and a proof that its three conditions suffice does not yet exist. The relation is read as a network-partition identity with a stated domain: published exponents for filamentous fungi (d=1d = 1) do not reject it (a mean of three factors on different axes; no equivalence shown), those for vertebrates and invertebrates with three-dimensional transport (d=3d = 3) agree with it on the group means compiled here, though the mammalian exponent is debated, and no organism with a two-dimensional hierarchical network has been identified. Under d=2/(1+3w)d = 2/(1+3w) the Friedmann solutions take the same form, an identity that carries no evidence. For finite dd the exponent stays below one, a bound on systems of that class only; how capability grows with recursive depth is a different pair of variables, which this geometry does not fix and which has to be measured. Five predictions are stated; one, on neural scaling exponents, is withdrawn because published measurements refute it.

1 Introduction

If the way a process combines its steps fixes the family of forms its growth can take, then how a self-improving system composes tells us, before it scales, which family its growth with recursive depth can follow; its exponent still has to be measured (Section 11). That matters for artificial intelligence. Whether correction can keep pace with a system that improves itself depends on how fast its capability compounds, and correction is best built into such a system before it scales, while people still hold oversight of it. This paper traces the forms behind Law I, the ARC Principle, a law under test, to their mathematical origin, in the systems where scaling laws were first measured: organisms, materials and the expanding universe.

A mouse’s heart beats about 600 times per minute. An elephant’s beats about 28 (Merck Veterinary Manual 2004). A blue whale’s, on a foraging dive, beats 4 to 8 (Goldbogen et al. 2019).

If you plot heart rate against body mass across mammals, the points fall close to a straight line on logarithmic axes, with a slope close to −14-\tfrac14, from the mouse to the whale (Stahl 1967; Noujaim et al. 2004).

Why?

For filamentous fungi, published colony-level measurements put the exponent of energy use against colony size near one half, and this paper predicts exactly 12\tfrac{1}{2} for a single thread (Section 14). Not 34\tfrac{3}{4}, like a mammal. Not 23\tfrac{2}{3}, like the matter-dominated universe. A different fraction entirely.

Why 12\tfrac{1}{2} for fungi, but 34\tfrac{3}{4} for mammals? And why do these same fractions appear in cosmology?

Now consider something entirely different. The universe is expanding. During the matter-dominated era, the scale factor grew as time raised to the power 23\tfrac{2}{3}. During the radiation era, it grew as time to the power 12\tfrac{1}{2}.

Why 23\tfrac{2}{3}? Why 12\tfrac{1}{2}? And why are these the same fractions that appear in biology?

Now consider one more thing. When a rock shatters, the distribution of fragment sizes follows a power law; Section 6.1 gives the status of its exponents.

This paper argues that these questions share one mathematical answer, under conditions it states, and grades where that answer is evidence and where it is only an identity.

The answer has two parts, and the paper grades each. The first is mathematical: where a process has a composition operator and meets regularity conditions, the way its steps combine fixes the family its growth can take, among four (Section 4). The second is the exponent: within the power-law family, the formula α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1) for a dd-dimensional hierarchical network, which earlier work derived (Section 2), gives 12\tfrac12, 23\tfrac23 and 34\tfrac34, and this paper offers an account of why derivations from different assumptions arrive at it. Where the fractions reappear in cosmology the agreement is an identity under a change of variables, and it carries no evidence (Section 9).

Our contributions are:

  • An account of convergence. Cauchy’s multiplicative functional equation, with space filling and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow, gives the power-law family and the exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1); this is offered as the reason why the earlier derivations, from different assumptions, converge on a formula that is theirs (Section 2). A proof that the three conditions suffice does not yet exist.

  • The classification applied to scaling forms. The four Cauchy families, linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic, set out as the homomorphisms between the additive and multiplicative structures under regularity, with saturation placed outside them and the variable pair named at each site (Sections 3 to 5).

  • The relation as a network-partition identity. Its domain and its failures stated, and its comparison with published exponents in biology and physics graded row by row (Sections 6 and 12).

  • The geometric bound, scoped. The exponent stays below one for every finite dd in the class of systems whose growth is carried through a space-filling hierarchical network, with the counterexamples outside that class conceded (Section 10), and the bound distinguished from the exponent of capability against recursive depth (Section 11).

  • Predictions and their status. Five predictions, one of them withdrawn because published measurements refute it (Section 14).

Sections 3 to 5 set out the patterns, Cauchy’s classification and the principle; Section 6 the formula and its domain; Section 7 the heart-rate chain; Section 8 the fission chain reaction, which the grid classifies and does not explain; Section 9 the cosmological identity; Section 10 the bound; and Section 11 the distinction between physical and recursive scaling. 2 Related work and what this paper adds

The mathematics of this paper is two hundred years old. The biological data is ninety years old. The cosmological solution is a century old. It is reasonable to ask: what, precisely, is the contribution?

2.1 Earlier work

The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) formula itself is not new. It is derived, in its general form or as a special case, in earlier work, and the first entry below gives each work what it states: West, Brown and Enquist (1997) from fractal branching networks, Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) and Banavar and co-authors (2002) from geometric constraints on transportation networks, He & Zhang (2004) from fractal cell geometry, Demetrius ((2003), (2006)) and Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010) from quantum oscillator coupling in the Debye model, Bettencourt (2013) from urban scaling theory, as a special case, Zhao (2022) from producer-consumer dynamics, and Maino et al. (2014) from Dynamic Energy Budget theory. Each derivation starts from different assumptions and arrives at the same functional form. This convergence is itself remarkable and requires explanation.

2.2 What this paper adds

The contribution is the unification. One formula, α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1), connects fungi, mammals, surface physics and, as an identity, the expansion of the universe, with earthquake energy as a variable-pair case study and rock fracture awaiting sourced values (Section 6.1). One equation with no fitted parameter: dd is set by the network’s dimension. The claim is not that this formula is original, but that Cauchy’s functional equations, combined with the space-filling and conservation conditions, explain why the earlier derivations, from different starting assumptions, arrive at the same form. That invariance under rescaling forces a power law, through the multiplicative functional equation, is a standard result (Newman 2005, sect. III.E); what this paper adds is the account of why the derivations of d/(d+1)d/(d+1) meet the space-filling and conservation conditions that select the exponent within that family. The derivation of West et al. (1997) has itself been questioned (Dodds et al. 2001; Kozłowski and Konarzewski 2004), and its three-quarter exponent shown to hold only in the limit of infinite network size (Savage et al. 2008); Section 2.3 states what follows if the convergence reflects shared assumptions.

A formal proof that those conditions are sufficient does not yet exist (see the strongest objection, below). Set against the earlier work, the paper’s claims are that it has:

  1. Given an account of why derivations from different assumptions converge on one formula. That account is what this paper adds; the formula, and the values 12\tfrac{1}{2}, 23\tfrac{2}{3} and 34\tfrac{3}{4} that it gives, are the earlier work’s. West, Brown and Enquist (1997) derive the three-quarter power law for metabolic rates from a model of transport through space-filling fractal networks of branching tubes, a landmark result, and note that, because the network must fill the entire DD-dimensional volume, the result generalises to D/(D+1)D/(D+1); West, Brown and Enquist (1999) obtain D/(D+1)D/(D+1) in DD dimensions again, from a more general model based on the geometry rather than the hydrodynamics of hierarchical networks. Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) derive a general relationship between size and flow rates in arbitrary networks with local connectivity, and find that in the most efficient class of networks in DD dimensions metabolic rate scales as mass to the power D/(D+1)D/(D+1), which gives quarter-power scaling for D=3D = 3; Banavar et al. (2010) show, in two models of resource distribution networks, that the exponent of metabolic rate on body mass is 23\tfrac{2}{3} if the velocity of flow remains constant and can attain a maximum of 34\tfrac{3}{4} if the velocity scales with its maximum exponent, 1/12. Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010) derive the exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1) for the scaling of metabolic rate with body size in the regime they call quantum, where dd is the dimension of the physical space in which their network of coupled energy-transducing oscillators is embedded, and state its values 12\tfrac{1}{2}, 23\tfrac{2}{3} and 34\tfrac{3}{4} for d=1d = 1, 2 and 3; in the regime they call classical the scaling is isometric. Bettencourt (2013) sets out a theoretical framework that predicts the average social, spatial and infrastructural properties of cities as a set of scaling relations. In it the land area of a city scales as its population to the power D/(D+H)D/(D+H) in DD dimensions, where HH is the fractal dimension of travel paths; the exponent is 23\tfrac{2}{3} for D=2D = 2 and H=1H = 1, and it has the form D/(D+1)D/(D+1) only when H=1H = 1. Zhao (2022) derives the n/(n+1)n/(n+1) power scaling law for an nn-dimensional system from producer-consumer dynamics. He and Zhang (2004) derive a 4/5 law for the human brain, the value d/(d+1)d/(d+1) takes at d=4d = 4, from the fractal dimension of a brain cell’s contour. Maino et al. (2014) derive from dynamic energy budget theory an equation for metabolic rate against mass, the same equation as the finite-size form of West, Brown and Enquist’s model, which converges on the three-quarter power only in the limit of large mass; they stress that under neither framework is metabolic rate a power function of mass. The surface area hypothesis predicts 23\tfrac{2}{3} but not 34\tfrac{3}{4} or 12\tfrac{1}{2}. The formula α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1) predicts all three from a single equation with one input, the dimension of the hierarchical transport network. The d=1d = 1 value is not rejected by published fungal data (a mean of three factors on different axes; no equivalence shown) and the d=3d = 3 value agrees with published group means; the d=2d = 2 value appears in cosmology as an identity, which carries no evidence (Section 9), and in physics in the rows whose grades Section 6.1 gives; in biology it is untested (Section 12).

  2. Connected biological scaling to physics scaling. The same formula gives Kleiber (1932)’s 34\tfrac{3}{4} law (biology), KPZ roughness at 12\tfrac{1}{2} (surface physics), the specific-heat analogue of two-dimensional percolation at 23\tfrac{2}{3} (statistical mechanics) and, once their values are sourced, fragmentation in 2D and 3D (materials science). Earlier work already takes the form beyond organisms: Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) verify the scaling relation that their theorem predicts on observational data from river drainage basins, where D=2D = 2, and say that their results should apply to distributed networks that carry water, blood, sewage, food, air and electrical currents; Zhao (2022) places biomass production, the growth of eukaryotes, mammalian brain size and the distribution of public facilities in cities in one universality class. The physics cases named here are what this paper adds.

  3. Set out the geometric scaling bound. That the exponent stays below 1 is in the earlier work. Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) state as a theorem that, in any spanning network in DD dimensions, the rate at which nutrients are delivered scales at most as the D/(D+1)D/(D+1) power of the volume of blood in the network, so that metabolic rate does not scale linearly with mass; Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010) note that their exponent tends to one as dd increases; and Zhao (2022) states that the scaling is sub-linear. Nor do the earlier explanations all rest on dissipation: West, Brown and Enquist (1997) assume that the energy dissipated is minimised, Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) do not, and West, Brown and Enquist (1999) work from geometry rather than hydrodynamics. This paper sets the bound out for every finite dd and reads it as geometric (Section 10): a network embedded in finite-dimensional space cannot produce α≥1\alpha \geq 1.

  4. Connected scaling laws to the expansion of the universe. The mapping d=2/(1+3w)d = 2/(1+3w) writes the Friedmann solutions in the form d/(d+1)d/(d+1). The cosmic boundary (w=−1/3w = -1/3), where expansion turns from decelerating to accelerating, maps to d→∞d \to \infty, where α→1\alpha \to 1, under that change of variables, so the coincidence is a property of the map and not a second derivation (Section 9).

  5. Defined the domain of applicability. The formula applies where hierarchical networks partition space. It fails for nearest-neighbour interactions, random walks, and gravitational clustering. The earlier works state limits of their own: West, Brown and Enquist (1997) predict small deviations from quarter-power scaling in the smallest mammals; West, Brown and Enquist (1999) do not expect their relationships to apply to filamentous algae and fungi, and filamentous fungi are this paper’s d=1d = 1 case (Section 14); Banavar et al. (2010) expect deviations towards 23\tfrac{2}{3} where the velocity of flow does not vary significantly with body mass and towards linear scaling where there is no vascular system; Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010), as the first entry says, keep d/(d+1)d/(d+1) to the regime they call quantum; and Zhao (2022) places linear and super-linear scalings outside his class. The three failures named here are the ones this paper states.

  6. Connected physical scaling to cognitive scaling. The same framework that constrains physical exponents below 1 allows cognitive exponents above 1. Exponents above 1 are in the earlier work too: in Bettencourt (2013) the socioeconomic rates of a city scale with its population to a power above 1, which is 7/6 for D=2D = 2 and H=1H = 1, in the framework that gives its land area a power below 1; and Zhao (2022) attributes the super-linear scalings he places outside his class to resources that flow more efficiently in larger systems. Neither is about cognition; the connection to recursive self-reference is the one this paper makes (Section 11).

  7. Shown that the admissible forms are finitely many. Under the stated conditions the admissible functional forms are finitely many, and the family they generate is finite-dimensional: a discrete form label and two continuous parameters, a coefficient and an exponent or rate. The step from this classification to all possible classical physical laws would require every physical law to be such a homomorphism, which this paper does not establish (Section 15).

  8. Identified the dimensional ladder. On this paper’s reading, the universe’s history is a monotonic increase in effective network dimension: d=1d = 1 (radiation era) to d=2d = 2 (matter era) to d=3d = 3 (biology). Each step produces a higher scaling exponent and more efficient composition. The trajectory through composition space has a direction.

  9. Constructed the phase diagram of complexity. Three phases (sublinear, exponential, saturation) with a critical boundary at d→∞d \to \infty. On the framework’s reading, the operator that dominates changes at the transition from matter domination to dark energy domination; that is a reading, not a thermodynamic phase transition.

This paper identifies the underlying principle: under the stated conditions the composition operator determines the scaling form, and the dimension of the network, as the earlier derivations found, determines the exponent. The analogy with Einstein’s work that earlier versions drew here, with its concession, is kept in Appendix E.

2.3 The strongest objection

One could object that the agreement of the earlier derivations on d/(d+1)d/(d+1) reflects shared implicit assumptions rather than independent physical confirmation. All models assume resource distribution across dd dimensions with some form of linear conservation, which may inevitably yield d/(d+1)d/(d+1) by dimensional analysis. The ‘independence’ of the derivations may be illusory.

We acknowledge this possibility. If the convergence reflects shared mathematical structure rather than independent physical mechanisms, then the Cauchy framework would explain why that structure produces the specific form it does, if multiplicative composition with conservation in dd-dimensional space admits no alternative to d/(d+1)d/(d+1); whether it does is the open problem stated below. The deeper question is whether these three conditions (multiplicative composition, space filling and conservation) are themselves inevitable features of any system that distributes resources hierarchically. If so, d/(d+1)d/(d+1) is a mathematical necessity for all resource distribution systems, not a contingent property of particular models. Answering this question requires a formal proof of the sufficiency of these three conditions independent of any specific physical mechanism. That proof does not yet exist. It is the most important open problem in this programme.

3 Three ways things grow

Many growth processes show one of three named patterns. Where a growth process has a well defined composition operator on its input and its output, and meets the regularity conditions stated in Section 4, the first two are among the admissible forms; the third, saturation, comes from bounded dynamics and lies outside that classification. The classification is of admissible functions under those conditions, not a survey of nature:

Definition 1 (Pattern 1: the power law). Big things grow proportionally slower. A city ten times larger does not need ten times more petrol stations: it needs about six times more (Bettencourt et al. 2007). A whale ten times heavier does not need ten times more energy: it needs about five and a half times more. Each doubling of size gives less than a doubling of the output. y=a⋅xb,where b<1\begin{equation*} y = a \cdot x^b, \quad \text{where } b < 1 \end{equation*}(1)

Definition 2 (Pattern 2: the exponential). Each step multiplies the previous. One infected person infects three, who each infect three more, giving nine, then twenty-seven. One fissioning uranium atom releases neutrons that split two more atoms, which split four, then eight, then sixteen. After eighty generations, about one microsecond, a single atom has become a trillion trillion atoms. y=a⋅ebx\begin{equation*} y = a \cdot e^{bx} \end{equation*}(2)

Definition 3 (Pattern 3: the saturation curve). Growth hits a ceiling. A room can only hold so many people. An enzyme can only process so many molecules per second. Adding more drug has diminishing effect once the receptors are saturated. y=L⋅x/(K+x)\begin{equation*} y = L \cdot x \,/\, (K + x) \end{equation*}(3)

These three patterns appear in biology, physics, chemistry, economics, computer science, linguistics, seismology and cosmology. Many measured scaling laws take one of these forms; most systems lie outside the grid, as the scope correction of 22 August 2026 states, and Section 4 sets out the grid’s four cells and places saturation outside them.

The question is: what determines which pattern a system follows?

4 The answer

The French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the answer in (1821), though he did not know he was answering this question.

Cauchy asked: what mathematical functions satisfy the property that combining inputs is the same as combining outputs?

Theorem 1 (Cauchy 1821). Under regularity (continuity, or measurability, or boundedness on an interval), a function on the positive reals that carries one of the two structures (addition, multiplication) into one of the two structures falls into exactly four families, one per cell of the two-by-two grid:

If inputs add and outputs add: f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)\begin{equation*} f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y) \end{equation*}(4) The only regular solution is linear: f(x)=cxf(x) = cx.

If inputs multiply and outputs multiply: f(x⋅y)=f(x)⋅f(y)\begin{equation*} f(x \cdot y) = f(x) \cdot f(y) \end{equation*}(5) The only regular solution is a power law: f(x)=xcf(x) = x^c.

If inputs add and outputs multiply: f(x+y)=f(x)⋅f(y)\begin{equation*} f(x + y) = f(x) \cdot f(y) \end{equation*}(6) The only regular solution is an exponential: f(x)=ecxf(x) = e^{cx}.

If inputs multiply and outputs add: f(x⋅y)=f(x)+f(y)\begin{equation*} f(x \cdot y) = f(x) + f(y) \end{equation*}(7) The only regular solution is logarithmic: f(x)=clog⁡xf(x) = c\log x.

This exhausts the homomorphisms between the additive and multiplicative structures on the positive reals. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure: saturating behaviour, critical exponents, curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers all lie outside the grid, and the grid says nothing about them.

This is not a theory or a model. It is a mathematical theorem. It has been proven for two hundred years: Cauchy proved it for continuous functions, and the forms under measurability or boundedness on an interval are later results.

4.1 Why the grid has four cells and no fifth

This can be proven with nothing more than multiplication.

Imagine a machine that takes in any number and gives back another number. Call it ff. Suppose this machine has one special property: if you multiply two inputs and feed the result through the machine, you get the same answer as feeding each input through separately and multiplying the outputs.

In symbols: f(2×3)=f(2)×f(3)f(2 \times 3) = f(2) \times f(3).

Now watch what happens. Since 4=2×2:f(4)=f(2)×f(2)=f(2)2.Since 8=2×2×2:f(8)=f(2)3.Since 16=24:f(16)=f(2)4.\begin{align*} \text{Since } 4 = 2 \times 2\text{:}&\quad f(4) = f(2) \times f(2) = f(2)^2.\\ \text{Since } 8 = 2 \times 2 \times 2\text{:}&\quad f(8) = f(2)^3.\\ \text{Since } 16 = 2^4\text{:}&\quad f(16) = f(2)^4. \end{align*}

The pattern is clear: f(2n)=f(2)nf(2^n) = f(2)^n.

Let f(2)=Af(2) = A. Then f(2n)=Anf(2^n) = A^n. Since 2n=x2^n = x means n=log⁡2(x)n = \log_2(x), we get f(x)=Alog⁡2x=xlog⁡2Af(x) = A^{\log_2 x} = x^{\log_2 A}.

That is a power law: f(x)=xcf(x) = x^c, where c=log⁡2Ac = \log_2 A. This shows the power law at the powers of two; for every positive real the same follows from rational powers and the regularity condition of Theorem 1, which is where that condition does its work.

We did not choose a power law. We did not want a power law. We merely said “multiplying inputs multiplies outputs”, and the only regular function that can do this is a power law. Try any other function (a polynomial, a logarithm, a sine wave) and the rule breaks immediately.

The same logic applies to the second equation. If adding inputs multiplies outputs, f(x+y)=f(x)×f(y)f(x+y)=f(x) \times f(y), then: f(2)=f(1+1)=f(1)×f(1)=f(1)2.f(3)=f(2+1)=f(2)×f(1)=f(1)3.f(n)=f(1)n.\begin{align*} f(2) &= f(1+1) = f(1) \times f(1) = f(1)^2.\\ f(3) &= f(2+1) = f(2) \times f(1) = f(1)^3.\\ f(n) &= f(1)^n. \end{align*}

That is an exponential. The logic forces it.

Saturation is a different matter, and Theorem 1 does not cover it: if growth has a physical ceiling (a glass can only hold so much water; an enzyme has a finite number of binding sites), the system must approach that ceiling and flatten. A saturation curve such as Equation (3) is one smooth way to do so; it comes from bounded dynamics, not from a Cauchy equation.

The consequence.

Under regularity, a function that carries one of the two structures on the positive reals into one of the two structures falls into exactly four families: linear, power law, exponential, and logarithmic. There is no fifth cell, because there is no third structure and no third slot.

This is a completeness statement about homomorphisms. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure. Saturating behaviour, critical exponents, curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers all lie outside the grid, and the grid says nothing about them. Saturation, in particular, arises from bounded dynamics (finite carrying capacity, mass action, receptor occupancy, depletion): it is not a Cauchy family, and any presentation of it as one is a category error. The earlier framing of this result as a physics-style no-go theorem, comparable to Bell’s theorem (Bell 1964) or Weinberg-Witten (Weinberg and Witten 1980), overstated its reach and has been withdrawn: Bell and Weinberg-Witten constrain what can physically exist, whereas this grid enumerates ways of writing one identity in four charts, and that is a category difference.

Where the physics does select a canonical composition variable (time and sequential steps composing additively; scale ratios composing multiplicatively), the grid picks a family. Where no canonical chart exists, the label carries no physical information, and the family assignment must state its variable pair rather than infer one from the substrate.

Assumption 3: Hyers-Ulam stability.

How often systems in nature satisfy one of these equations in the first place is a separate empirical question that stability cannot settle.

5 The principle

Many growing systems amplify themselves recursively: each output becomes input for the next step. Under the stated composition and regularity conditions, the ways the steps can combine give the first two forms below; the linear and logarithmic cells of the grid are set out in Section 4, and the third case below, a capped process, lies outside the grid:

  • If each step multiplies the input by a fraction (like blood vessels branching into smaller vessels, each carrying a fraction of the flow), the result is a power law.

  • If each step adds independently (like radioactive atoms decaying one by one, or neutrons splitting atoms that release more neutrons), the result is an exponential.

  • If growth is capped by a physical limit (like enzyme binding sites filling up), the result is a saturation curve, which comes from bounded dynamics rather than from a Cauchy equation.

The ARC Principle.

The ARC Principle is Law I of the ARC Theory, a law under test (Section 16): under the stated composition and regularity conditions, the composition operator, how the steps of recursive amplification combine, determines the functional form of the resulting scaling law. Multiplicative composition produces power laws. Additive composition produces exponentials. Bounded composition, which lies outside the Cauchy grid, produces saturation curves. The grid itself has four cells, the fourth logarithmic, and it does not exhaust scaling laws (Section 4).

The Friedmann-ARC mapping is an algebraic identity. It does not mean that the universe is a biological organism, or that biology causes the universe to expand, or that cosmology ‘explains’ metabolism. It means that the mathematical structure governing optimal network partition in dd dimensions is the same mathematical structure that appears in the Friedmann equation when ww is related to dimensionality by d=2/(1+3w)d = 2/(1+3w). The physical mechanisms are entirely different. The mathematics is the same. The claim is structural, not causal: two apparently unrelated phenomena, biological metabolic scaling and cosmic expansion, are written in the same mathematical form under the change of variables, which is all the identity shows.

The geometric bound.

For a system whose growth is carried through a space-filling hierarchical network of finite dimension, the exponent stays below one, because d/(d+1)d/(d+1) is below one for every finite dd (the earlier derivations’ result). The constraint is geometric, not thermodynamic. No amount of engineering, no level of technology, can overcome it. You can make a network more efficient, but you cannot make three-dimensional space have four dimensions. The geometric scaling bound is the shape of space itself.

That d/(d+1)<1d/(d+1) < 1 for every finite dd is a mathematical necessity, not an empirical observation, and it follows directly from the formula; whether a given system belongs to the class is an empirical question, which no written rule yet decides (Section 17).

11 The escape

A brain is physical: its blood vessels branch through three-dimensional space, paying the geometric tax that every network of that class pays.

The computation a brain or a machine performs is physical too, and obeys every physical bound. What differs is the variable. The bound d/(d+1)d/(d+1) concerns how metabolic rate grows with the mass of a body supplied through a space-filling network; the ARC Principle concerns how capability grows with recursive depth. Nothing in the geometry of the network fixes the second exponent, which is why it has to be measured, and why a value above one would contradict no physical law.

Each layer of recursive self-reference creates, in effect, a new dimension. The ‘network’ of recursive thought is not constrained by the skull’s geometry. The skull constrains the hardware. It does not constrain the software.

11.1 Quantum error correction: three regimes classified

Quantum computing provides a striking illustration of ARC composition operators working within a single technology.

A quantum computer’s raw error rate is a physical process: each qubit decoheres through interaction with its environment. Independent errors at a fixed rate per operation make an error-free run exponentially unlikely as circuits deepen. Left uncorrected, this is Pattern 2, and it makes useful quantum computation impossible beyond roughly the inverse of the error rate per operation.

Quantum error correction changes the composition operator. It takes the output of each layer of computation and feeds it back through a recursive correction cycle: measure syndromes, identify errors, apply corrections, and feed the corrected state forward as input to the next cycle. This is recursive amplification with bounded composition: below threshold, each correction cycle holds the logical error per cycle at a fixed level for a given code distance, instead of letting it compound with depth. On this paper’s reading that is Pattern 3, saturation, which lies outside the Cauchy grid.

Below threshold, the surface code suppresses the logical error rate exponentially in code distance, by a factor Λ=2.14±0.02\Lambda = 2.14 \pm 0.02 for each increase of two (Google Quantum AI and Collaborators 2025); the preprint was public from 24 August 2024 and the article appeared in Nature on 9 December 2024. Code distance adds and the logical error multiplies, so this regime sits in the grid’s exponential cell.

12.3 Derivations from different physics, one form

The most striking feature of the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) formula is that it has been derived from radically different physics. West, Brown and Enquist (1997, 1999) derived it from the geometry of hierarchical, space-filling fractal vascular networks. Separately, Demetrius (2003, 2006) and Demetrius & Tuszynski (2010) derived the same formula from quantum statistical mechanics, applying the Debye model of thermal properties to coupled energy-transducing oscillator networks embedded in dd-dimensional space. Different physics. Different starting assumptions. The same equation. The same predictions: d=1d = 1 gives 12\tfrac{1}{2}, d=2d = 2 gives 23\tfrac{2}{3}, d=3d = 3 gives 34\tfrac{3}{4}, and d→∞d \to \infty gives 1.

The field has treated these as competing theories (reviewed in Agutter & Tuszynski (2011)). This paper’s reading is that they are not competing but complementary: both are physical instantiations of the same set of mathematical constraints. Cauchy’s multiplicative functional equation (1821) constrains any system with continuous multiplicative composition to the power-law family. The dd-dimensional space-filling condition, combined with a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow, then constrains the exponent within that family to d/(d+1)d/(d+1). West’s branching networks satisfy multiplicative composition (branching ratios), space-filling, and energy minimisation. Demetrius’s oscillator chains satisfy multiplicative composition (Debye functions), dd-dimensional embedding, and steady-state energy balance. Both satisfy the three conditions. If those conditions suffice, both must arrive at the same formula and the convergence is not coincidental; whether they suffice is the open problem of Section 2.3.

To our knowledge, this explanation for why the two derivations converge has not been previously published. The observation that Cauchy’s theorem, combined with space-filling and conservation, provides the meta-constraint underlying both programmes appears to be novel; no dated literature search has been recorded for it (Appendix E).

The three values.

The earlier derivations already give 12\tfrac{1}{2}, 23\tfrac{2}{3} and 34\tfrac{3}{4} from the same formula (Section 2). Three numbers, for one-, two- and three-dimensional transport networks. One equation with no fitted parameter: dd is set by the network’s dimension. The values at d=1d = 1 (filamentous fungi) and d=3d = 3 (vertebrates and invertebrates with three-dimensional transport) are compared with published measurements in Level 2a; the value at d=2d = 2 is predicted and untested in biology. The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) formula is derived, in its general form or as a special case, in the earlier work that Section 2.1 lists, each given what it states. The contribution of this paper is not the formula itself but the unifying Cauchy framework that explains why these derivations converge on the same form: Cauchy’s multiplicative functional equation constrains continuous multiplicative composition to the power-law family, and the space-filling condition combined with a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow would select the exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1) within that family. If those conditions suffice, the convergence is compelled; whether they do is open (Section 2.3).

Note on the d=2d = 2 biological prediction.

The note on d=2d = 2 in Section 6 applies here: no known organism has a genuinely two-dimensional hierarchical transport network, so the d=2d = 2 value is untested in biology. Published data show near-isometric scaling for jellyfish and colonial bryozoans (Purcell 2009; Hartikainen et al. 2014; Glazier 2006), not 2/3; planarians show Kleiber’s three-quarter scaling, which Thommen et al. (2019) trace to body-size-dependent energy storage (Section 6).

Until this version, this paper predicted that the neural scaling exponent (of loss against parameters) would equal d/(d+1)d/(d+1), with dd the intrinsic dimension of the training data’s manifold. Once dd reaches 1, that fraction is never less than one half, and it climbs towards 1 as dd grows. The exponents measured for language models lie far below it. For language models trained to convergence on large datasets, Kaplan et al. (2020) report about 0.076 (their equation 1.1). For the parameter term of the loss, with its irreducible part set apart, Hoffmann et al. (2022) report 0.34 (their equation 10). They note that both of their exponents are lower than one half. The intrinsic dimensions estimated for language and image data run from about 7 to more than 90 (Sharma and Kaplan 2020; Pope et al. 2021).

The dependence runs the wrong way too. In a transport network a higher dimension lifts the exponent towards 1. In neural networks the measurements run the other way. Where the dimension of the data is dialled in controlled teacher and student experiments, the measured exponent falls, close to 4/d4/d (Sharma and Kaplan 2020; Bahri et al. 2021), while d/(d+1)d/(d+1) rises. 15 The composition operator

The preceding sections have treated the composition operator (multiplicative, additive or bounded) as a property of individual systems. A whale has multiplicative composition. A chain reaction has additive composition. An enzyme has bounded composition. But there is a deeper reading.

On this paper’s reading, the composition operator is not a detail of specific systems but a descriptor of how a process combines its recursive steps; where a process composes in one of the two structures under regularity, the operator fixes the family its law can take (Theorem 1).

Consider the hierarchy:

  1. The composition operator determines the functional form (Cauchy, (1821)).

  2. The functional form constrains the scaling exponent (α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1)).

  3. The scaling exponent governs observable quantities (heart rates, expansion rates, error rates).

  4. The specific laws of physics (Newton’s gravity, Maxwell’s electrodynamics, Friedmann’s cosmology) can be written in these forms for particular physical systems.

The meta-law.

On this paper’s reading, the laws of physics can be written in the forms that the composition operator allows; they do not choose it. Where a law composes in one of the two structures under regularity, the operator is a mathematical structure that sits above the specific equations of a particular physical theory and fixes the form that law can take.

15.1 A completeness statement for composition homomorphisms

Now consider what this means for physics as a whole. Strip away the specifics of any physical law, and ask: what is a physical law? Many physical laws can be stated in the form ‘when you combine X and Y in manner Z, you get W.’ Force is mass combined with acceleration. Energy is mass combined with the square of the speed of light. Entropy is microstates combined by counting. Many physical laws can be read as composition rules.

Cauchy’s classification, under its regularity conditions, gives every homomorphism between the additive and multiplicative structures one of four forms: linear, power law, exponential or logarithmic. It does not exhaust scaling laws, and saturation, which arises from bounded dynamics, lies outside the grid (Section 4).

The consequence for physics.

Under the stated conditions, the admissible functional forms are finitely many, and the family they generate is finite-dimensional. Its members carry a discrete form label (linear, power law, exponential or logarithmic) and two continuous parameters, a coefficient and an exponent or rate. What is provable, and provable without data, is this classification of homomorphisms under regularity (Cauchy 1821); the step from it to all possible classical physical laws would require every physical law to be such a homomorphism, which this paper does not establish.

15.2 Three results combined

Three results, from three different centuries, combine into a single structural insight:

  1. Cauchy (1821): Each of the four functional equations has a unique continuous solution family. Bounded growth is a separate, capped case rather than a solution of those equations.

  2. Hyers (1941), on a question of Ulam (1960): Approximate solutions lie near exact solutions of the same equation (for the multiplicative and exponential equations, with the approximation measured in relative terms; for the logarithmic equation, directly). The stability is per-equation; the earlier ‘attractors in function space’ phrasing is withdrawn.

  3. ARC (this paper): Given the three conditions of Section 2.3, the exponent within the power-law family is determined by network dimension: α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1). The formula is the earlier derivations’ (Section 2); what this paper adds is the account of why they arrive at it.

The consequence.

If the three conditions of Section 2.3 suffice, the exponent of a system in the network class is fixed by its network’s dimension, whatever selection does; selection acts on whether and how such a network is built. Under the three conditions named in Section 2.3, a three-dimensional network gives three quarters; measured values range from about 0.67 to 0.75, and the conditions are where the debate lies. Status: an invariant relation under test, conditional on the three sufficiency conditions that Section 2.3 identifies (multiplicative composition, space-filling, conservation), a formal proof of which does not yet exist and is named there as the most important open problem in the programme.

The distinction matters. ‘Why is Kleiber (1932)’s exponent 3/4?’ has two kinds of answer. The biological answer is: because three-dimensional fractal vascular networks optimise resource delivery (West, Brown, Enquist (1997)). The mathematical answer is that, given the three conditions, no other value is possible for multiplicative composition in three dimensions. The biological answer explains the mechanism. The mathematical answer explains why that mechanism produces that specific number. Given those conditions, the mechanism could not have produced any other.

This is the difference between a law of nature (describing what happens) and a meta-law (constraining what can happen). The speed of light constrains what velocities are possible. The composition operator constrains what scaling forms are possible.

15.3 The phase diagram of complexity

On this paper’s reading, a system with a composition operator and an effective dimension occupies a position on a phase diagram defined by the two. The diagram has three phases:

Definition 4 (Phase I: sublinear scaling). Multiplicative composition, finite dd, 0<α<10 < \alpha < 1. This is the domain of physical systems: organisms, ecosystems, city infrastructure networks (sublinear in population; Bettencourt et al. 2007), geological processes, cosmic structure. Every system in Phase I suffers diminishing returns. Bigger is better, but with decreasing marginal gains. A whale is more efficient per gram than a mouse, but the improvement slows as mass increases. Phase I is the domain of sustainable complexity. The scaling exponent tells you how severe the tax is: d=1d = 1 pays 50% (α=0.5\alpha = 0.5), d=2d = 2 pays 33% (α=0.667\alpha = 0.667), d=3d = 3 pays 25% (α=0.75\alpha = 0.75). Higher-dimensional networks are more efficient, but the tax never reaches zero.

Definition 5 (Phase II: exponential growth). Additive composition, outside the network class (under the cosmological mapping the exponential, at w=−1w = -1, sits at d=−1d = -1). This is the domain of unconstrained amplification: nuclear chain reactions, viral epidemics, compound interest, dark energy. Phase II systems grow without diminishing returns: growth accelerates. But Phase II is inherently unsustainable in physical systems. Chain reactions exhaust their fuel. Epidemics run out of susceptible hosts. Bubbles burst. If dark energy stays constant, the universe’s dark energy phase is sustained Phase II behaviour, the only known example, and drives the universe toward heat death: maximum entropy, zero structure. Whether it stays constant is open (Section 15.5).

Definition 6 (Phase III: saturation). Bounded composition, finite ceiling. This is the domain of constrained systems: enzyme kinetics, logistic population growth, controlled nuclear reactors, market saturation. Phase III systems approach their ceiling and stop. Growth is self-limiting.

The critical boundary.

Phase I ends at the critical line d→∞d \to \infty, α→1\alpha \to 1, the limit of the network class; Phase II lies outside that class. This is the geometric scaling bound. In cosmology, it corresponds to w=−1/3w = -1/3, and the expansion past it is still a power law, accelerating, which becomes exponential only at w=−1w = -1; in Cauchy’s classification d→∞d \to \infty is the linear limit of the power law, not a boundary with the exponential family. On this paper’s reading, it is the dividing line between sustainable complexity and runaway growth.

(2012); it bounds the energy cost of an irreversible step from below and does not convert energy processed into information gained, so a link from II to energy needs a model of how many bits each cycle erases. The equation U=I×RαU = I \times R^{\alpha} is dimensionally consistent because RR is a dimensionless count.

The precedent.

Whether II becomes a permanent physical quantity depends on whether the scaling law U=I×RαU = I \times R^{\alpha} is confirmed across domains with independently measured values of II and RR. Entropy became permanent because the second law required it. Information became permanent because the channel capacity theorem required it. II becomes permanent if the ARC scaling law requires it. The quantity is justified by the law it obeys, not by the convenience of having a name for it.

16 What this paper does and does not show

What it shows.

As mathematics, under the stated regularity conditions, a function that carries one of the two structures on the positive reals into one of the two falls into one of four families (Theorem 1); that classification of homomorphisms does not exhaust scaling laws. The paper offers an account of why derivations of α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1) from different assumptions converge, the formula and its values being earlier work’s (Section 2), and it compares the formula with published exponents: not rejected at d=1d = 1 for filamentous fungi on colony-level data (a mean of three factors on different axes; no equivalence shown), in agreement at d=3d = 3, on the group means compiled here, for organisms with three-dimensional transport, untested at d=2d = 2 in biology for want of an organism, and set beside four physics rows, two of them analytic identities and two, of fragmentation, not yet sourced (Sections 6 and 12). For every finite dd the exponent stays below one in the class of systems whose growth is carried through a space-filling hierarchical network (Section 10).

What it does not show.

It does not show that every scaling law takes one of these forms, or that the three conditions suffice for d/(d+1)d/(d+1), which is the open problem of Section 2.3. The Friedmann mapping and the coincidence of the two boundaries are identities under a change of variables and carry no evidence (Section 9). It claims no exception to physics for intelligence: how capability grows with recursive depth is a different pair of variables, which the geometry does not fix and which has to be measured (Section 11). Its summaries of the programme’s AI experiments report one model’s inconclusive fit for capability against depth and one unregistered alignment run under the code-review caveat of Section 12.4, and its Eden Protocol pilot was scored without blinding. 17 Limitations

  • Scope of the classification. Cauchy’s classification covers homomorphisms between the additive and multiplicative structures under regularity. Most systems are not such homomorphisms, and saturation, critical exponents, curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers lie outside the grid (Section 4).

  • No proof of sufficiency. No formal proof yet shows that multiplicative composition, space filling and conservation suffice for d/(d+1)d/(d+1); the convergence of the earlier derivations could reflect shared assumptions (Section 2.3).

  • The biological comparison. The fungal values are three colony-level scaling factors from small data sets over narrow ranges, one of them against colony extension rate rather than biomass, which their authors present as hypothesis generators; definitive testing needs respirometry on isolated hyphae (Prediction 1). No organism with a genuinely two-dimensional hierarchical transport network has been identified. The three-group comparison of Section 12 was computed with a two-dimensional group that the note on d=2d = 2 sets aside, and the aggregate error that Section 6 printed until this version, which counted the two cosmology identities, is withdrawn (Appendix A). The four biological values of Table 2 are each their source’s own, read at that source; of the seven group exponents behind the three-dimensional mean of Table 10, three (fish, amphibians and crustaceans) are not yet confirmed at source in this paper, and published group exponents depend on the data set and the method (Section 12).

  • The physics rows. Two of the four rows are analytic identities, which carry no evidence, and the two fragmentation rows have no sourced values yet and await the row-by-row provenance audit that Section 6.1 names.

  • The bound. No written rule yet decides, before an exponent is fitted, which systems are governed by multiplicative composition through a finite-dimensional hierarchical network, so a counterexample could be reclassified after the fact (Prediction 4).

  • The cosmology. The mapping d=2/(1+3w)d = 2/(1+3w) is an identity; the causal reading of Section 9 is this paper’s interpretation, and it conflicts with established cosmology on when and how the cosmic web formed (Cautun et al. 2014).

  • The AI-side results. The capability fit is one model’s and inconclusive; the alignment tiers come from one unregistered run under the October 2026 code-review caveat; the Eden Protocol pilot was scored without blinding (Section 12).

  • Novelty. The paper’s novelty statements were made without a dated literature search (Appendix E).

18 Conclusion

Under stated conditions, how a recursive process combines its steps fixes the family its growth can take, among four. Within the power-law family, the exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1) that earlier work derived for hierarchical networks agrees with published values where such a network partitions space, and this paper offers an account of why those derivations converge. Where the same form appears in cosmology it is an identity. The exponent of capability against recursive depth is a different quantity, which this geometry does not fix and which has to be measured; the registered programme exists to measure it.

“One may say ‘the eternal mystery of the world is its comprehensibility.’ ”
Albert Einstein (1936)

Much of what the universe does can be described with three composition operators; that is part of why it is comprehensible. The rest is mathematics.

Every system whose growth is carried through a space-filling hierarchical network of finite dimension stays below the geometric scaling bound: the scaling exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1) approaches 1 but never reaches it, and such systems suffer diminishing returns. Systems outside that class can exceed one (Section 10).

Intelligence is no exception: its computation is physical. How its capability grows with recursive depth is a different pair of variables, which the geometry does not fix and which has to be measured (Section 11).
