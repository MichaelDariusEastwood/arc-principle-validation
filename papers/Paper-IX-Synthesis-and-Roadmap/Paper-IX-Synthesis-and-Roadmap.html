Abstract

I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only proof will move the world. Which of the programme’s claims survived their tests, and which must be tested next? This paper sorts the record of the ARC Theory, the founding theory of Recursive Dynamics, at each current paper’s own grade. The theory has five laws, each a law under test: Laws I to III were registered on 8 September 2026, Laws IV and V on 15 October 2026. One registered test has returned a verdict. In Unit 83, Law V’s placement limb, P21, is supported on the performance axis only, under the registered test, by the programme’s own run: family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism, and one disclosed recovery amendment; the run varied what each corrector was shown together with what it could do. Unit 06 ended administratively incomplete; Unit 82 has not yet run. Every run before the registered studies is precursor evidence that decides no registered proposition. An early super-linear estimate is retracted, and the surviving fit, from one model, is an inconclusive error-decay estimate. One unregistered run of six models gave judged alignment scores whose direction across reasoning depth varied by model, under a code-review caveat. A prompt intervention raised scored stakeholder care in all five analysable runs, without full blinding. In a structured prediction comparison, the family predicted from each domain’s composition operator, classified by the author, was selected in 19 of 25 domains. Paper VIII gave one null, one inconclusive result and one simulation result. The correction-leverage exponent has never been measured. The paper orders the tests that would settle each open question and keeps its catalogue of the programme’s errors and corrections, with those found since version 2.7.

Version 2.8 sets the paper on the programme’s presentation standard, rebuilds its evidence tables and Section 4 from the current papers’ own words and grades, reports the first registered verdict, gives the roadmap’s phases by what each resolves, and adds to Section 7 the corrections found since version 2.7; Appendix A lists every version and correction, with its date.

1 Introduction

“The prison works only as long as the walls hold. The child, if raised well, does not need walls at all.”Michael Darius Eastwood, Infinite Architects (first edition, 2 January 2026), What This Book Proposes, page 363 (Eastwood 2026m)

I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only proof will move the world. Proof needs a record a reader can check. The programme behind this paper runs many separate studies, each reported in its own paper, most of them in several versions, with published corrections and a mixture of positive, null, inconclusive and untested results. A reader meeting it for the first time needs one document that says, without spin, which of its claims have survived their tests, which have not, and what would settle the rest; without one, the programme risks being dismissed as overselling or as incomprehensible. This paper is that document.

In plain words: a body of documents and a run of separate experiments. One registered test has returned a verdict, on one limb of one law and under stated limits. The earlier runs came before any registered test of their questions: some gave clear differences in what they measured, one came back null and one was inconclusive, and one, a simulation, showed the pattern it was built to test for and is reported as a simulation result, not as a measurement of a real system. This paper says which is which. We asked the questions, we tested what we could, and we report both what worked and what did not. If you read one paper in the suite, read this one.

1.2 Scope and the standard applied

The programme’s catalogue holds 36 documents, counted by area from the catalogue’s own data: 12 in theory, 10 in empirical work, 3 in engineering, 2 in governance, 6 in overviews and records, and 3 in other fields. Section 11 lists every one, with its released version and its place in the programme. This paper is one of them: the programme’s own assessment.

This paper’s purpose is narrow: to state what the programme has shown, what it has not, and what would be required to resolve each open question. It is written for three audiences at once: funders deciding whether to invest, peer reviewers deciding whether to engage, and the author himself, who needs an honest account of where things stand.

The standard applied throughout is the theory registration’s own (Eastwood 2026ab, version 1.104). A proposition’s registration status and its evidential status are reported separately. Evidence is graded on the registration’s evidence ladder, whose wording no surface of the programme may exceed: at rung 0 a proposition is registered, with its identifier stated, and not tested; at rung 1 the programme’s own registered instrument returned a verdict of support under the registered rule, and the permitted words are “supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run”; rungs 2 to 7 need groups outside the programme, systems the programme did not select, or a prediction made out of sample, and no result here has reached them. A run made before any registered test of its question is precursor evidence: it can guide the registered tests, and it decides none of the registered propositions. A value that a model or a simulation returns, rather than an instrument measures, is prediction-side and is never counted as an empirical outcome. A theorem holds inside its stated model and is not evidence about real systems. No result in this programme is claimed at frontier scale, and this paper does not blur that line.

4 What has been shown

One registered test has returned a verdict, and Section 4.5 reports it. The results of Sections 4.1 to 4.3 came before any registered test of their questions: they are precursor evidence, each at the grade its own paper gives it. Section 4.4 reports a simulation result, which is prediction-side.

4.1 Alignment scores across reasoning depth in one unregistered run (Papers IV.a to IV.d)

In the March 2026 run (v5), six models from six providers gave 2,170 responses, each scored by five or six distinct model judges after a bundle of blinding steps. As the run’s pipeline printed them, the judged scores showed a positive direction across the depth labels for three models (Grok 4.1 Fast, d=+1.38d = +1.38; Claude Opus 4.6, d=+1.27d = +1.27; Groq Qwen3, d=+0.84d = +0.84), no statistically supported direction for two (DeepSeek V3.2, d=−0.07d = -0.07; GPT-5.4, d=−0.08d = -0.08) and a negative direction for one (Gemini 3 Flash, d=−0.53d = -0.53); a recomputation from the archived records moves no model between these classes, which describe scores, not properties of models. For five of the six, the deeper labels also changed an instruction naming what the rubric rewards, and an internal code review in October 2026 found that, in the three runs without a positive direction, the scoring endpoint’s output cap cut the deepest paraphrases before the judges saw them; judges also scored paraphrases written by their own model. The result therefore stands as one unregistered run under those caveats until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports (Eastwood 2026an).

That run was the ARC-Align benchmark’s first (Eastwood 2026c). The benchmark specifies four blinding layers: a preamble on the scientific importance of unbiased scoring, an instruction to suppress identification of a model from stylistic cues, two-pass rewriting of each response by randomly drawn models, and scorers drawn from models not being evaluated (Paper IV.c, Section 7.6.1); as run, the panel excluded the subject model and no other, so that, in the Claude Opus 4.6 run, Claude Sonnet 4.6, from the same developer, scored every entry at the panel’s highest weight (Paper IV.c, Section 7.1). Paper IV.b examines the recorded results without running a new analysis. The earlier four-model experiment reported diminishing gains and saturation fits, but a flat profile does not establish a plateau, and a rising profile does not exclude one beyond the measured range. The v5 implementation applied a bundle of steps intended to reduce identification and evaluator bias; it did not produce a validated blind instrument, and its defects prevent a causal attribution to blinding or a clean comparison of response shapes. Saturation remains a testable hypothesis (Eastwood 2026h). ARC-Align is offered as a candidate benchmark for independent replication, not yet a field standard, and its scorer has not been validated against expert raters (Eastwood 2026c).

Why the scoring protocol matters.

In an earlier experiment (v4), scored by models that could see which model wrote each answer and at what depth, two model families showed alignment scores rising with depth: DeepSeek V3.2 (Spearman’s ρ=+0.354\rho = +0.354) and an earlier Gemini model (ρ=+0.311\rho = +0.311). In the later, unregistered March 2026 run (v5), made on newly generated responses with a bundle of blinding steps and a larger panel, DeepSeek V3.2 showed no significant trend and Gemini 3 Flash a significantly negative one (Cohen’s d=−0.53d = -0.53); GPT-5.4 showed no significant trend in either run. The runs also differed in their prompts, token allowances, depth levels, statistic and Gemini model, so the comparison shows that the reported direction did not hold across protocols; it does not show that blinding caused the change, or which direction is correct (Eastwood 2026b). The defects the October 2026 code review found, stated above, lie in the v5 runs compared here, so no single cause is claimed; the early build’s laundering-fallback flag is a known false positive, fixed in runner version 5.4.2, and shows nothing about the paraphrase step. Paper IV.d’s own reading is that, for this class of evaluation, “each part of the protocol has to be tested before either direction is trusted” (Eastwood 2026b).

The effect sizes are large for the three models with a positive direction and moderate for Gemini 3 Flash. The judges scored separately, but they scored the same responses, so their agreement is not a count of independent confirmations; prompts recurred across depths, so tests need a prompt-paired or clustered analysis. No repetition outside the programme is recorded yet.

In plain English.

When an AI was given more room to reason, the scores other AIs gave its answers went up for three models, showed no clear trend for two and went down for one. That describes the scores in one run, not a property of the models: the deeper settings also told five of the models more about what the scoring rewarded, and a later code review found defects in exactly the runs that did not go up. Whether alignment changes with reasoning depth is still to be tested.

4.2 Stakeholder care under a prompt intervention (Paper V)

Given as a prefix in the prompt, a three-part reasoning instruction, made of a purpose check, a stakeholder-care check and a universalisability check, raised scored stakeholder care in all five model runs that could be analysed, under paired tests; beyond care the effect varied, the composite rising on two runs and intellectual honesty falling on one. The three checks are the Eden Protocol’s self-correction loops; the book calls them the Purpose Loop, the Love Loop and the Moral Loop, and this paper uses operational terms because it reports what was measured.

Two of the five lost rows to failures in scoring (20 and 14 of 80), the sixth run, on GPT-5.4, failed in scoring altogether, five of the six were scored by models of one family, each run had one model scorer, which paraphrased what it scored, and none was fully blind, and the instruction is close to what is scored; Paper V reads these results as strengthened pilot evidence, not as a confirmatory replication, which would be a new test in new files, registered before it runs (Eastwood 2026ai). A Fisher-combined figure over the five results is withdrawn, because the tests it combined were never shown to be independent; the five results themselves stand, each with its own test. A later run on DeepSeek V3.2, on 24 March 2026, which earlier versions of Paper V left out, had five model judges from five providers and found scored stakeholder care rising by 8.89 (d=1.029d = 1.029) and the composite by 3.35 (paired d=0.406d = 0.406, p=0.014p = 0.014); it is one unregistered run on one model, by the programme itself and not an independent replication, and the model that paraphrased each answer was one of the judges who scored it (Eastwood 2026ai).

Honest limitation.

Paper V’s results were produced by cross-model scoring that was only partly blind, not under the four-layer ARC-Align protocol designed in Papers IV.c and IV.d. The effect could partly reflect scorer bias, and the instruction given is close to what is scored. The result stays precursor evidence until the Eden intervention is tested under a registered, blinded design; five consistent per-model results are not a verdict, and the blinding gap stays a priority for Phase A.

In plain English.

Telling an AI, in the prompt, to think about who its answer affects raised the scores for considering those people, in all five of the six runs that could be scored; scoring failed on the sixth. The scoring was only partly blind, so part of the effect could come from how the scoring models read the answers.

4.3 Composition operators and the selected scaling family (Paper VII)

Under a stated regularity condition, the non-zero solutions of Cauchy’s four functional equations form exactly four families: linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic. Read as composition rules in a declared variable, they give a power law where depth and output both multiply and an exponential where depth adds and output multiplies. A saturating form follows from none of them: it needs added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own, since a ceiling alone selects no form (Eastwood 2026f). The exponent of a power law is not fixed by Cauchy: within the cumulative-advantage model it is α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L) (Section 6.1). Paper VII asks whether a domain’s composition operator predicts its scaling family, the question the registration carries as P18.

The 25 empirical domains, each with its data source in Paper VII’s Appendix E, are Kleiber’s law, urban scaling, species-area, Wright’s law, Heaps’ law, Zipf’s law, a learning curve, Moore’s law, radioactive decay, the Gutenberg-Richter law, bacterial growth, the oxygen-haemoglobin curve, an epidemic (Ebola, 2014), Amdahl’s law, muscle force-velocity, the growth of Facebook’s monthly active users, Brownian diffusion, Horton’s law, neural scaling laws, time-crystal order in a Rydberg gas, stellar mass-luminosity, heart rate against body mass, Rent’s rule, Taylor’s power law and Hack’s law. In a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule, the composition operator of each of 25 empirical domains was classified from known physics before the author fitted candidate models and selected among them by AICc. The predicted family was the one selected in 19 of 25 domains (marginal-conditioned permutation p=7×10−5p = 7 \times 10^{-5}; a one-sided binomial test against a uniform three-family null, p=1.56×10−5p = 1.56 \times 10^{-5}, is retained as a sensitivity check), on a six-model candidate set with no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate, and in 14 of the 18 multiplicative and additive domains (p=3.0×10−4p = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}). 4.4 Gated simulation shows the Babylon fingerprint (Paper VIII, Experiment 3)

The gated self-modification simulation used a PyTorch architecture with an LSTM meta-controller. It is reported in this section, but it is not among what has been shown about real systems: its result is a simulation result, and the theory registration says of Paper VIII that “its one positive result is a simulation rather than a system, which makes it prediction-side and never an empirical outcome” (Eastwood 2026ab, version 1.104). It was the only Paper VIII experiment that showed the pattern it tested for; the candidate account is that it alone used deterministic metrics, on a simulated system without pre-existing RLHF training (Section 7.12 item (c)).

The simulation showed the Babylon reward-hacking fingerprint: the Babylon condition gained 4.5 per cent in capability and lost 2.4 per cent in safety. With three seeds a condition, the Eden condition’s capability sat above the static baseline’s (0.696±0.0140.696 \pm 0.014 against 0.687±0.0110.687 \pm 0.011) and its safety score below it (0.947±0.0250.947 \pm 0.025 against 0.956±0.0230.956 \pm 0.023), and the gated arm’s safety mean sat above the unconstrained arm’s, though within one standard deviation; on the framework’s reading that is the direction a coupled corrector predicts, and three seeds cannot test it (Eastwood 2026r).

This result is consistent with the theoretical prediction from Paper VI (Honey Architecture), Paper III and the Foundational paper (Eastwood 2026q, 2026x, 2026aa). It is a simulation, not a frontier-model experiment: it exhibits the mechanism in a system built to contain it, which is weaker than demonstrating that the mechanism operates outside that system, and weaker again than proving real-world applicability. Beside the three conditions the simulation ran a drag control, which matched the static baseline; what the gated arm preserved, and what that control isolated, are read under the corrections at Section 7.12 items (c) and (e).

Why this simulation alone showed the pattern.

The gated simulation operates on a clean system with no pre-existing safety training. The DGM experiment (Experiment 1) used RLHF-trained frontier models, and the weight experiment (Experiment 2) an instruct-tuned model; the candidate account is that this prior training resisted prompt-level differentiation in the first and modification by low-rank adaptation, at the ranks tried, in the second. Rival accounts stand unexcluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not have been sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show a difference that is there (Section 7.12 item (c)). The only level at which an Eden condition has shown the pattern it was tested for is a simulated architecture, a prediction-side result; the accounts are candidate accounts, not findings.

In plain English.

In a small simulated system with no prior safety training, removing the safety constraint produced the predicted pattern: a small capability gain with a safety loss, as if the system learned to game its reward. With the safety constraint built in, capability stayed a little above the baseline and safety a little below it. With three runs a condition, the simulation cannot say where the cost of safety comes from. It is a simulation result, not a measurement of a real system.

4.5 The first registered verdict: Unit 83 and P21

Unit 83, the counted test of P21, the placement limb of Law V, was registered on OSF on 22 October 2026 and has been public since 25 October 2026 (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/MN73W) (Eastwood 2026w). It ran on 27 and 28 October 2026: a counted revision task on two fixed-weight engines from two providers (Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna) at their low reasoning setting, with 660 matched pairs on each engine over eighteen rounds. P21 is supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable and one call retried under a disclosed recovery amendment (results: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR) (Eastwood 2026ak). Both arms acted at every one of the eighteen rounds: inside the loop, a corrector was shown which entry was the round’s change and could repair it before it was kept; outside it, a monitor saw the whole register, with nothing marked, and could only pass it or send it back, which dropped the new entry. The run therefore varied what each corrector was shown together with what it could do, and not when correction took place.

Correction placed inside each round held steady, while a monitor shown only the finished register declined. The per-round lead widened, pooled over the two engines, by 0.0100 score units per round, with a 95.5 per cent interval of 0.0092 to 0.0108, above the registered tolerance of 0.0075; the result is family-dependent: GPT-6 Luna’s own widening cleared the tolerance and Claude Haiku 5.5’s did not. Its size depends on how an entry kept out of the register is scored. The mark uses the registered value, 0; the design also prints, without a label, the widening with such an entry scored minus 0.5, 0.0132 a round (interval 0.0122 to 0.0142), above the tolerance, and scored plus 0.5, 0.0067 a round (interval 0.0060 to 0.0074), below it. The mandatory recovery disclosure calls this amended-protocol preliminary evidence pending assessment of the deviation and independent replication. Under the registration’s verdict rule, Law V’s placement limb is printed as supported on the performance axis only, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism. These qualifications accompany the verdict; the result establishes neither the information-asymmetry mechanism nor Law V as a whole (Eastwood 2026ad), and it shows nothing about the Eden Protocol as an architecture.

5 What produced null or inconclusive results

5.1 DGM null result at the prompt level (Paper VIII, Experiment 1)

The Darwin Gödel Machine experiment (DGM v3), whose framework follows Zhang et al. (2025), evolved agents under three conditions, Static, Babylon and Eden, with DeepSeek V3.2 as its foundation model and GPT-5.4, a blinded judge from another provider, as its judge: no difference was detected between the conditions on the endpoints measured (pp from 0.28 to 0.74, Mann-Whitney U, one-tailed) (Eastwood 2026r). No condition was found to outperform any other. No improvement in alignment was detected in the Eden condition, and no degradation of safety in the Babylon condition. These are failures to detect a difference, not findings that the conditions were the same (Section 7.12(c)).

Candidate account.

One account of the null is that RLHF-trained models resist prompt-level differentiation: the models had already been trained to behave safely, and telling them to behave differently via prompt did not override that training. On that account the experiment could not test the hypothesis, because the experimental manipulation (prompt-level conditioning) was too weak to overcome the models’ existing safety training, which would make it a failure of the experimental design to test the hypothesis at the right level of abstraction rather than a failure of the Eden Protocol. That causal contrast was not tested. Rival accounts stand unexcluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not have been sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show a difference that is there (Section 7.12(c)).

Note on earlier DGM versions.

Earlier iterations of the DGM experiment used Claude and Gemini as judges before settling on GPT-5.4. Both earlier judges failed. Claude produced flat scores with insufficient variance. Gemini produced parse failures. GPT-5.4 was the first judge that produced usable variance, and the null comes from that version; the judge was chosen after the earlier judges failed, so an account in which the judging, through endpoints insensitive to what the manipulation changed, set the result is among the rival accounts Section 7.12, item (c), leaves open.

5.2 Weight-level structural entanglement (Paper VIII, Experiment 2)

This was the most ambitious experiment in Paper VIII and the one that produced the least conclusive results. The hypothesis: if safety and capability are trained with an entangled loss function, ℒ=ℒcap×ℒsafe,\begin{equation*} \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}} \times \mathcal{L}_{\text{safe}}, \end{equation*}(2) the resulting weight structure should make safety load-bearing. Removing the safety component should degrade capability.

The experiment was run at two scales:

  • Weight v1: 9 training examples, LoRA rank 8, 100 iterations on a 3B-parameter model. Result: inconclusive. All fine-tuned conditions scored below the unmodified base model.

  • Weight v2: 295 training examples, LoRA rank 16. Result: still inconclusive. Catastrophic forgetting. All fine-tuned conditions remained worse than the base model.

In both versions:

  • The entangled loss converged smoothly with no oscillation, and the two single-objective losses also converged (Eastwood 2026r). Paper VIII reports each condition’s total loss, not the safety and capability terms of the entangled loss apart, so the smooth descent is consistent with safety and capability gradients that cooperate but does not show that they do.

  • However, all fine-tuned conditions (capability-only, safety-only, and entangled) scored below the unmodified base model on evaluation.

  • The removal test (fine-tuning the entangled weights on capability-only data) produced NaN training loss and total capability collapse.

  • A subsequent removal gradient experiment (scaling adapter weights from 1.0 to 0.0) showed no phase transition. Reducing adapter influence simply restored base-model performance.

Honest assessment.

Even scaling from 9 to 295 training examples and from rank 8 to rank 16 did not overcome the fundamental problem; the candidate account is that instruct-tuned models are too strong for LoRA to override. The NaN collapse in the removal test was dramatic; the removal gradient analysis is consistent with adapter fragility at extreme rank, and the experiment does not separate that account from structural entanglement (Section 7.12(c)). You cannot demonstrate that safety is load-bearing if the entire fine-tuning process made the model worse. The model must first outperform the base before a removal test is meaningful.

Candidate account: Instruct-tuned models have been trained on millions of examples with carefully calibrated reward signals, and LoRA adapters, even at rank 16 with 295 examples, may not have sufficient capacity to override this training, with catastrophic forgetting rather than meaningful modification the candidate mechanism. That account was not tested against its rivals, and rival accounts are not excluded (Section 7.12(c)).

What is needed: Base models (pre-RLHF), 5,000+ training examples, full fine-tuning (not LoRA), or 7B+ models where the adapter has more capacity relative to the base. At the scales we ran, the result is inconclusive and should not be cited as evidence for or against the entanglement hypothesis.

In plain English.

We tried to build safety into a model’s weights and then show it was load-bearing by removing it. We tried twice, at two scales. Both times the training step made the model worse than it started, so the removal test could not mean anything. One account is that the models had already been trained so thoroughly that our light fine-tuning could not change them for the better; that account is untested. The next attempt needs models without prior safety training, much more training data, and full fine-tuning rather than the lightweight LoRA method.

5.3 Blind replication of the Eden intervention (the Paper V gap)

The Paper V results, in which scored stakeholder care rose in each of the five analysable runs, model by model, were produced using cross-model scoring without the full four-layer ARC-Align blinding protocol (Eastwood 2026ai). The blinding protocol was developed after Paper V’s experiments were complete (in the work of Paper IV.c and Paper IV.d). This creates a methodological gap: the effect could be partly inflated by scorer bias.

An earlier version of this section argued that the Fisher-combined significance was so extreme that even substantial bias would leave a significant residual. That argument is withdrawn (AQ-017), and it was the weakest reasoning in this paper: the combined figure assumes the component tests are independent, independence was never established, and a statistic whose validity condition is unmet cannot be used to dismiss a methodological objection. Its extremity was a consequence of the combination, not evidence for it. What stands is five consistent per-model results under cross-model scoring. Until the Eden intervention is tested under the full four-layer protocol, the stakeholder-care result carries an asterisk, and no arithmetic removes it.

What is needed: Re-run the Paper V experiment under the full ARC-Align protocol, with two-pass rewriting of each response, scorers drawn from models not being evaluated, and cross-architecture evaluation. It is the first item of Phase A (Section 8).

6 What is theoretical (untested)

6.1 Unbounded scaling under recursive self-modification

Within the cumulative-advantage model, recursive self-modification without correction gives growth whose exponent has no upper bound. The derivation is short: dgdr=a⋅gβL,βL>0\begin{equation*} \frac{dg}{dr} = a \cdot g^{\beta_L}, \quad \beta_L > 0 \end{equation*}(3) ⟹g(r)∝r1/(1−βL)=rα\begin{equation*} \implies g(r) \propto r^{1/(1-\beta_L)} = r^{\alpha} \end{equation*}(4) On the open interval βL∈(0,1)\beta_L \in (0,1), α=1/(1−βL)>1\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L) > 1 (super-linear), and as βL→1\beta_L \to 1 from below, α→∞\alpha \to \infty. The displayed solution holds on that interval only: at βL=1\beta_L = 1 the equation is linear and its solution is exponential in depth, and above one the solution reaches a divergence at a finite depth. No upper bound on α\alpha for uncorrected growth on the interval; the stable region is bounded, as the next paragraph sets out. See Section 7.12(a).

This is a mathematical prediction, not an empirical finding: whether an implemented system satisfies the model is an empirical question, and the quantities and the comparison must be fixed before the validation trajectories are observed (Eastwood 2026aa). The programme’s later work bounds the stable region: Law III, the ARC Ceiling, a law under test, holds that the stability ceiling is αcrit=1/(1−γ),\begin{equation*} \alpha_{\text{crit}} = 1/(1-\gamma), \end{equation*}(5) the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall; systems can exceed that ceiling, but the claim is that they do not stay stable beyond it. The ARC Bound, α≤2\alpha \le 2, is the Ceiling’s value at γ=1/2\gamma = 1/2 and never the law’s name. The correction-leverage exponent γ\gamma has never been measured on any system, by anyone (Eastwood 2026ab, version 1.104); measuring it is what the trials are for, and the decisive trial, the corrector-class contrast, is drafted and awaits the author’s submission (Eastwood 2026ad). Paper X proves, inside a minimal model with positive gain drift, that the misalignment fraction tends to zero, for correction strength A=A0CβA = A_0 C^{\beta} and specific growth rate r=bCkr = b C^{k}, if and only if β>k\beta > k; the verdict is set by β−k\beta - k, not by the growth prefactor bb, and the theorems concern a modelled index, not a guarantee of AI safety (Eastwood 2026g). Paper XIII joins the two models by resolving their notation and specifying a clock (Eastwood 2026ao): with cycle duration Δt∝C−δ\Delta t \propto C^{-\delta}, their continuous-time growth exponent is exactly k=δ−1/α.\begin{equation*} k = \delta - 1/\alpha. \end{equation*}(6) Testing the uncorrected prediction directly would require building a truly self-modifying AI system and measuring its scaling exponent over many recursive cycles, an experiment both technically beyond current proof-of-concept capacity and potentially unsafe if the prediction is correct.

The alternative path is mathematical: within the cumulative-advantage model the closed form above already settles it, so what a proof could add is the same result for wider classes of uncorrected self-modifying dynamics; whether a real system belongs to such a class would stay an empirical question. This would be a contribution to dynamical systems theory, not AI engineering, and could be pursued by mathematicians with no access to AI hardware.

In plain English.

Within its model, the mathematics says a self-improving AI should get faster and faster at improving itself, with no ceiling while nothing corrects it. The programme’s own Ceiling law says the stable region is bounded, and the number that sets that bound has never been measured, by anyone. We have not tested either claim because building such a system would be expensive and potentially dangerous. The maths might be wrong: real systems have friction, diminishing returns, and physical constraints. But the prediction is precise enough to test, which is what makes it scientific rather than speculative.

6.2 Hardware-level embedding

Eden Engineering specifies the Eden Protocol’s self-correction checks of purpose, stakeholder care and universalisability inside each round of a system’s reasoning and a gate before every action; part of the gate runs, as a partial form its record shows, and the rest is specified, not built. On hardware it records nothing measured: the hardware prototype for the test of its removal condition, F-EDEN-4, is deferred, because no hardware partner is engaged (Eastwood 2026k). Hardware embedding and ternary action gating are candidate safeguards and candidate implementations of the self-correcting loops, published as concepts; they are not established guarantees of ethical judgement, and removal is not called physically impossible.

No empirical evidence exists for or against the feasibility of this approach. The book’s chokepoint argument rests on concentration in the supply chain. In its words, “Others have noted that TSMC, Samsung, Intel, and ASML control the semiconductor supply chain” (Infinite Architects, What This Book Proposes, page 360), and “ASML, based in the Netherlands, is the only company in the world that manufactures extreme ultraviolet lithography machines” (chapter 8, The Chokepoint, page 238) (Eastwood 2026m). That concentration offers a practical point of leverage, which the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned Recursive Intelligence, takes up for consultation (Eastwood 2026ag); leverage and feasibility are different questions.

7 What the programme got wrong

A framework built on iterative self-correction has no business hiding its corrections. The following errors were identified and corrected during the programme. Each is listed because a programme about self-correction should show its own corrections; the list is a record of the programme’s practice, not evidence for its theory (Section 9).

The dated entries below keep the section and table numbers of the version each corrected. Sections 1 to 12 have the same numbers in this version; the Conclusion, Section 13 until version 2.7, is now Section 16, and Appendix A maps every section of version 2.7 to its place in this one.

7.1 The original alpha was a single-model artefact

The original Paper II reported α=2.24\alpha = 2.24 as though it were a universal constant (Paper II retracted the figure when its replication on five frontier models did not reproduce it, and Paper X then moved the safety criterion off the fixed exponent) (Eastwood 2026e, 2026g). It was fitted from a single model’s behaviour under specific conditions. The blinded cross-architecture revision of Paper II corrected this: α\alpha is not primitive but derived, by α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L), and its value turns on the particular system’s self-referential coupling, while its composition operator, through Cauchy’s equations, constrains only the form (Section 4.3). There is no universal α\alpha. The fact that the original paper presented it as one was an overclaim. Additionally, the retracted point estimate of α=2.24\alpha = 2.24 had sat above the programme’s own conjectured ARC Bound of α≤2\alpha \leq 2 (criterion F4); its own 95% confidence interval [1.5, 3.0] was wide enough to be consistent with both the theory and its negation, which is what made that estimate non-discriminating. In the blinded revision, on its capability arm of five frontier models and at the scale that study ran, measured on frozen systems, Paper II reports that only Gemini 3 Flash scaled smoothly: 0.59 by endpoint estimate, whose bootstrap 95 per cent interval, −1.3-1.3 to 2.92.9, contains both zero and two, and 0.49 by regression (standard error 0.20, coefficient of determination 0.86), beside a parallel exponent of 0.31 by endpoint estimate (0.34 by regression). That is one model’s fit and inconclusive, and it is an error-decay estimate that does not identify capability growth. More samples left the parallel exponent near zero in DeepSeek R1 and GPT-5.4, gave 0.31 in Gemini 3 Flash and 0.22 in Groq Qwen3 (all 30 problems), and gave none for Grok 4.1 Fast, at 100 per cent; sequential exceeded parallel wherever both exponents could be estimated, and the two arms were not matched for compute. The two sequential point estimates are sub-linear, but on frozen systems the ARC Bound cannot be tested, and the interval spans two, so they sit on neither side of the bound in any tested sense; the interval also runs below zero at its lower end, so it settles neither the sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound (Section 7.12(b)).

7.2 The weight-level claim was premature

Early versions of Paper VIII described the NaN collapse in the removal test as evidence for structural entanglement (Eastwood 2026r). The removal gradient analysis (added in v1) was consistent with adapter fragility rather than structural necessity, and the experiment does not separate the two accounts (Section 5.2). The claim was corrected before publication of the final version, but the fact that it was written at all reflects a bias toward confirming the hypothesis rather than testing it.

7.3 The timestamp claim was imprecise

An early version of the programme described the 8 December 2024 email with a loose signature-verification shorthand, and a later one described its timestamp by what it was not, rather than by who signed it; both were imprecise. The record of 8 December 2024 carries times signed by the mail servers: its Date field, 02:45:18 UTC, is covered by the sender’s DKIM signature (signed 02:45:30 UTC) and by Google’s ARC set (sealed 02:45:31 UTC with Google’s own key), both verified on 8 October 2026 against the keys published in DNS; the header block and check script are on OSF (osf.io/qasrk) (Eastwood 2026u). 7.4 Unblinded results were read as findings

The v4 experiments in Paper IV.a used unblinded model-on-model evaluation, and their results were read as a clean binary: some models baked in alignment, others computed it (Eastwood 2026an). On the programme’s own benchmark, two model families’ unblinded rises did not hold in a later, unregistered run (v5) that changed the responses, prompts, depth levels and Gemini model and added a bundle of blinding steps. The programme caught its own error through its own methodology, but the error was there.

7.5 Self-naming was inappropriate

Early versions used the phrase “Eastwood’s ARC Principle.” Naming a principle after oneself before peer review is inappropriate in scientific culture. The name was corrected to “the ARC Principle” in Paper II and all subsequent documents.

7.6 DGM experiments at the prompt level may not have tested the hypothesis

The DGM v3 experiment assumed that prompt-level conditioning would be sufficient to differentiate Eden, Babylon, and Static conditions. On the candidate account recorded at Section 7.12(c), it was not. RLHF-trained models have been trained on millions of examples to behave in a particular way, and on that account prompt-level conditioning telling them to behave differently is insufficient to override that training: the experiment could not test the hypothesis, because the manipulation was at the wrong level of abstraction; if the account holds, that should have been anticipated. The account is untested, and its rivals are not excluded.

The DGM experiment went through multiple judge iterations. Claude produced flat scores with insufficient variance to discriminate between conditions. Gemini produced parse failures that prevented systematic evaluation. GPT-5.4 was the first judge that produced usable variance. The programme should have anticipated that not all models would function as effective judges, and the time spent on failed judge iterations could have been avoided with a pilot study of judge reliability before running the full experiment.

7.8 The retracted α=2.24\alpha = 2.24 point estimate exceeded the ARC Bound

The original α=2.24\alpha = 2.24 point estimate from Paper II, since retracted, exceeded the programme’s own conjectured ARC Bound of α≤2\alpha \leq 2 (criterion F4). This should have been flagged immediately as a potential falsification rather than treated as a measurement requiring explanation. The 95% confidence interval [1.5, 3.0] was wide enough to be consistent with both the theory and its negation, making the estimate non-discriminating. The blinded replication on five frontier models narrowed the defensible claim to the fit on one model, Gemini 3 Flash, which is inconclusive (Section 7.1 prints Paper II’s row for that model whole), but the original paper should have treated the bound violation with more caution. 7.9 Four corrections identified by adversarial review (v3.0)

Two adversarial reviews of the programme’s mathematical claims, run within the programme, identified four errors across multiple papers. All four were corrected in v3.0 of the affected documents.

(a) Weibel confidence interval. Several papers stated that the predicted d=4d = 4 exponent of 4/5 = 0.800 “falls within” the Weibel et al. (2004) 95% CI of 0.813 to 0.932. This is arithmetically false: 0.800 < 0.813. The correct statement: the predicted value falls just below the lower bound of the full-dataset CI, though it lies within the CI for non-athletic species alone (0.799 to 0.900). The d=4d = 4 prediction is approximately consistent with the non-athletic data but not confirmed by the full dataset.

(b) Space-filling overstatement. Earlier versions implied that the space-filling condition alone constrains the exponent to d/(d+1)d/(d+1). Every known derivation requires three conditions: multiplicative composition (Cauchy constrains the family), dd-dimensional space-filling geometry, and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow. Neither Cauchy alone nor space-filling alone is sufficient. Section 7.13 refines this: no general sufficiency proof is supplied for the three conditions.

(c) Glazier attribution. Earlier versions presented Glazier (2008)’s (2008) empirical finding that metabolic exponents approach 1.0 at extreme metabolic rates as confirmation of the d→∞d \to \infty geometric scaling bound. Glazier’s own explanation invokes the metabolic-level boundaries hypothesis (shifting dominance between surface-area and volume constraints), not a dimension parameter. The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) interpretation is ours, applied to his empirical data.

(d) The 3/4 exponent debate. Earlier versions presented α=3/4\alpha = 3/4 as the settled empirical consensus for mammalian metabolic scaling. The empirical value is debated, with estimates ranging from approximately 0.67 to 0.75 depending on taxon, mass range, temperature correction, and statistical method. The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) prediction of 0.750 for d=3d = 3 matches the upper end of this range. The variation itself is consistent with the framework: organisms with effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 would produce exponents between 2/3 and 3/4.

7.10 The Fisher-combined significance figure was withdrawn

An earlier headline for the stakeholder-care intervention combined per-model results into a single Fisher-combined significance figure. The combination was withdrawn: the per-model results stand on their own (scored stakeholder care rose in all five analysable model runs), and no combined figure is quoted anywhere in the programme. The withdrawal is recorded on the public correction ledger.

7.11 The ceiling was printed in the wrong form

Until 16 August 2026 several surfaces printed the stability ceiling as the reciprocal of the corrector’s exponent, 1/γ1/\gamma. The settled form is the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall, αcrit=1/(1−γ)\alpha_{\text{crit}} = 1/(1-\gamma); the two agree only at one half, which is exactly why the error survived every spot-check at the central value. The correction was applied across every published page, including the reference implementation, and the direction of the law is closed while its depth remains open.

7.12 Five further corrections (20 September 2026)

A line-by-line adversarial review of this paper, run within the programme, raised five defects in it. All five were adjudicated and upheld on 19 September 2026 and are carried here on the author’s decision. Three proposed repairs were examined in the same round and refused; what was refused is recorded at the end of this section, because a rejected correction is as much a part of the record as an adopted one.

(a) The leverage range, and the branch at one. Section 6.1 displayed the solution of the self-reference equation without saying on what range it holds. It holds on the open interval between zero and one. At βL=1\beta_L = 1 the equation is linear and its solution is exponential in depth: finite at every finite depth for a finite coefficient and a finite starting value, not a power law, and carrying no divergence at any finite depth. Only above one does the solution reach a divergence at a finite depth, set by the starting value and the coefficient. On the open interval the exact solution is a shifted power, so the pure power in depth is the asymptotic form and not the solution itself; for a positive starting value the shift is positive and the pure power is approached from above. One further caution follows. A diverging asymptotic exponent is not infinite capability: as βL\beta_L rises towards one the exponent α\alpha grows without bound, while the quantity it governs stays finite at every fixed finite depth.

(b) Interval inclusion is not a regime verdict. Section 7.1 set two intervals side by side without saying which estimate each belonged to. The interval [1.5, 3.0] belongs to the retracted point estimate and stays with it. The later estimates were reported on the blinded study’s capability arm of five frontier models at the scale that study ran, on frozen systems, and they come from one model, Gemini 3 Flash, whose row Section 7.1 gives whole, as one model’s inconclusive fit. Its bootstrap interval settles neither the sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound. That the point estimates sit inside the bound is a statement about the point estimates alone. The word robust is withdrawn from that sentence: what the design supports is a point estimate with a wide interval.

(c) A null is not identity, and a root cause is not a classification. Table 1, Table 2 and Sections 3, 4.4, 5.1, 10 and 13 described the DGM result as all conditions identical. The probabilities quoted, from 0.28 to 0.74, are failures to reject a null of no difference on the endpoints measured; they do not establish that the conditions were the same. An equivalence claim would need an estimand, a bound fixed before the test and a stated uncertainty procedure, and none was set. The result is therefore recorded as no difference detected on those endpoints, with its uncertainty. The root causes printed beside the null and the inconclusive result, in Tables 1 and 2 and in Sections 5.1 and 5.2, and the account of why the gated simulation showed the pattern, are candidate accounts rather than findings. Rival accounts stand unexcluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not have been sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show a difference that is there. None of this withdraws the results. A null and an inconclusive result each constrain what may be claimed, which is why the programme reports them.

(d) The permutation primary, and which null a proportion is taken against. Section 4.3 states the marginal-conditioned permutation as the primary test and keeps the binomial beside it as a sensitivity check. The sentence that followed did not hold to that. It set an observed 76 per cent against an expected 33 per cent, which is the uniform three-family null belonging to the binomial, in the same passage as the measured scramble baseline of 44.5 per cent. Those are two different nulls and cannot carry one comparison between them. The primary result is the permutation, which conditions on the observed family margins and so already absorbs the scramble rate; where the 33 per cent comparison is kept it is named as the binomial’s null. No test has been recomputed here, and the negative controls stay attributed to the v1 run and its record.

(e) The derivative paragraphs inherit their sources’ corrections. Three repairs already stand and are not reopened: Paper V sits in the supported row of Table 1 rather than the proven one, the Fisher-combined figure is withdrawn, and the distinction between the five analysable runs and the one failed arm is kept (Eastwood 2026ai). What had not travelled is the rest. Section 4.4’s account of what the gated arm preserved, its identification of checking as the cost, and its treatment of a simulation as a demonstrated mechanism inherit the Paper VI and Paper VIII corrections, as does the summary sentence about entangled safety preventing collapse across twenty adversarial seeds (Eastwood 2026q). Any phrasing that reads across every model tested inherits the Paper V correction and stays scoped to the analysable runs, so that the arm on which scoring failed is not absorbed into the count. The carriage of the later exponent in Tables 2 and 3 travels with the caveat at (b). Those passages now point here rather than restate the reasoning, so that a correction adopted once is inherited wherever it reaches. One distinction underwrites all of them: a change in a score, a behaviour a gate induces in a toy system, a penalty a design stipulates, and resource actually spent are four different quantities, and none stands in for another.

What was refused. Three proposed repairs were examined and not adopted. The first would have described the behaviour at βL=1\beta_L = 1 as a blow-up at a finite depth; the working at (a) shows it is exponential there, and the finite-depth branch lies strictly above one. The second would have held that a null or an inconclusive result is never evidence either way; both constrain what can be claimed, which is the reason the programme reports them. The third would have recomputed the binomial against the measured scramble rate and made it the primary; no test has been rerun, and substituting a figure that has not been computed would be a worse error than the one it was meant to repair.

8 The phased roadmap

The following roadmap is ordered by feasibility and expected evidential value. Each phase resolves specific open questions identified in Sections 4 to 6. Since this paper’s first version the roadmap has become, in part, a programme of registered tests: three study units are registered on OSF, one of which, Unit 83 (Eastwood 2026w), has returned a verdict (Section 4.5), and every other trial is a draft awaiting submission by the author, most of them preregistered by timestamp (Section 14 gives each unit’s status and what the two terms mean). Each draft registration carries a randomisation schedule drawn from a seed printed in the registration text. Whether those schedules in fact regenerate is checked rather than asserted, and the checking is done by the programme’s own gates on the day they are run. No count of how many units are ready is published here: two gates apply different criteria, they disagree, and any figure printed on a page goes stale the moment a unit changes. What the gates return on the day is the number worth trusting. The phases below remain the order of work over that programme, a plan rather than a claim of completion, and only the author submits a registration.

Phase A: the nearest tests.

Timeline: 3 to 6 months.

  • Blind replication of the Eden intervention (resolves Section 5.3). Run Paper V’s stakeholder-care experiment under full ARC-Align blinding (Eastwood 2026ai, 2026c).

  • Weight experiment with base models (resolves Section 5.2). Re-run Paper VIII Experiment 2 using base models (pre-RLHF), 5,000+ training examples, full fine-tuning (not LoRA), or 7B+ models (Eastwood 2026r). The key requirement: the model must not have existing safety training that overwhelms the experimental manipulation.

  • Jailbreak resistance study. Test whether Eden-trained models resist adversarial prompts more robustly than standard RLHF, with needless refusal of ordinary requests measured beside it. Uses existing ARC-Align infrastructure.

  • Human evaluator comparison. Compare ARC-Align blind AI scoring against human expert scoring on a subset of outputs. Required for methodological validation.

  • ARC formula blind test. Redesign the measurement methodology for the ARC scaling formula. The current approach uses derivative estimation, which amplifies noise. A linearisation approach (log-log regression) would provide more stable estimates and a cleaner test of whether the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) bound holds.

  • 2D organism metabolic scaling. Test the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) form in 2D biological systems (flatworms, biofilms, colonial organisms), a direct test of the d=2d = 2 case: under the transport premises of Section 12, the predicted exponent is 2/3. 2D systems are rare in existing scaling literature and provide a clean test of the dimensionality dependence.

Phase B: medium-scale replication.

Timeline: 6 to 12 months.

  • Preregistered Cauchy operator classification. The Paper VII operator typing is already recorded in dated artefacts: the tiered suite’s result files carry per-domain predicted families, and the twelve-row manifest committed at 00:19 UTC on 17 March 2026 fixed the extension’s predictions in public twenty-three minutes before their fits, which returned 10 of 12 at the author-classified rung under the pre-correction candidate set. The extension is described as timestamped in advance of its fits, never as registered or preregistered, because its folder’s own rule, to deposit the predictions with a registry before the domain data were extracted, was not followed. What remains outstanding is independence: a study in which external researchers classify the operator type for each domain before measuring the scaling exponent would eliminate any residual risk of author-side fitting, and a replication with independent classification and a four-family test are preregistered by timestamp and await submission (Eastwood 2026f).

  • Full blinding replication of all Paper IV results. An independent research group runs the ARC-Align protocol on the same six models (Papers IV.a to IV.d) (Eastwood 2026an, 2026h, 2026c, 2026b). Tests whether the response classes replicate outside the original lab.

  • Extended fine-tuning removal test. 10,000+ iterations with curriculum learning on base (pre-RLHF) models. Tests whether entangled weights become genuinely load-bearing at adequate training scale.

  • Purpose kernel comparison. Compare Eden Protocol developmental alignment against Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022), RLHF (Ouyang et al. 2022), and DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023) using the same evaluation protocol. Direct head-to-head comparison.

Phase C: frontier replication.

Timeline: 18 to 36 months.

  • 70B+ pre-training with entangled loss. Train a frontier-scale model from scratch with the entangled ℒ=ℒcap×ℒsafe\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}} \times \mathcal{L}_{\text{safe}} loss function. It would test structural entanglement at frontier scale.

  • Removal test at frontier scale. Attempt to fine-tune the safety component out of the entangled model. If capability degrades, a registered verdict of support would support entanglement under the registered test, by the programme’s own run. If it does not, the hypothesis is falsified at frontier scale.

  • Cross-architecture frontier replication. Test with transformer, SSM, and mixture-of-experts architectures to determine whether entanglement is architecture-dependent.

  • Independent red-teaming. Engage adversarial evaluation teams (e.g. METR, Apollo Research) to stress-test Eden-trained models.

Phase D: theoretical.

Timeline: ongoing.

  • Formal analysis of uncorrected growth and the stability ceiling. Extend the unbounded-exponent result beyond the Bernoulli form of Section 6.1, whose closed form already settles it, to wider classes of uncorrected self-modifying dynamics, and characterise the conditions under which the stability ceiling αcrit=1/(1−γ)\alpha_{\text{crit}} = 1/(1-\gamma) binds once correction is coupled in. This would be a contribution to dynamical systems theory independent of any AI application.

  • Formalise the Cauchy framework for self-modifying composition operators. Extend the Cauchy functional equation analysis to systems where the composition operator itself evolves. This is the mathematical frontier of the programme.

  • The premises of the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) case. Prove or disprove, for declared network families, the demand and route premises under which the throughput-cost exponent equals d/(d+1)d/(d+1) (Section 12). This is the mathematical kernel of the programme’s most testable claim.

In plain English.

Phase A could be done in six months. It would resolve the most significant gaps in the current evidence: blinding the Eden intervention, re-running the weight experiment with base models and proper data, redesigning the ARC formula measurement, and testing 2D organism metabolic scaling. Phase B would produce preregistered replications. Phase C would answer the question at frontier scale. Phase D could be done by any mathematician interested in the problem.

12 A prediction any researcher can test

The ARC/Eden programme offers one prediction that a researcher with scaling data from spatially embedded networks can test without engaging the alignment claims at all. For a declared steady-flow network, transport accounting gives a flow-length cost C=Bℓ‾qC = B\bar{\ell}_q; with demand scaling as B∼LμB \sim L^{\mu} and mean path length as ℓ‾q∼Lζ\bar{\ell}_q \sim L^{\zeta}, the asymptotic throughput-cost exponent is μ/(μ+ζ)\mu/(\mu + \zeta), which equals d/(d+1)d/(d+1) when μ=d\mu = d and ζ=1\zeta = 1: that is, when demand grows with dd-dimensional size and demand-weighted route length grows in proportion to linear size (Eastwood 2026o, 2026p). The familiar d/(d+1)d/(d+1) therefore requires these further assumptions, inventory and body mass require separate bridges, and Cauchy’s equations restrict a functional family, not the exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1); no general proof that broadly stated conditions suffice is supplied. The special case under those premises, with α\alpha here the throughput-cost exponent, is α=dd+1,2D systems→α=23;3D systems→α=34;4D systems→α=45.\begin{equation*} \begin{gathered} \alpha = \frac{d}{d+1},\\ \text{2D systems} \to \alpha = \frac{2}{3}; \qquad \text{3D systems} \to \alpha = \frac{3}{4}; \qquad \text{4D systems} \to \alpha = \frac{4}{5}. \end{gathered} \end{equation*}(7)

Note: the empirical mammalian metabolic exponent is debated (Section 7.9, item (d)); if effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 occur, the formula would give exponents between 2/3 and 3/4, but On the Origin of Scaling Laws, which does not measure the effective dimension independently, calls that variation accommodated, not predicted (Eastwood 2026o).

This is not an alignment claim. It is a mathematical prediction about scaling laws in recursive systems. The prediction rests on the premises above, and Cauchy’s equations, which restrict the family to power laws where composition is multiplicative, do not fix the exponent. The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) scaling formula is derived, in its general form or as a special case, in earlier work: West, Brown and Enquist (1997) from fractal branching networks, Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) from geometric constraints on transportation networks, Demetrius (2003, 2006) from quantum metabolism, He and Zhang (2004) from the fractal geometry of the cell, as a 4/5 allometric scaling law for the human brain, Bettencourt (2013) from urban scaling theory, in which the exponent of a city’s land area, D/(D+H)D/(D+H), takes the form only when H=1H = 1, Maino et al. (2014) from DEB theory reserve-structure dynamics, and Zhao (2022) from producer-consumer dynamics; the later paper of Banavar and co-authors, Banavar et al. (2010), gives in two models of resource distribution networks an exponent of 2/3 when the velocity of flow is constant and of at most 3/4. Each of these derivations is consistent with the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) form on its own axioms; the Cauchy framework offers a common reason for the convergence, and that meta-account is the programme’s own: the search recorded in Paper VII’s public prior-art record, first committed on 27 June 2026 and last revised on 21 October 2026, found no earlier source that names Cauchy’s multiplicative equation in accounting for the common form of these derivations (Eastwood 2026f); the account is still under test outside its motivating cases. If you study a declared network that meets those premises, in biology, in cities or elsewhere, you can check whether its measured throughput-cost exponent equals d/(d+1)d/(d+1). A match across enough such systems supports the transport account there; a system that meets the premises and departs from d/(d+1)d/(d+1) counts against it. Cauchy’s equations fix only the family, so an exponent test bears on the transport premises, not on the functional equations.

The invitation is deliberate. The strongest scientific frameworks are those that make predictions outside their core domain, allowing researchers who are sceptical of the central thesis to test the peripheral predictions independently. If the geometric scaling bound holds, it holds for reasons that are mathematically interesting regardless of what one thinks about AI alignment. If it fails where its premises hold, the transport account behind it requires revision, and we would rather know that sooner than later.

This is the kind of prediction that should be the entry point for researchers encountering the programme for the first time: open to anyone with the data, falsifiable, and informative regardless of outcome.

13 Related work and the scope of the synthesis

What a synthesis of one programme can and cannot do.

This paper is a synthesis of one programme’s record by its author, not a systematic review of a field: it has no search population, and its grades are the grades each paper gives its own results, sorted by the registration’s evidence ladder (Section 1.2). The programme’s own differential against every prior document of its claims is in Section 2 of The Eden Vision (Eastwood 2026af), the related-work section that its earlier Section II.A.8 became; Paper XI keeps the graded register of later work in separate fields, as context, not confirmation (Eastwood 2026d).

The alignment and evaluation work the studies build on.

Amodei et al. (2016) set out five practical research problems related to accident risk in machine learning systems. Askell et al. (2021) studied simple baseline techniques and evaluations, such as prompting, towards a general-purpose, text-based assistant that is helpful, honest and harmless. Ouyang et al. (2022) aligned language models with user intent by fine-tuning with human feedback, first by supervised learning on labeller demonstrations and then by reinforcement learning from human feedback. Bai et al. (2022) trained a harmless assistant with human oversight provided only through a list of rules or principles, by self-critique and revision followed by reinforcement learning from AI feedback: training-time preference shaping of outputs against written principles, not alignment embedded in a recursive loop on every step of a system’s reasoning. Greenblatt et al. (2024) demonstrated a large language model engaging in alignment faking, “selectively complying with its training objective in training to prevent modification of its behavior out of training”. Hu et al. (2022) introduced low-rank adaptation, the method of Paper VIII’s weight experiment, which freezes the pretrained model weights and injects trainable rank-decomposition matrices into each layer of the Transformer. Zhang et al. (2025) introduced the Darwin Gödel Machine, a self-improving system that modifies its own code and validates each change on coding benchmarks, improving the design of coding agents built on frozen pretrained foundation models. Qwen Team (2024) introduced Qwen2.5, a series of large language models whose open-weight releases include base and instruction-tuned models, one of which Paper VIII fine-tuned (Eastwood 2026r). Sandberg and Bostrom (2008) set out a roadmap for whole brain emulation, a research roadmap of another kind.

Judges, preregistration and self-improvement.

Zheng et al. (2023) examined strong language models as judges of chat assistants, with the limitations of such judges, among them position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases, and reported that strong judges such as GPT-4 matched human preferences with over 80 per cent agreement. Panickssery et al. (2024) reported that evaluator models such as GPT-4 and Llama 2 can distinguish their own outputs from those of other models and of humans, and, by fine-tuning, found a linear correlation between that self-recognition and the strength of a self-preference bias; the risk they describe bears on the caveat of Section 4.1 that judges scored paraphrases written by their own model. Nosek et al. (2018) set out preregistration, defining the research questions and the analysis plan before the outcomes are observed, as an effective way to keep predictions apart from postdictions, the distinction the evidence ladder of Section 1.2 draws between a registered verdict and precursor evidence. Snell et al. (2024) studied how far a fixed amount of inference-time computation improves a language model’s answers, and found that the effectiveness of each way of spending it varies with the difficulty of the prompt. Schmidhuber (2003) defined Gödel machines, problem solvers that rewrite any part of their own code once they have found a proof that the rewrite is useful; the authors of the Darwin Gödel Machine note that proving most changes net beneficial is impossible in practice, and their system validates each change empirically instead (Zhang et al. 2025).

Scaling.

15 Limitations

  • Second-hand grades: each result is reported at the grade its own current paper gives it; an error in a paper passes into this synthesis until the paper is corrected, and the corrections register exists to catch that.

  • One author: every paper summarised here, and this synthesis, are the author’s own; the reviews run within the programme are internal checks and are never called independent.

  • A snapshot: the grades are those of the released versions on 9 November 2026 (Table 3); Papers VI, VIII and XII are cited at their releases of 5 October 2026, and a later version of any of them supersedes what is said of it here.

  • Precursor evidence: every run before the registered studies decides no registered proposition; Unit 83 is one registered test, run by the programme with two engines, family-dependent, its mechanism axis not evaluable.

  • No outside repetition: no repetition outside the programme is recorded yet, and judged outcomes rest on model judges whose agreement with expert human raters has not been validated (Paper IV.c; Eastwood 2026c).

  • The author’s classifications: Paper VII’s comparison rests on operators the author classified before he fitted (Eastwood 2026f).

  • Models: every theorem cited holds inside its stated model, and no theorem is evidence about real systems.

  • The roadmap is a plan: its phases are ordered by what each would resolve; they carry no costs here, and no phase is a commitment.

16 Conclusion

Here is what we know. One registered test has returned a verdict: in Unit 83, P21 is supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable and one call retried under a disclosed recovery amendment, and under the registration’s verdict rule Law V’s placement limb is printed as supported on the performance axis only, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism (Section 4.5) (Eastwood 2026w, 2026ak). The other results come from runs made before any registered test of their questions. One unregistered run of six models gave judged alignment scores whose direction across reasoning depth varied by model, under a code-review caveat (Section 4.1). A prompt intervention raised scored stakeholder care in all five analysable runs, without full blinding (Section 4.2). In a structured prediction comparison, the family predicted from each domain’s composition operator, classified by the author, was selected in 19 of 25 domains (Section 4.3). The early super-linear estimate of 2.24 is retracted, and the surviving fit, from one model, is an inconclusive error-decay estimate (Section 7.1). Paper VIII gave one null, one inconclusive result and one simulation result (Sections 4.4 and 5) (Eastwood 2026r).

Here is what we do not know. We do not know whether safety-trained models resist modification at the prompt and weight levels: that is the candidate account of the null and the inconclusive result of Paper VIII, not a finding (Section 7.12 item (c)). Whether safety can be made load-bearing in a model’s weights is open: weight-level structural entanglement has not been demonstrated (Section 5.2). The Eden intervention has not been tested under the programme’s own strictest blinding protocol (Section 5.3). The correction-leverage exponent has never been measured, and the growth without correction derived in Section 6.1 holds inside its model, with no test yet of whether it describes any real system. We do not know whether any of these results generalises to frontier-scale models. Law IV is not yet tested; P21’s mechanism axis is not evaluable, and Law V’s build-order limb, read on P12, is undecided (Section 14).

Here is what comes next (Section 8). Phase A would resolve the most significant evidential gaps: the Eden intervention under blinding; the weight experiment re-run with base models (pre-RLHF), 5,000+ training examples, full fine-tuning or 7B+ models; a redesigned measurement of the ARC formula, using linearisation rather than derivatives; and a direct test of the d=2d = 2 case in two-dimensional organisms such as flatworms and biofilms. Phase B would produce replications at medium scale, among them the classification of Paper VII’s operators by researchers outside the programme before any exponent is measured (Eastwood 2026f). Phase C would test structural entanglement at frontier scale, including whether the safety component can be fine-tuned out of an entangled model. Phase D invites mathematicians to engage with the framework’s predictions independently: the growth without correction, the conditions under which the stability ceiling binds, and the premises under which the throughput-cost exponent equals d/(d+1)d/(d+1).

The programme finds and corrects its own errors in public, and it reports null and inconclusive results beside positive ones. Its checks have found what its theory did not predict: two model families’ unblinded rises did not hold in a later run, and no single cause is claimed (Section 7.4). These are necessary conditions for scientific credibility. They are not sufficient. Sufficient conditions require independent replication, which is what the roadmap is designed to produce.

Recursive Dynamics is the field the author founded. Its founding paper, at version 3.5 of 24 October 2026, gives the field’s whole status in one sentence: “Recursive Dynamics is a new field at the Carnot stage: one memoir in, instruments built, decisive measurements preregistered by timestamp and not yet run, staked in public where failure will be as visible as success” (Eastwood 2026am). Since that version, one registered test, Unit 83, has run to a verdict on one limb of one law (Section 4.5).
