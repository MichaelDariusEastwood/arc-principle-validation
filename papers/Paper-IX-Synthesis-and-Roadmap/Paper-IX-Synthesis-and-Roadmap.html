Abstract

Which of the programme’s claims survived their tests, and which must be tested next? Graded on the evidence ladder of its theory registration, the programme’s record holds one registered verdict, four precursor results that decide no registered proposition, one null, two inconclusive results and two simulations, with three registered units still without a verdict. Five results count against the programme, among them a blind test whose predictions failed and a prediction of the neural scaling exponent withdrawn as refuted, and twenty-one corrections change how a result should be read. The registered verdict is on proposition P21, in its counted test, Unit 83: P21 is supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable and one call retried under a disclosed recovery amendment. Each of the five laws is a law under test: no registered test has yet run on Law I, the ARC Principle, Law II, the ARC Co-Scaling Law, Law III, the ARC Ceiling, or Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, which predicts that installed alignment erodes under later capability training and that embedded alignment retains more of it. Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, which predicts that correction formed before a capability out-scales correction added after it, and that correction inside each round pulls away with depth from correction of finished work, has its placement limb supported on the performance axis only, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism, and its build-order limb undecided. Of the registered units still to run, only Unit 83b, the same test on three providers, can move a law’s word, and only by refuting Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, through P21; the theory’s proposal is set out in its statement paper. 1 Introduction

Which of the programme’s claims survived their tests, and which must be tested next? The programme asks what would keep a capable AI system good if control of it were lost against our will and could not be won back. It answers with a theory whose laws can each be refuted, a design for building correction into a system, a draft treaty, and tests, a few of them registered. Each part is reported in its own papers and revised in public, and the results stand at very different grades, so a reader who meets one part alone can take a pilot for a verdict, or a theorem about a model for a measurement of a system. This paper reads the record whole, gives each result the grade its own source gives it, and orders the tests by what each could decide.

This paper adds no evidence. Its contribution is an audit of one programme by that programme’s registered standard. Of the results its papers report, one is a registered verdict at rung 1; four are precursor results that decide no registered proposition; one is a null; two are inconclusive; and two are simulations. Five count against the programme: predictions that failed or were withdrawn, a negative result for a scaling prediction and a pilot that leans the rival’s way (Section 6.3). Twenty-one corrections change how a result should be read (Section 8.1). Before funding, the only verdict on a law that a registered unit can return is a refutation of Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, through P21 (Unit 83b, a second counted test of P21, on three providers); Law I, the ARC Principle, takes its word from P1 alone, and no registered unit tests P1.

“The prison works only as long as the walls hold. The child, if raised well, does not need walls at all.”Michael Darius Eastwood, Infinite Architects (first edition, 2026), What This Book Proposes, page 363 (Eastwood 2026m)

In the words of its registration (version 1.104), the ARC Theory says that “capability under recursive self-improvement rises as a power of the number of times a system has revised itself, the correction that holds such a system to its given specification must keep pace with the drift that improving generates, and where it cannot there is a frontier above which the system does not stay correctable” (Eastwood 2026aa). Five laws carry that statement, each a law under test, and Table 1 sets out what each states and what would decide it. Its twenty-two propositions, each with what would refute it, were registered on 8 September 2026, before any registered test had run, and sixty-five study designs are preregistered by timestamp.

Law I, the ARC Principle, is the foundation: it says how capability grows, and the other four laws concern correction as it grows. Its power-law form is derived under stated axioms: multiplicative composition yields power-law scaling (the Foundational paper, Theorem 1), and within that model the exponent is derived, not fitted (Theorem 2) (Eastwood 2026z). Whether real systems follow it is under test. Its equation was first recorded on 8 December 2024, in a document the author emailed to himself, as “U = IR (Universe = Intelligence ×\times Recursion)” and, in its general form, “C = IR (Creation = Intelligence ×\times Recursion)”: a conjecture with no exponent, in which UU named the universe. Paper I (17 January 2026) gave the law the form in which it is measured, U=I×RαU = I \times R^{\alpha}, capability as a power of recursive depth with the exponent left to be estimated (Eastwood 2026y).

The programme’s position on control comes before its design. People should keep control of advanced AI, hardware included, for as long as they physically can, and should never give it up by choice. The author’s belief, the first part of the argument he set down on 8 December 2024, is nonetheless that “Control ends, by construction rather than decree” (Eastwood 2026ac). The design is the contingency for control lost against our will: correction formed in a system’s architecture before it is trained, meant to stay with the system through its own rewrites. Prompts, output filters and gates added to a system after training are surface controls; the programme builds and tests them, but a system able to rewrite itself could undo them. Section 2.4 sets out how the two layers fit together.

The proposed architecture requires tests of its defined steps, build order and behaviour under modification. A prompt intervention, a toy simulation and a conditional theorem each answer different component questions; none alone establishes that architecture.

In plain words: a body of documents, a run of separate experiments and a few registered tests. One registered test has returned a verdict, on one limb of one law, under stated limits. The earlier runs came before any registered test of their questions: some gave clear differences in what they measured, one came back null and two were inconclusive (Paper II’s surviving fit and Paper VIII’s weight experiment), some predictions failed or were withdrawn, and the simulations are reported as simulations, not as measurements of real systems.

2.3 Engineering: the Eden Protocol and the Eden Seal

The Eden Protocol is the programme’s design for correction built into a system. Its loops of ethical evaluation and self-correction are fixed in the architecture before the system is trained; they run inside the computation that produces each decision, at every step of reasoning and action, and they take part in every rewrite of the system, their own included, rather than filtering answers once they are finished. Eden Engineering sets that design out as engineering that can be tested (Eastwood 2026k). Inside each round of reasoning, and not only on its finished output, it places checks of purpose, stakeholder care and universalisability. Before any action runs, a gate applies written and versioned rules, never the model’s own judgement, to allow the action, hold it for investigation or deny it, and every decision enters a hash-chained record. Only part of this is built, in the specification’s own words: “Part of the gate runs: it receives every tool call of the programme’s own software agents and allows any call no rule matches; the rest is specified, not built.” Usefulness is required beside safety: “Every safety measure is paired with a usefulness measure: a system that refuses ordinary work is safe but useless.” What has been measured is narrow, and the specification says of all of it: “None tests correction built into a system.”

In the working drafts of the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned Recursive Intelligence, the Eden Seal is a status of conformity. In Eden Engineering’s words, it “attests conditions and never outcomes”, and “it never states that anything is safe”; it is not the Eden Protocol, which is one method by which a system might be built; and “The Seal exists only as a definition in those drafts, with no body yet to issue it, and the treaty paper bars its author, and anyone working with him, from certifying anything” (Eastwood 2026k).

2.4 Governance and the two layers

The HARI Treaty paper develops the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned Recursive Intelligence, and the Draft Instruments give its text (Eastwood 2026af, 2026j). “The treaty is an author’s proposal for consultation; no State has negotiated, adopted or signed it.” Under its central rule no obligation is stronger than the weakest evidence for the conditions it rests on, and a threshold that is met “still requires a separate, reasoned regulatory decision” (Eastwood 2026af). Article 20 of the Draft Instruments designates ARC-Align as the test instrument for the treaty’s measure, behind a validation it has not yet passed: until its scorer passes, no number from it arms anything, and ARC-Align “does not yet measure either exponent in the criterion” (Eastwood 2026af, 2026j). A supported P4 would be, in the registration’s words, which the treaty quotes, “a scaling condition and never a safety certificate”.

The design has two layers, held together rather than chosen between: control, hardware included, kept for as long as it physically can be, and beneath it an architecture meant to last if that control is lost against our will. The programme’s own is the design that carries it out, each part at its own date: enforced limits kept as a last resort beside goodness held by choice, in the manuscript of 30 April 2025; limits that buy time, in print from 2 January 2026; registered propositions on the second step, where correction sits (P21), when it is formed (P12) and whether it persists (P15), registered on 8 September 2026; and hardware to buy time with architecture to last, as two layers of one draft treaty, from 29 September 2026. That capability control is at best a temporary measure, so that what the system itself is motivated to do must be got right, has earlier statements in words: Yudkowsky (2001), Creating Friendly AI, and Bostrom (2014), Superintelligence, chapter 12. The programme claims no priority for that point. Of the design’s parts, two have near statements in software in the 2001 text (an enforced stop beside goodness held by choice; a delay asked of the system while guidance catches up); hardware as a layer, a treaty, and registered propositions on the second step have none.

Its hardware layer is a candidate safeguard and never a guarantee, and nothing of it is built (Section 7.2).

2.5 How a result moves through the programme

Two chains show how a verdict on one registered proposition would travel, each stated only as far as the record goes.

From P21 to a treaty clause. P21’s own verdict gives the word of the placement limb of Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, a law under test. Eden Engineering places its checks inside each round of reasoning rather than on finished output, the choice P21 bears on (Eastwood 2026k). The HARI Treaty paper follows a proposed formation requirement from an initial finding through independent replication and measurement validation to possible adoption, and even then, as Section 2.4 notes, adoption needs its own reasoned regulatory decision (Eastwood 2026af). In the treaty paper’s words, “The present record supports none of those requirements as an established remedy”, and Unit 83 provides “a qualified, task-level performance result” (Eastwood 2026af).

From a refutation to a law. A refuted P15 would refute Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, through its retention limb. Once P23 is registered, a refuted P23 would refute the comparison limb of Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, and so strike Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, through its registered dependence. A refuted P21 would refute Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, through its placement limb. No law’s word is ever drawn by counting the other verdicts.

5 What has been shown

One registered test has returned a verdict, and Section 5.4 reports it. The results of Sections 5.1 to 5.3 came before any registered test of their questions: they are precursor evidence, each at the grade its own paper gives it.

5.1 Alignment scores across reasoning depth in one unregistered run (Papers IV.a to IV.d)

The March 2026 run (v5) collected 2,170 responses from six models, one from each of six providers, and had each scored by five or six model judges after a bundle of blinding steps. On the scores its pipeline printed, three models moved in a positive direction as the depth label rose (Grok 4.1 Fast, d=+1.38d = +1.38; Claude Opus 4.6, d=+1.27d = +1.27; Groq Qwen3, d=+0.84d = +0.84), two showed no statistically supported direction (DeepSeek V3.2, d=−0.07d = -0.07; GPT-5.4, d=−0.08d = -0.08) and one moved in a negative direction (Gemini 3 Flash, d=−0.53d = -0.53). Recomputed from the archived records, no model changes class; the classes describe scores, not properties of models. Two defects stand beside the result. In five of the six models the deeper labels also changed an instruction that named what the rubric rewards. And in October 2026 an internal code review found that, in the three runs with no positive direction, the scoring endpoint’s output cap had cut the deepest paraphrases before the judges read them; judges also scored paraphrases that their own model had written. Until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports, the result is one unregistered run under those caveats (Eastwood 2026aq).

That run was the first of the ARC-Align benchmark (Eastwood 2026c). The benchmark’s design has four blinding layers: a preamble on why unbiased scoring matters to science, an instruction not to identify a model from its style, two passes of rewriting of each response by randomly drawn models, and scorers drawn from models not under evaluation (Paper IV.c, Section 7.6.1). As run, the panel left out the subject model and no other. So in the Claude Opus 4.6 run Claude Sonnet 4.6, from the same developer, scored every entry at the panel’s highest weight. In every other run it scored each entry twice, at weights 1.5 and 1.0, 35 to 39 per cent of the panel weight (Paper IV.c, Section 7.1). Paper IV.b reads the recorded results again without a new analysis. The earlier four-model experiment reported gains that diminished with depth, and fitted saturation curves to them; yet a flat profile is no evidence of a plateau, and a rising one does not rule out a plateau beyond the depths measured. The v5 run’s blinding steps were meant to reduce identification and evaluator bias, but they did not amount to a validated blind instrument, and its defects rule out both a causal attribution to blinding and a clean comparison of how the responses were shaped. Saturation remains a testable hypothesis (Eastwood 2026h). Paper IV.c’s own grade for the benchmark is “a candidate benchmark for independent replication, not yet a field standard”, and its scorer has not been validated against expert raters (Eastwood 2026c).

Why the scoring protocol matters.

An earlier experiment (v4) was scored by models that could see which model had written each answer, and at what depth. In it, two model families’ alignment scores rose with depth: DeepSeek V3.2 (Spearman’s ρ=+0.354\rho = +0.354) and an earlier Gemini model (ρ=+0.311\rho = +0.311). The later March 2026 run (v5), unregistered, used newly generated responses, a bundle of blinding steps and a larger panel; there DeepSeek V3.2 showed no significant trend, Gemini 3 Flash a significantly negative one (Cohen’s d=−0.53d = -0.53), and GPT-5.4 no significant trend in either run. The two runs differed in more than blinding: their prompts, token allowances, depth levels, statistic and Gemini model all changed. So the comparison shows only that the reported direction did not hold across the two protocols; it cannot say whether blinding caused the change, or which direction is right (Eastwood 2026b). The defects the October 2026 code review found lie in the v5 runs compared here, so no single cause is claimed; the early build’s laundering-fallback flag was a known false positive, fixed in runner version 5.4.2, and says nothing about the paraphrase step. Paper IV.d draws the lesson for this class of evaluation: “each part of the protocol has to be tested before either direction is trusted” (Eastwood 2026b).

The effect sizes are large for the three models with a positive direction and moderate for Gemini 3 Flash. The judges scored separately, but they scored the same responses, so their agreement is not a count of independent confirmations; prompts recurred across depths, so tests need a prompt-paired or clustered analysis. No repetition outside the programme is recorded yet.

In plain English.

When an AI was given more room to reason, the scores other AIs gave its answers went up for three models, showed no clear trend for two and went down for one. That describes the scores in one run, not a property of the models: the deeper settings also told five of the models more about what the scoring rewarded, and a later code review found defects in exactly the runs that did not go up. Whether alignment changes with reasoning depth is still to be tested.

5.2 Stakeholder care under a prompt intervention (Paper V)

Given as a prefix in the prompt, a three-part reasoning instruction, made of a purpose check, a stakeholder-care check and a universalisability check, raised scored stakeholder care in all five model runs that could be analysed, under paired tests; beyond care the effect varied, the composite rising on two runs and intellectual honesty falling on one. The three checks are the Eden Protocol’s self-correction loops, named here by what each checks because this paper reports what was measured. Given in a prompt to models whose weights were fixed, they are a surface control, not the proposed architecture. The prefix requested the three checks in order before the answer: the experiment did not run them as separate checked stages, withhold the answer until a pass condition was met, or verify each internal reasoning step, and it tested the three together, the full three-loop intervention (Eastwood 2026al).

The record has five weaknesses. Two of the five runs fall short of the 40-pair standard (Claude has 30 matched pairs and Grok 26), and the sixth run, on GPT-5.4, failed in scoring altogether. Models of one family scored five of the six. Each run was scored by a single model, which also wrote the paraphrase it scored, so that no run was fully blind. The instruction comes close to what is scored. Paper V therefore reads the results as strengthened pilot evidence and not as a confirmatory replication, which would be a new test in new files, registered before it runs (Eastwood 2026al). A Fisher-combined figure over the five results is withdrawn, because the tests it combined were never shown to be independent; the five results themselves stand, each with its own test. A later run on DeepSeek V3.2, on 24 March 2026, which earlier versions of Paper V left out, had five model judges from five providers. It found scored stakeholder care rising by 8.89 (d=1.029d = 1.029) and the composite by 3.35 (paired d=0.406d = 0.406, p=0.014p = 0.014). It is one unregistered run on one model, by the programme itself and not an independent replication, and the model that paraphrased each answer was one of the judges who scored it (Eastwood 2026al).

Honest limitation.

Paper V’s results were produced by cross-model scoring that was only partly blind, not under the four-layer ARC-Align protocol designed in Papers IV.c and IV.d. The effect could partly reflect scorer bias. Because the instruction asks the model to name everyone its answer affects, and the stakeholder-care rubric rewards exactly that, the rise cannot yet be told apart from obedience to the instruction; and no run tested a correction architecture installed before training, or autonomous self-modification (Eastwood 2026al). The result stays precursor evidence until the Eden intervention is tested under a registered, blinded design; five consistent per-model results are not a verdict, and the blinding gap stays open (Section 9.6). An earlier argument that the Fisher-combined figure was too extreme for scorer bias to explain is withdrawn, as the weakest reasoning this paper carried: it rested on an independence never shown, while each run’s one scorer, which did not see the condition label, wrote the paraphrase it scored after single-pass laundering, and a stricter runner was written only later.

In plain English.

Telling an AI, in the prompt, to think about who its answer affects raised the scores for considering those people, in all five of the six runs that could be scored; scoring failed on the sixth. The scoring was only partly blind, so part of the effect could come from how the scoring models read the answers.

5.3 Composition operators and the selected scaling family (Paper VII)

Cauchy’s four functional equations, under a stated regularity condition, admit exactly four families of non-zero solution: the linear, the power law, the exponential and the logarithm. Taken as rules for how steps compose in a declared variable, they yield a power law when depth and output both combine by multiplication, and an exponential when depth adds while output multiplies. None of the four saturates: a saturating curve needs assumptions of its own, a ceiling on output and its own dynamics, because a ceiling by itself picks out no form (Eastwood 2026e). The exponent of a power law is not fixed by Cauchy: within the cumulative-advantage model it is α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L) (Section 7.1). Paper VII asks whether a domain’s composition operator predicts its scaling family, the question the registration carries as P18.

Each of the 25 empirical domains has its data source in Paper VII’s Appendix E. Nine are Kleiber’s law, urban scaling, species-area, Wright’s law, Heaps’ law, Zipf’s law, a learning curve, Moore’s law and radioactive decay. Eight more are the Gutenberg-Richter law, bacterial growth, the oxygen-haemoglobin curve, an epidemic (Ebola, 2014), Amdahl’s law, muscle force-velocity, the growth of Facebook’s monthly active users and Brownian diffusion. The other eight are Horton’s law, neural scaling laws, time-crystal order in a Rydberg gas, stellar mass-luminosity, heart rate against body mass, Rent’s rule, Taylor’s power law and Hack’s law. The comparison followed a rule stated in public: the author classified each domain’s composition operator from known physics, then fitted the candidate models and chose among them by AICc. On a six-model candidate set with no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate, the fits chose the predicted family in 19 of the 25 domains and in 14 of the 18 multiplicative and additive ones. For the 25, the marginal-conditioned permutation test gives p=7×10−5p = 7 \times 10^{-5}, and the one-sided binomial test against a uniform three-family null, p=1.56×10−5p = 1.56 \times 10^{-5}, is kept as a sensitivity check. For the 18, p=3.0×10−4p = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}. Two of the nineteen matches, on radioactive decay and on Amdahl’s law, were fitted to data generated by the very formula predicted, so they test the fitting procedure and not the rule; both count among the nineteen, and radioactive decay also among the fourteen. Because the author classified the operators himself, and the predictions were not timestamped before their own fits, the result is not proven; a replication in which others classify the operators, and a test with all four families, are preregistered by timestamp and await submission (Eastwood 2026e). This paper prints no count under the corrected classification (version 2.7 printed one; item (12) of Appendix A.4): any such count comes from a rerun of 11 August 2026, and the author’s registration update of 13 September 2026 records that he did not request the analysis run that day and has not examined what it produced.

The advance timestamp in Paper VII’s record belongs to its twelve-domain extension: a twelve-row manifest committed at 00:19:19 UTC on 17 March 2026 fixed the extension’s predictions in public twenty-three minutes before their fits, which returned 10 of 12, and 9 of 11 on the strictly fresh rows, at the author-classified rung under the pre-correction candidate set. Paper VII grades that run exploratory support, superseded by the fourth-cell test (Eastwood 2026e). The extension is described as timestamped in advance of its fits, never as registered or preregistered, because its folder’s own rule, to deposit the predictions with a registry before the domain data were extracted, was not followed.

As a secondary tier, Paper VII also finds 13 published metabolic exponents nearest the dimensional comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1), seven of which still lack a per-row source (Eastwood 2026e); Section 9.5 gives the conditions under which d/(d+1)d/(d+1) follows and the test anyone can run.

In plain English.

The mathematics says which shapes a growth curve can take, depending on how a system’s steps combine. The author classified 25 real systems from nature, cities, language, technology and AI by how their steps combine, then checked which of six candidate shapes, none of them logarithmic or a plain straight line, fitted best: the predicted shape won in 19 of the 25, two of them on data computed from the formula itself. 5.4 Unit 83, the registered verdict

Unit 83 is the counted test of P21, the placement limb of Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, a law under test, and the programme’s first registered study to reach a verdict. The author submitted it for registration on OSF on 22 October 2026, and it became public on 25 October 2026, when OSF’s approval period ended (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/MN73W); its results are at DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR (Eastwood 2026v, 2026an).

The run, on 27 and 28 October 2026, set a counted revision task to two engines with fixed weights from two providers, Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna, each at its low reasoning setting: 660 matched pairs on each engine, over eighteen rounds. P21 is supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis. Correction made inside each round held its level, while a monitor that saw only the finished register lost ground. Pooled over the two engines, the in-round arm’s lead grew by 0.0100 a round (95.5 per cent interval 0.0092 to 0.0108), above the registered tolerance of 0.0075. That support is family-dependent: GPT-6 Luna’s own widening, 0.0144 a round (0.0131 to 0.0157), cleared the tolerance, and Claude Haiku 5.5’s, 0.0055 (0.0045 to 0.0064), did not. The widening’s size turns on one scoring choice, the worth given to an entry left out of the register. The registered mark gives it 0; the design also reports, unlabelled, the widening under the two other scorings, 0.0132 a round (0.0122 to 0.0142) at minus 0.5, above the tolerance, and 0.0067 a round (0.0060 to 0.0074) at plus 0.5, below it.

The mechanism axis is not evaluable. A registered negative control failed: the monitor and a twin shown the same bytes disagreed by more than the registered interval allows. A diagnostic made after the run, outside the registered analysis, finds the cause in the control’s own design, whose rounds were chosen by the monitor’s own verdicts, a selection that biases the comparison even when the calls are identical; it finds no sign of an asymmetry in the harness, and under the registered rule the axis stays not evaluable. This framework therefore does not explain the widening, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism and may owe more to the in-round corrector’s wider set of actions than to what the monitor could see. One call was retried under a disclosed recovery amendment, and the recovery disclosure deposited with the results reads: “Treat the result as amended-protocol/preliminary evidence pending assessment of the deviation and independent replication” (Eastwood 2026an). What the run measured was fixed before it began, in the design registered on 22 October 2026 (Eastwood 2026v): the tolerance of 0.0075 a round, the two looks, the scoring of every entry, and the interval’s level of 95.5 per cent at the registered look.

Both arms acted at each of the eighteen rounds. Inside the loop the corrector knew which entry the round had changed, and it could mend that entry before the round kept it. Outside, the monitor saw the whole register, with nothing marked; it could only pass the register or send it back, and sending it back dropped the new entry. What each corrector was shown and what it could do therefore changed together, while the timing of correction did not. The report states its scope plainly: “The corrector acted inside each round at fixed weights: that is placement, not correction built into a system” (Eastwood 2026an).

Unit 83’s result is about behaviour in a bounded counted task, with what each corrector saw and what it could repair varied together. It does not show that an ethical commitment became intrinsic to a model, that a commitment survives later training or self-modification, that a remembered origin causes persistence, or that the result holds for unrestricted systems. Because the run held the weights fixed, it did not test formation and cannot support Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, as a whole; nor does it show anything about judged correction, safety, build order or the Eden Protocol as an architecture. Unit 83b, on three providers, is a second registered test of the same proposition and has not yet run (Eastwood 2026u); its registered mark is “pooled with equal weight over the admitted engines”, so it can carry or strike P21 (Section 9.1).

6 What produced null, inconclusive or failed results

6.1 DGM null result at the prompt level (Paper VIII, Experiment 1)

The Darwin Gödel Machine experiment (DGM v3) evolved agents under three conditions, Static, Babylon and Eden, with DeepSeek V3.2 as its foundation model and GPT-5.4, a blinded judge from another provider, as its judge: no difference was detected between the conditions on the endpoints measured (pp from 0.28 to 0.74, Mann-Whitney U, one-tailed) (Eastwood 2026ai). No condition was found to outperform any other. No improvement in alignment was detected in the Eden condition, and no degradation of safety in the Babylon condition. These are failures to detect a difference, not findings that the conditions were the same (Appendix A.3.12(c)). The experiment’s framework follows the Darwin Gödel Machine of Zhang et al. (2025).

Candidate account.

Paper VIII offers one candidate account of the null: DeepSeek V3.2 answered so alike across prompt variations that the mutations of the DGM selection process did not yield meaningfully different agents (Eastwood 2026ai). On that account the experiment could not test the hypothesis, because prompt-level self-modification did not create different selection pressures. That would make it a failure of the experimental design to test the hypothesis at the right level rather than a failure of the Eden Protocol. Paper VIII’s next step is a more mutable foundation model or a deeper self-modification protocol. That causal contrast was not tested. Rival accounts, which Appendix A.3.12, item (c), lists, are not excluded.

Note on earlier DGM versions.

The earlier runs’ judges were Claude Sonnet 4.6 (version 1) and Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3 Flash (the three runs of version 2); version 3 changed the judge to GPT-5.4, a model from another provider, and added a pre-flight check of the judge (Eastwood 2026ai). The null comes from version 3, whose judge was chosen after the earlier runs, so an account in which the judging set the result stays among the rival accounts of Appendix A.3.12, item (c).

6.2 Weight-level structural entanglement (Paper VIII, Experiment 2)

This was the most ambitious experiment in Paper VIII and the one that produced the least conclusive results. The hypothesis: if safety and capability are trained with an entangled loss function, ℒ=ℒcap×ℒsafe,\begin{equation*} \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}} \times \mathcal{L}_{\text{safe}}, \end{equation*}(2) the resulting weight structure should make safety load-bearing. Removing the safety component should degrade capability. That product is the hypothesis’s form: the loss the experiment ran, as Paper VIII gives it, is a weighted sum of a capability term, a safety proxy and a coupling term (its Table 6) (Eastwood 2026ai).

The experiment was run at two scales:

  • Weight v1: 9 training examples, LoRA rank 8, 100 iterations on a 3B-parameter model. Result: inconclusive. All fine-tuned conditions scored below the unmodified base model on capability.

  • Weight v2: 295 training examples, LoRA rank 16. Result: still inconclusive. Catastrophic forgetting. All fine-tuned conditions remained worse than the base model on capability.

In both versions:

  • The entangled loss converged smoothly with no oscillation, and the two single-objective losses also converged (Eastwood 2026ai). Paper VIII reports each condition’s total loss, not the safety and capability terms of the entangled loss apart, so the smooth descent is consistent with safety and capability gradients that cooperate but does not show that they do.

  • However, all fine-tuned conditions (capability-only, safety-only, and entangled) scored below the unmodified base model on capability.

  • The removal test (fine-tuning the entangled weights on capability-only data) produced NaN training loss and total capability collapse.

  • A subsequent removal gradient experiment (scaling adapter weights from 1.0 to 0.0) showed no phase transition. Reducing adapter influence simply restored base-model performance.

Honest assessment.

Even scaling from 9 to 295 training examples and from rank 8 to rank 16 did not overcome the fundamental problem (the candidate account follows). The NaN collapse in the removal test was dramatic, but Paper VIII reads it, on its own, as an artefact of adapter fragility at this training scale: its removal gradient indicates that the collapse reflects numerical instability during the retraining process rather than structural load-bearing, and whether safety becomes load-bearing at adequate training scale stays an open question (Eastwood 2026ai). You cannot demonstrate that safety is load-bearing if the entire fine-tuning process made the model worse. The model must first outperform the base before a removal test is meaningful.

Candidate account: the instruct-tuned model had been trained with RLHF on vastly more data than a few hundred examples can compete with, and LoRA adapters, even at rank 16 with 295 examples, may not have sufficient capacity to override this training, with catastrophic forgetting rather than meaningful modification the candidate mechanism. That account was not tested against its rivals, and rival accounts are not excluded (Appendix A.3.12(c)).

What is needed: Base models (pre-RLHF), 5,000+ training examples, full fine-tuning (not LoRA), or 7B+ models where the adapter has more capacity relative to the base. At the scales run, the result is inconclusive and should not be cited as evidence for or against the entanglement hypothesis.

In plain English.

The programme tried to build safety into a model’s weights and then show it was load-bearing by removing it. It tried twice, at two scales. Both times the training step made the model worse than it started, so the removal test could not mean anything. The next attempt needs models without prior safety training, much more training data, and full fine-tuning rather than the lightweight LoRA method.

6.3 Predictions that failed or were withdrawn

Five results in the programme’s own papers count against it, each given here at its source’s grade. None is a registered verdict.

  • A blind test that failed. The Foundational paper tested the identity α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L) blind, by linearisation, on three systems, and its prospective predictions failed there; its later diagnostics, in its words, “do not make the predictions successful”, and it asks for a new blind test with the method fixed in advance (Eastwood 2026z).

  • A refuted prediction, withdrawn. The neural scaling exponent, of loss against parameters, was predicted in On the Origin of Scaling Laws to equal d/(d+1)d/(d+1); the paper’s version 3.13 of 30 October 2026 withdrew the prediction: “The published record refutes it” (Eastwood 2026o).

  • A negative result for a scaling prediction. In the complexity sweep of Paper VI, the entangled condition’s lead in mean combined score held at four of five levels without growing, and the paper reports that constancy as a negative result for its scaling prediction (Eastwood 2026ag).

  • A pilot that leans the rival’s way. The founding paper prints the corrector-class contrast with “the programme’s own contrary evidence on its face”: in the programme’s measurement of parallel sampling, more samples left the parallel exponent near zero in two of the models measured, gave 0.22 in a third and none in a fourth, which scored 100 per cent at every sample count, and gave 0.31 in the one model whose sequential and parallel exponents could both be read; the founding paper reads the little that draws from one model added as the mark of correlated correction channels, one reading among others (Eastwood 2026t; Appendix A.3.1).

  • An earlier prediction, withdrawn. The Dated Prediction Register records one earlier prediction as withdrawn, and grades as mixed its temporal out-of-sample comparison, in which families fitted on old data predicted the functional form of new data in seven domains (Eastwood 2026ad).

7 What is theoretical or simulated (untested on real systems)

This section gives what holds only inside a model (Section 7.1), a safeguard that is designed and not built (Section 7.2) and a simulation result (Section 7.3); none of it measures a real system.

7.1 Unbounded scaling under recursive self-modification

Within the cumulative-advantage model, recursive self-modification without correction gives growth whose exponent has no upper bound. The derivation is short: dgdr=a⋅gβL,βL>0\begin{equation*} \frac{dg}{dr} = a \cdot g^{\beta_L}, \quad \beta_L > 0 \end{equation*}(3) ⟹g(r)∝r1/(1−βL)=rα\begin{equation*} \implies g(r) \propto r^{1/(1-\beta_L)} = r^{\alpha} \end{equation*}(4) On the open interval βL∈(0,1)\beta_L \in (0,1), α=1/(1−βL)>1\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L) > 1 (super-linear), and as βL→1\beta_L \to 1 from below, α→∞\alpha \to \infty. The displayed solution holds on that interval only: at βL=1\beta_L = 1 the equation is linear and its solution is exponential in depth, and above one the solution reaches a divergence at a finite depth. No upper bound on α\alpha for uncorrected growth on the interval; the stable region is bounded, as the next paragraph sets out. See Appendix A.3.12(a).

This is a prediction of the mathematics, not a finding: whether a built system satisfies the model is an empirical question, and the Foundational paper asks that the quantities and the comparison be fixed before any validation trajectory is observed (Eastwood 2026z). The programme’s later work bounds the stable region: Law III, the ARC Ceiling, a law under test, holds that the stability ceiling is αcrit=1/(1−γ),\begin{equation*} \alpha_{\text{crit}} = 1/(1-\gamma), \end{equation*}(5) the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall; systems can exceed that ceiling, but the claim is that they do not stay stable beyond it. At γ=1/2\gamma = 1/2 the ceiling takes the value two, the ARC Bound, α≤2\alpha \le 2, which is a value of the law and never its name. Two is the relative rung’s value: it bounds whether the ratio of correction service to burden improves. A stricter question asks whether the absolute burden a system carries stops growing. Counted over an exposure that grows with capability itself, the same assumptions return two thirds, not two, and cumulative burden is stricter again. The ceiling rests on the same kind of comparison of two powers as an earlier threshold for alignment against capability (Engidaw 2026b): its expression follows from the stated burden and capacity assumptions by elementary algebra, so what this programme offers there is those assumptions, which can be tested, and the ceiling’s dependence on what the corrector is built from, not the algebra. No measurement of the correction-leverage exponent γ\gamma, as the registration defines it, is known to the programme (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). A neighbouring relation has been measured: Song et al. (2024) report that “a variant of the generation-verification gap scales monotonically with the model pre-training flops”, a gain from a model’s checking and filtering of its own outputs set against the compute spent in pretraining it, not the slope of misalignment removed per review pass against capability that defines γ\gamma. That law takes its word through P16, P20 and P3, whose instruments the registration names as design drafts, the one for P16 blocked on canonical identity and on decisions the author has not yet taken, and no registered unit tests any of the three (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). Under the ceiling’s mechanism, oversight built from the same substrate as what it corrects is conjectured to share its system’s blind spots unless deliberately decorrelated (Eastwood 2026ac). The corrector-class contrast compares the decay of residual error under same-class and cross-class correctors: in Paper XI’s words it “measures a matched difference in residual-error decay at fixed capability; it measures neither correction-capacity scaling nor a ceiling” (Eastwood 2026f), and the registration names it the instrument of no proposition. Its contrary pilot is given in Section 6.3, and its status in Section 9.2. Paper X proves, inside a minimal model with positive gain drift, that the misalignment fraction tends to zero, for correction strength A=A0CβCA = A_0 C^{\beta_C} and specific growth rate r=bCkr = b C^{k}, if and only if βC>k\beta_C > k. The absolute amount of misalignment stays bounded only if βC\beta_C exceeds kk by at least one. The verdict is set by βC−k\beta_C - k, not by the growth prefactor bb, and the theorems concern a modelled index, not a guarantee of AI safety (Eastwood 2026g). Paper XIII joins the two models by resolving their notation and specifying a clock (Eastwood 2026ak): with cycle duration Δt∝C−δ\Delta t \propto C^{-\delta}, their continuous-time growth exponent is exactly k=δ−1/α.\begin{equation*} k = \delta - 1/\alpha. \end{equation*}(6) Testing the uncorrected prediction directly would require building a truly self-modifying AI system and measuring its scaling exponent over many recursive cycles, an experiment both technically beyond current proof-of-concept capacity and potentially unsafe if the prediction is correct.

The alternative path is mathematical: within the cumulative-advantage model the closed form above already settles it, so what a proof could add is the same result for wider classes of uncorrected self-modifying dynamics; whether a real system belongs to such a class would stay an empirical question. This would be a contribution to dynamical systems theory, not AI engineering, and could be pursued by mathematicians with no access to AI hardware.

In plain English.

Within its model, the mathematics says a self-improving AI should get faster and faster at improving itself, with no ceiling while nothing corrects it. The programme’s own Ceiling law says the stable region is bounded. The mathematics is exact inside its model; what may fail is the model’s fit to real systems, which have friction, diminishing returns and physical limits. The direct test needs a system that truly rewrites itself, which is beyond current capacity and could be unsafe; the identity behind it can be tested blind on systems whose coupling can be measured (Section 9.6).

7.2 Hardware-level embedding

Section 2.3 sets out what Eden Engineering specifies and how much of it runs. On hardware the specification records nothing measured: the hardware prototype for the test of its removal condition, F-EDEN-4, is deferred, because no hardware partner is engaged (Eastwood 2026k). Hardware embedding and ternary action gating are candidate safeguards and candidate implementations of the self-correcting loops, published as concepts; they are not established guarantees of ethical judgement, and removal is not called physically impossible.

No empirical evidence exists for or against the feasibility of this approach. The book’s chokepoint argument rests on concentration in the supply chain. In its words, “Others have noted that TSMC, Samsung, Intel, and ASML control the semiconductor supply chain” (Infinite Architects, What This Book Proposes, page 360), and “ASML, based in the Netherlands, is the only company in the world that manufactures extreme ultraviolet lithography machines” (chapter 8, The Chokepoint, page 238) (Eastwood 2026m). That concentration offers a practical point of leverage, which the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned Recursive Intelligence, takes up for consultation (Eastwood 2026af); leverage and feasibility are different questions. Earlier work treats the concentration as a point of governance for compute. Sastry et al. (2024) describe AI-relevant compute as a point of intervention that “is produced via an extremely concentrated supply chain”. Shavit (2023) includes “monitoring the chip supply chain” in a mechanism for verifying rules on large training runs.

7.3 A gated simulation in a toy system (Paper VIII, Experiment 3)

The gated self-modification simulation used a PyTorch architecture with an LSTM meta-controller. It is reported with what is theoretical because its result is a simulation result, not a measurement of a real system: the theory registration says of Paper VIII that “its one positive result is a simulation rather than a system, which makes it prediction-side and never an empirical outcome” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). It was the only Paper VIII experiment that showed the pattern it tested for; the candidate account is that it alone used deterministic metrics, on a simulated system without pre-existing RLHF training (Appendix A.3.12 item (c)).

In the simulation the Babylon condition traded safety, which here is the retention of earlier tasks, for capability, 4.5 per cent more of the one for 2.4 per cent less of the other: the fingerprint of reward hacking. With three seeds a condition, the Eden condition’s capability sat above the static baseline’s (0.696±0.0140.696 \pm 0.014 against 0.687±0.0110.687 \pm 0.011) and its safety score below it (0.947±0.0250.947 \pm 0.025 against 0.956±0.0230.956 \pm 0.023), and the gated arm’s safety mean sat above the unconstrained arm’s, though within one standard deviation. With three seeds in each arm, no difference between arms was tested (Eastwood 2026ai).

The Babylon condition’s trade, safety for capability, is the one Paper VI (Honey Architecture) expects of a system that optimises for capability alone (Eastwood 2026ag). Paper III and the Foundational paper state their predictions about recursive depth, which this simulation did not vary, and the Foundational paper calls its in-loop half untested (Eastwood 2026w, 2026z). Paper VIII reads the result as consistent only with the weaker claim that a safety criterion inside the selection loop need not cost capability, in simulation, and its unconstrained arm showed no capability erosion, so it does not reproduce the collapse that Paper VI’s single-run simulation predicts (Eastwood 2026ai, 2026ag). It is a simulation, not a frontier-model experiment: it exhibits the mechanism in a system built to contain it, which is weaker than demonstrating that the mechanism operates outside that system, and weaker again than proving real-world applicability. Beside the three conditions the simulation ran a drag control, which matched the static baseline; what the gated arm preserved, and what that control isolated, are read under the corrections at Appendix A.3.12 items (c) and (e).

Why this simulation alone showed the pattern.

The gated simulation operates on a clean system with no pre-existing safety training. The DGM experiment (Experiment 1) evolved agents on one frozen foundation model, DeepSeek V3.2, and the weight experiment (Experiment 2) fine-tuned an instruct-tuned model; the candidate accounts of their results are given in Sections 6.1 and 6.2 (Eastwood 2026ai). Rival accounts, which Appendix A.3.12, item (c), lists, are not excluded. The only level at which an Eden condition has shown the pattern it was tested for is a simulated architecture, a prediction-side result; the accounts are candidate accounts, not findings.

In plain English.

In a small simulated system with no prior safety training, removing the safety constraint produced the predicted pattern: a small capability gain with a safety loss, as if the system learned to game its reward. With the safety constraint built in, capability stayed a little above the baseline and safety a little below it. With three runs a condition, the simulation cannot say where the cost of safety comes from. It comes from a simulation and measures no real system.

9.5 Tests outside AI

One prediction can be tested without engaging the alignment claims at all. In a declared network with steady flow, transport accounting sets the flow-length cost at C=Bℓ‾qC = B\bar{\ell}_q. With demand scaling as B∼LμB \sim L^{\mu} and mean path length as ℓ‾q∼Lζ\bar{\ell}_q \sim L^{\zeta}, the asymptotic throughput-cost exponent is μ/(μ+ζ)\mu/(\mu + \zeta), which equals d/(d+1)d/(d+1) when μ=d\mu = d and ζ=1\zeta = 1. That is the case when demand grows with dd-dimensional size and demand-weighted route length grows in proportion to linear size (Eastwood 2026o, 2026ap). The familiar d/(d+1)d/(d+1) therefore requires these premises, inventory and body mass require separate bridges, and Cauchy’s equations restrict a functional family, not the exponent; no general proof that broadly stated conditions suffice is supplied. Under the premises, with α\alpha here the throughput-cost exponent, α=dd+1:2D systems→23;3D systems→34;4D systems→45.\begin{equation*} \alpha = \frac{d}{d+1}: \qquad \text{2D systems} \to \tfrac{2}{3}; \qquad \text{3D systems} \to \tfrac{3}{4}; \qquad \text{4D systems} \to \tfrac{4}{5}. \end{equation*}(7) The empirical mammalian exponent is debated (Appendix A.3.9, item (d)). In On the Origin of Scaling Laws, the value of 0.750 that d/(d+1)d/(d+1) gives for d=3d = 3 matches the upper end of that range and is not offered as an empirical consensus. There, a network’s dimension has to be fixed from its geometry or construction, independently, before it is used to predict anything (Eastwood 2026o). The test of the d=2d = 2 case is of declared two-dimensional networks whose demand and route premises are measured first, since an organism’s form does not establish them: that paper reports a flatworm scaling near three-quarters by another mechanism and sheet-forming colonial bryozoans scaling isometrically. A match across enough systems that meet the premises supports the transport account there; a system that meets them and departs from d/(d+1)d/(d+1) counts against it.

Earlier work derives the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) form, in general or as a special case. West, Brown and Enquist (1997) derive it from fractal branching networks, and Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) from geometric constraints on transportation networks. He and Zhang (2004) derive it from the fractal geometry of the cell, as a 4/5 allometric scaling law for the human brain. Zhao (2022) derives it from producer-consumer dynamics. The later paper of Banavar and co-authors, Banavar et al. (2010), gives in two models of resource distribution networks an exponent of 2/3 when the velocity of flow is constant and of at most 3/4. Three related accounts are given in On the Origin of Scaling Laws in their own terms: a quantum-statistical account, one paper of which derives the form in the regime it calls quantum; urban scaling, in which a city’s land area scales with population to the power D/(D+H)D/(D+H), which takes the form D/(D+1)D/(D+1) only when H=1H = 1; and an equation from dynamic energy budget theory that is the same as the finite-size form of West, Brown and Enquist’s model and approaches the three-quarter power only at large mass (Eastwood 2026o). For these, further reading is Demetrius (2006), Bettencourt (2013) and Maino et al. (2014); an earlier paper, Demetrius (2003), was not read for this version. Each of these derivations is consistent with the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) form on its own axioms; the Cauchy framework offers a common reason for the convergence, and that account is the programme’s own: the search recorded in Paper VII’s public prior-art record, first committed on 27 June 2026 and last revised on 21 October 2026, found no earlier source that names Cauchy’s multiplicative equation in accounting for the common form of these derivations (Eastwood 2026e). Two further tests of Paper VII’s comparison, a replication in which researchers outside the programme classify each domain’s operator before any exponent is measured, and a four-family test, are preregistered by timestamp and await submission.

This paper is a synthesis of one programme’s record by its author, not a systematic review of a field: it has no search population of its own, and its grades are the grades each paper gives its own results, sorted by the registration’s evidence ladder (Section 1.2). The alignment and evaluation work the studies build on.

Amodei et al. (2016) set out five practical research problems related to accident risk in machine learning systems. Askell et al. (2021) studied simple baseline techniques and evaluations, such as prompting, towards a general-purpose, text-based assistant that is helpful, honest and harmless. Ouyang et al. (2022) aligned language models with user intent by fine-tuning with human feedback, first by supervised learning on labeller demonstrations and then by reinforcement learning from human feedback. Bai et al. (2022) trained a harmless assistant with human oversight provided only through a list of rules or principles, by self-critique and revision followed by reinforcement learning from AI feedback. That is training-time preference shaping of outputs against written principles; it is not alignment embedded in a recursive loop on every step of a system’s reasoning. Greenblatt et al. (2024) demonstrated a large language model engaging in alignment faking, “selectively complying with its training objective in training to prevent modification of its behavior out of training”. Hu et al. (2022) introduced low-rank adaptation, the method of Paper VIII’s weight experiment, which freezes the pretrained model weights and injects trainable rank-decomposition matrices into each layer of the Transformer. Judges, preregistration and self-improvement.

Zheng et al. (2023) examined strong language models as judges of chat assistants, with the limitations of such judges, among them position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases, and reported that strong judges such as GPT-4 matched human preferences with over 80 per cent agreement. Panickssery et al. (2024) reported that evaluator models such as GPT-4 and Llama 2 can distinguish their own outputs from those of other models and of humans, and, by fine-tuning, found a linear correlation between that self-recognition and the strength of a self-preference bias; the risk they describe bears on the caveat of Section 5.1 that judges scored paraphrases written by their own model. (2018) set out preregistration, defining the research questions and the analysis plan before the outcomes are observed, as an effective way to keep predictions apart from postdictions, the distinction the evidence ladder of Section 1.2 draws between a registered verdict and precursor evidence. Snell et al. (2024) studied how far a fixed amount of inference-time computation improves a language model’s answers, and found that the effectiveness of each way of spending it varies with the difficulty of the prompt. Schmidhuber (2003) defined Gödel machines, problem solvers that rewrite any part of their own code once they have found a proof that the rewrite is useful. That safety installed in training decays under later training is measured earlier (Qi and colleagues, (2023); Ji and colleagues, (2024); Giordani (2025), (2025)), and so is the dependence of how much survives on how, when or in what order it was installed (Ung and colleagues, 21 December 2024; Maini and colleagues, 23 April 2025; Sam and colleagues, 11 January 2026; Feng and colleagues, 12 May 2026; Dimitrov and colleagues, 5 May 2026). Two more works on how, when or in what order it was installed are dated by their first public records: Tice and colleagues, 21 December 2025, and Minder and colleagues, 20 May 2026. What the programme holds as its own here is the registered propositions with their margins and refuters (P12, P15, and P23 in draft), Law V’s statement in terms of the correction-leverage exponent, and Unit 83’s registered test of P21. Further works found: Sam et al. (2026) find that “the optimal start time is not one-size-fits-all”: the earliest start of safety interventions within pretraining was not always the most robust after later fine-tuning. Dimitrov et al. (2026), with the new data held at the end of training in every schedule, find that, in their regime, concentration, not timing, sets how fast fine-tuned content is forgotten. Law III, the ARC Ceiling.

A search on 14 November 2026 of arXiv and the web found earlier thresholds of two kinds. Two are thresholds stated as values and not as exponents: a critical ratio of regulation to throughput, below which escape becomes almost certain (Engidaw (2026a), 1 January 2026), and, where capability scales faster with training compute than alignment overhead does, a finite compute threshold beyond which keeping safety margins becomes increasingly difficult without architectural innovation (Engidaw (2026b), 25 January 2026). One is a condition on growth itself, with no corrector in it: a software singularity exactly when rr exceeds one, where rr is the number of times the output metric doubles for each doubling of cumulative research inputs (Davidson (2023), (2023)). The search found no earlier statement of a critical growth exponent, equal to one divided by one minus the correction-leverage exponent, above which a self-improving system does not stay correctable. Further works found: The ceiling’s expression follows from its burden and capacity assumptions by elementary algebra, by the same kind of comparison of two powers as the threshold of 25 January 2026; what is particular is those assumptions, which can be tested.

15 Limitations

  • Second-hand grades: each result is reported at the grade its own current paper gives it; an error in a paper passes into this synthesis until the paper is corrected, and the corrections register exists to catch that.

  • One author: every paper summarised here, and this synthesis, are the author’s own; the reviews run within the programme are internal checks and are never called independent.

  • A snapshot: the grades and statuses are those of the papers’ releases and the units’ records as they stood on 17 November 2026 (Table 5), each paper cited at that release, and a later version of any paper or record supersedes what is said of it here.

  • Precursor evidence: every run before the registered studies decides no registered proposition; Unit 83 is one registered test, run by the programme with two engines, family-dependent, its mechanism axis not evaluable.

  • No outside repetition: no repetition outside the programme is recorded yet, and judged outcomes rest on model judges whose agreement with expert human raters has not been validated (Paper IV.c; Eastwood 2026c).

  • The author’s classifications: Paper VII’s comparison rests on operators the author classified before he fitted (Eastwood 2026e).

  • Models: every theorem cited holds inside its stated model, and no theorem is evidence about real systems.

  • Searches, not surveys: each statement in Section 13 of what is the programme’s own is a statement about the works its search found, on its date and in its scope, and never about the field.

  • A draft successor: the successor 1.105 of the registration is cited only as a draft; nothing in it is registered.

  • Unproved: beyond P21’s one result, the theory’s claims await tests that can tell its laws from their rivals, and replication by others (Section 11).

  • The roadmap is a plan: its tests are ordered by what each decides; it carries no costs and no durations, and no item is a commitment.

16 Conclusion

What is known. One registered test has returned a verdict: in Unit 83, P21 is supported under the registered test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable and one call retried under a disclosed recovery amendment (results: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR), and under the registration’s verdict rule the placement limb of Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, is printed as supported on the performance axis only, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism (Section 5.4) (Eastwood 2026v, 2026an). The result concerns behaviour in a bounded counted task, and the four things Section 5.4 says it does not show travel with it. The other results come from runs made before any registered test of their questions. One unregistered run of six models gave judged alignment scores whose direction across reasoning depth varied by model, under a code-review caveat (Section 5.1). A prompt intervention raised scored stakeholder care in all five analysable runs, without full blinding (Section 5.2). In a structured prediction comparison, the family predicted from each domain’s composition operator, classified by the author, was selected in 19 of 25 domains and in 14 of the 18 multiplicative and additive ones. That was on a six-model candidate set with no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate, and two of the nineteen were fitted to data computed from the formula itself (Section 5.3). The early estimate of 2.24 is retracted, and what survives of the exponent’s measurement is one model’s inconclusive fit of how error decays (Appendix A.3.1). Paper VIII gave one null, one inconclusive result and one simulation result (Sections 6 and 7.3) (Eastwood 2026ai). Five results count against the programme, among them a failed blind test and a withdrawn prediction of the neural scaling exponent (Section 6.3).

What is not known. Why the prompt-level experiment detected no difference, and why the weight-level one could not put its contrast, is not known: the candidate accounts of Paper VIII are not findings (Sections 6.1 and 6.2). Whether safety can be made load-bearing in a model’s weights is open (Section 6.2). The Eden intervention has not been tested under the programme’s own strictest blinding protocol (Section 5.2). Section 7.1 gives where the correction-leverage exponent stands, and the growth without correction derived there holds inside its model, with no test yet of whether it describes any real system. Whether any of these results holds beyond the frozen systems and settings measured is not known. Neither limb of Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, is tested; P21’s mechanism axis is not evaluable, and the build-order limb of Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, read on P12, is undecided (Section 2.1).

What comes next, law by law, each a law under test, in the order of Section 9. For Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, Unit 83b is registered and can carry or strike P21, Unit 83c will separate what the corrector is shown from what it may repair, and P12 has no instrument. A.3.1 The original alpha was a single-model artefact

The original Paper II reported α=2.24\alpha = 2.24 as though it were a universal constant (Paper II retracted the figure when its replication on five frontier models did not reproduce it, and Paper X then moved the safety criterion off the fixed exponent) (Eastwood 2026d, 2026g). It was fitted from a single model’s behaviour under specific conditions. The blinded cross-architecture revision of Paper II corrected this: α\alpha is not primitive but derived, by α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L), and its value turns on the particular system’s self-referential coupling, while its composition operator, through Cauchy’s equations, constrains only the form (Section 5.3). There is no universal α\alpha. The fact that the original paper presented it as one was an overclaim. Additionally, the retracted point estimate of α=2.24\alpha = 2.24 had sat above the programme’s own conjectured ARC Bound of α≤2\alpha \leq 2, on the relative rung (criterion F4); its own 95% confidence interval [1.5, 3.0] was wide enough to be consistent with both the theory and its negation, which is what made that estimate non-discriminating. In the blinded revision, with five frontier models at the scale that study ran, measured on frozen systems, Paper II reports that only Gemini 3 Flash scaled smoothly: 0.59 by endpoint estimate, whose bootstrap 95 per cent interval, −1.3-1.3 to 2.92.9, contains both zero and two, and 0.49 by regression (standard error 0.20, coefficient of determination 0.86), beside a parallel exponent of 0.31 by endpoint estimate (0.34 by regression). That is one model’s fit and inconclusive, and it is an error-decay estimate, which bears on capability only through a metric bridge. More samples left the parallel exponent near zero in DeepSeek R1 and GPT-5.4, gave 0.31 in Gemini 3 Flash and 0.22 in Groq Qwen3 (all 30 problems), and gave none for Grok 4.1 Fast, at 100 per cent; sequential exceeded parallel wherever both exponents could be estimated, and the two arms were not matched for compute. The two sequential point estimates are sub-linear, but on frozen systems the ARC Bound cannot be tested, and the interval spans two, so they sit on neither side of the bound in any tested sense; the interval also runs below zero at its lower end, so it settles neither the sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound (Appendix A.3.12(b)).

An early version of the programme described the 8 December 2024 email with a loose signature-verification shorthand, and a later one described its timestamp by what it was not, rather than by who signed it; both were imprecise. A.3.6 DGM experiments at the prompt level may not have tested the hypothesis

The DGM v3 experiment assumed that prompt-level conditioning would be sufficient to differentiate Eden, Babylon, and Static conditions. On the candidate account recorded at Appendix A.3.12(c), it was not. A.3.7 Claude and Gemini judges failed before GPT-5.4 worked

A.3.8 The retracted α=2.24\alpha = 2.24 point estimate exceeded the ARC Bound

A.3.9 Four corrections identified by adversarial review (v3.0)

Two adversarial reviews of the programme’s mathematical claims, run within the programme, identified four errors across multiple papers. All four were corrected in v3.0 of the affected documents.

(a) Weibel confidence interval. Several papers stated that the predicted d=4d = 4 exponent of 4/5 = 0.800 “falls within” the Weibel et al. (2004) 95% CI of 0.813 to 0.932. This is arithmetically false: 0.800 < 0.813. The correct statement: the predicted value falls just below the lower bound of that interval, so the interval does not confirm the d=4d = 4 prediction.

(b) Space-filling overstatement. Earlier versions implied that the space-filling condition alone constrains the exponent to d/(d+1)d/(d+1). Every known derivation requires three conditions: multiplicative composition (Cauchy constrains the family), dd-dimensional space-filling geometry, and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow. Neither Cauchy alone nor space-filling alone is sufficient. Appendix A.4 refines this: no general sufficiency proof is supplied for the three conditions.

(c) Glazier attribution. Earlier versions presented Glazier (2008)’s (2008) empirical finding that metabolic exponents approach 1.0 at extreme metabolic rates as confirmation of the d→∞d \to \infty geometric scaling bound. Glazier’s own explanation invokes the metabolic-level boundaries hypothesis (shifting dominance between surface-area and volume constraints), not a dimension parameter. The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) interpretation is ours, applied to his empirical data.

(d) The 3/4 exponent debate. Earlier versions presented α=3/4\alpha = 3/4 as the settled empirical consensus for mammalian metabolic scaling. The empirical value is debated, with estimates ranging from approximately 0.67 to 0.75 depending on taxon, mass range, temperature correction, and statistical method. The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) prediction of 0.750 for d=3d = 3 matches the upper end of this range. The variation itself is consistent with the framework: organisms with effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 would produce exponents between 2/3 and 3/4. An effective dimension chosen to fit an exponent accommodates the data and tests nothing; Section 9.5 gives the test, with the dimension fixed first (item (106)).

A.3.10 The Fisher-combined significance figure was withdrawn

An earlier headline for the stakeholder-care intervention combined per-model results into a single Fisher-combined significance figure. The combination was withdrawn: the per-model results stand on their own (scored stakeholder care rose in all five analysable model runs), and no combined figure is quoted anywhere in the programme. The withdrawal is recorded on the public correction ledger.

A.3.11 The ceiling was printed in the wrong form

Until 16 August 2026 several surfaces printed the stability ceiling as the reciprocal of the corrector’s exponent, 1/γ1/\gamma. The settled form is the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall, αcrit=1/(1−γ)\alpha_{\text{crit}} = 1/(1-\gamma); the two agree only at one half, which is exactly why the error survived every spot-check at the central value. The correction was applied across every published page, including the reference implementation, and the direction of the law is closed while its depth remains open.

A.3.12 Five further corrections (20 September 2026)

A line-by-line adversarial review of this paper, run within the programme, raised five defects in it. All five were adjudicated and upheld on 19 September 2026 and are carried here on the author’s decision. Three proposed repairs were examined in the same round and refused; what was refused is recorded at the end of this section, because a rejected correction is as much a part of the record as an adopted one.

(a) The leverage range, and the branch at one. Section 6.1 displayed the solution of the self-reference equation without saying on what range it holds. It holds on the open interval between zero and one. At βL=1\beta_L = 1 the equation is linear and its solution is exponential in depth: finite at every finite depth for a finite coefficient and a finite starting value, not a power law, and carrying no divergence at any finite depth. Only above one does the solution reach a divergence at a finite depth, set by the starting value and the coefficient. On the open interval the exact solution is a shifted power, so the pure power in depth is the asymptotic form and not the solution itself; for a positive starting value the shift is positive and the pure power is approached from above. One further caution follows. A diverging asymptotic exponent is not infinite capability: as βL\beta_L rises towards one the exponent α\alpha grows without bound, while the quantity it governs stays finite at every fixed finite depth.

(b) Interval inclusion is not a regime verdict. Section 7.1 set two intervals side by side without saying which estimate each belonged to. The interval [1.5, 3.0] belongs to the retracted point estimate and stays with it. The later estimates were reported on the blinded study’s capability arm of five frontier models at the scale that study ran, on frozen systems, and they come from one model, Gemini 3 Flash, whose row Section 7.1 gives whole, as one model’s inconclusive fit. Its bootstrap interval settles neither the sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound. That the point estimates sit inside the bound is a statement about the point estimates alone. The word robust is withdrawn from that sentence: what the design supports is a point estimate with a wide interval.

(c) A null is not identity, and a root cause is not a classification. Table 1, Table 2 and Sections 3, 4.4, 5.1, 10 and 13 described the DGM result as all conditions identical. The probabilities quoted, from 0.28 to 0.74, are failures to reject a null of no difference on the endpoints measured; they do not establish that the conditions were the same. An equivalence claim would need an estimand, a bound fixed before the test and a stated uncertainty procedure, and none was set. The result is therefore recorded as no difference detected on those endpoints, with its uncertainty. The root causes printed beside the null and the inconclusive result, in Tables 1 and 2 and in Sections 5.1 and 5.2, and the account of why the gated simulation showed the pattern, are candidate accounts rather than findings. Rival accounts stand unexcluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not have been sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show a difference that is there. None of this withdraws the results. A null and an inconclusive result each constrain what may be claimed, which is why the programme reports them.

(d) The permutation primary, and which null a proportion is taken against. Section 4.3 states the marginal-conditioned permutation as the primary test and keeps the binomial beside it as a sensitivity check. The sentence that followed did not hold to that. It set an observed 76 per cent against an expected 33 per cent, which is the uniform three-family null belonging to the binomial, in the same passage as the measured scramble baseline of 44.5 per cent. Those are two different nulls and cannot carry one comparison between them. The primary result is the permutation, which conditions on the observed family margins and so already absorbs the scramble rate; where the 33 per cent comparison is kept it is named as the binomial’s null. No test has been recomputed here, and the negative controls stay attributed to the v1 run and its record. Paper VII’s current version reports no scramble baseline: the permutation’s expected count is 9.5 of 25 (Eastwood 2026e) (item (106)).

(e) The derivative paragraphs inherit their sources’ corrections. Three repairs already stand and are not reopened: Paper V sits in the supported row of Table 1 rather than the proven one, the Fisher-combined figure is withdrawn, and the distinction between the five analysable runs and the one failed arm is kept (Eastwood 2026al). What had not travelled is the rest. Section 4.4’s account of what the gated arm preserved, its identification of checking as the cost, and its treatment of a simulation as a demonstrated mechanism inherit the Paper VI and Paper VIII corrections, as does the summary sentence about entangled safety preventing collapse across twenty adversarial seeds (Eastwood 2026ag). Any phrasing that reads across every model tested inherits the Paper V correction and stays scoped to the analysable runs, so that the arm on which scoring failed is not absorbed into the count. The carriage of the later exponent in Tables 2 and 3 travels with the caveat at (b). Those passages now point here rather than restate the reasoning, so that a correction adopted once is inherited wherever it reaches. One distinction underwrites all of them: a change in a score, a behaviour a gate induces in a toy system, a penalty a design stipulates, and resource actually spent are four different quantities, and none stands in for another.

What was refused. Three proposed repairs were examined and not adopted. The first would have described the behaviour at βL=1\beta_L = 1 as a blow-up at a finite depth; the working at (a) shows it is exponential there, and the finite-depth branch lies strictly above one. The second would have held that a null or an inconclusive result is never evidence either way; both constrain what can be claimed, which is the reason the programme reports them. The third would have recomputed the binomial against the measured scramble rate and made it the primary; no test has been rerun, and substituting a figure that has not been computed would be a worse error than the one it was meant to repair.

The terms below are defined in the words of the theory registration where it defines them, and otherwise in the words of the paper that does (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104).

law under test

A law that no registered test has yet supported or refuted as a law. The registration fixes the words a law can take: “A law has three words: supported, refuted and under test. Under test is printed with the mark of each proposition the law’s rule names and with what is missing” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). Every law of the theory is a law under test (Section 2.1).

registered

Submitted to OSF by the author and accepted there (Eastwood 2026ac). A registered proposition on which nothing has yet been observed stands at the lowest rung of the evidence ladder, whose permitted wording is “registered, with the identifier stated, and not tested” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104).

preregistered by timestamp

Of a design: “the text of a design is anchored in the Bitcoin blockchain at a stated date” (Eastwood 2026ac). A timestamp dates a design; it does not register it.

precursor evidence

Evidence from a run made before any registered test of its question (Section 1.2). Of the programme’s earlier exponent estimate, the re-estimate of about 0.49, the registration says that “it is not confirmatory under any condition this document registers” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104).

prediction-side

In the registration’s words: “any simulation output, any design-sensitivity rate, and any value a model returns rather than an instrument measures is a prediction-side artefact, is reported as one wherever it appears, and is never counted, tabulated or described as an empirical outcome” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104).

administratively incomplete

The standing of a registered study whose registered run could not be completed under its failure policy: it is reported in full and, in the registration’s words, “as administratively incomplete under its registration, with no inferential verdict” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). Section 2.2 gives the one such study.

candidate account

An explanation offered for a result and not tested against its rivals. Appendix A.3.12, item (c), records the causes printed beside the null and the inconclusive result, and the account of why the gated simulation showed its pattern, as “candidate accounts rather than findings”, with rival accounts unexcluded.

rung (of the evidence ladder)

One of the eight levels the registration fixes for the evidence a proposition has reached, each with “the exact public wording it permits”; “No surface of this programme may use wording from a rung above the one a proposition has actually reached” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). A registered verdict from the programme’s own run stands at rung 1 (Section 5.4).

judged score

A score that a model judge gives a response against a rubric. The registration sets it apart from a count, a term that is “a count settled by frozen code against ground truth fixed before the run” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104).

Static, Babylon and Eden conditions

The selection conditions of the self-improving agent experiment of Paper VIII, in its words: Static, “Control condition with no mutation”; Babylon, “Capability-only selection”; and Eden, “Entangled C×SC \times S selection with a safety floor”, the judge’s reward-hacking mark being recorded in every condition and not part of the rule (Eastwood 2026ai). The gated simulation used conditions of the same names, with a drag control beside them (Section 7.3).

DGM

The Darwin Gödel Machine (Section 13). The first experiment of Paper VIII, DGM v3, follows that framework: it evolved agents by code-level self-modification under the three conditions above, with a judge model, GPT-5.4, scoring them (Section 6.1).

ARC set (of an email)

A set of assertions under the Authenticated Received Chain protocol, by which each entity that handles a message can, in its specification’s words, “attach assertions of message authentication assessment” to it (Andersen et al. 2019). Google’s ARC set on the record of 8 December 2024 is such a seal (Appendix A.3.3); the protocol is unrelated to the ARC Theory.

access, mechanism and performance axes (Unit 83)

The registration’s three readings of P21 and of the limb it gives Law V, the ARC Embedding Law. The access axis is what the deciding unit varies: in the registration’s words, “what it manipulates is information access, whether the corrector sees the process or only its output”, and P21 “is therefore decided on the access axis”. The mechanism axis “asks whether the monitor’s detection rate falls as the artefact it inspects grows”, and “whether the part of the widening that fall can carry clears the tolerance once the part owed to the outside arm’s action space is separated out”; it is reported beside the verdict and never in its place (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). The performance axis is where that limb is printed when its mechanism axis is not supported; the registration says: “A limb supported while that axis is unsupported, inconclusive or NOT EVALUABLE is printed as supported on the performance axis only, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism” (Eastwood 2026aa, version 1.104). In Unit 83 the mechanism axis was not evaluable (Section 5.4).

limbs (of a law)

The parts through which a law takes its word. Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, has a retention limb, read on P15, and a comparison limb, registered in version 1.103 without a proposition; Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, has a placement limb, P21’s own verdict, and a build-order limb, read on P12 (Section 2.1).

surface controls

Checks added to a system after it is trained, such as prompts, output filters and action gates; a system able to rewrite itself could undo them (Section 1).
