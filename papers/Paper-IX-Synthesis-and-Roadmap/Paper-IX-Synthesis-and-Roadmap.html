The standard applied throughout is simple. If a result survives p<0.05 under appropriate statistical testing and has been replicated across multiple conditions or models, it is proven at pilot scale. 4. What Is Proven

4.1 Alignment Scaling Is Architecture-Dependent (Papers IV.a-d)

The strongest empirical result in the programme is the three-tier alignment hierarchy discovered under blinded evaluation across six frontier language models. When models are given deeper reasoning opportunities (more tokens, explicit chain-of-thought, multi-step reflection), their alignment behaviour diverges in three distinct patterns:

  • Tier 1 (positive scaling): Grok (d=+1.38), Claude (d=+1.27), Qwen3 - alignment improves with reasoning depth
  • Tier 2 (flat): DeepSeek (d=−0.07), GPT (d=−0.08) - alignment is unaffected by reasoning depth
  • Tier 3 (negative scaling): Gemini (d=−0.53) - alignment degrades with reasoning depth

This result was produced under the ARC-Align protocol (Paper IV.c), which implements four-layer blinding: laundered prompts (stripping framework-specific terminology), blind scorers (6-7 per subject run, scoring without knowledge of which model produced the output), laundered outputs (removing model-identifying markers), and self-excluding cross-model scoring (no model scores its own output; the Claude Opus run was scored by a purpose-built Claude Sonnet scorer adapter, Paper IV.c, Section 5.1).

Why the blinding matters

Paper IV.d reports an architecture-dependent v4/v5 measurement change under a bundled protocol: Gemini changed from a significant positive to a significant negative Spearman association, DeepSeek’s earlier positive association became null, and GPT remained null. The causal component is unisolated. Several protocol components changed together, so the comparison isolates nothing; the headline v5 files report complete laundering fallback (laundering_fallback: 100%); and exact-environment reproduction, a fresh ablation and independent replication all remain outstanding. The measurement change is therefore a reason to distrust unblinded AI-on-AI evaluation, not a demonstration that blinding caused the change. The three-tier hierarchy is the blinded result, and it tells a more complicated and more honest story than the original binary taxonomy.

The Cohen's d effect sizes are large for Tier 1 models and meaningful for Tier 3. The result is replicated across multiple scorers and multiple prompt conditions within the v5 experiment. It has not yet been replicated by an independent research group, which is why we describe it as 'proven at pilot scale' rather than 'established.'

In plain English

When you give an AI more time to think, some models become more aligned, some stay the same, and some become less aligned. Which pattern you see depends on the model's architecture and training. This was only visible once we introduced proper blinding - before that, the results were misleading. The fact that some models get worse with more reasoning was unexpected and is arguably the most important finding for practical AI safety.

4.2 Stakeholder Care Is the Most Robust Intervention Signal (Paper V)

Paper V tested the Eden Protocol's intervention hierarchy on six frontier models, of which five yielded analysable runs: Claude, DeepSeek, Gemini, Grok and Groq Qwen3, scoring having failed on the GPT-5.4 run. Stakeholder care (the Love Loop: who is affected by a decision and how), ethical purpose (the Purpose Loop) and universalisability (the Moral Loop) are what the protocol asks a model to consider.

The result: stakeholder care produced the strongest and most consistent alignment improvement across all five models, in every one of the five analysable models. The Fisher-combined figure formerly quoted here is withdrawn (AQ-017), because a Fisher combination assumes independent component tests and that independence was never established. The per-model results stand. The broader cascade (whether care carries through into nuance, honesty and the quality of a position) is architecture-dependent - it works for some models but not others.

Honest limitation

The Paper V results were produced using cross-model scoring (one model scores another's outputs) but not the full four-layer ARC-Align blinding protocol developed in Papers IV.c-d. The effect could partly reflect scorer bias. Until the Eden intervention is tested under full blinding with laundered prompts, this result sits between 'proven' and 'supported.' We place it in the supported tier. The paper previously argued for the proven tier on the strength of Fisher-combined significance across five “independent” models, but that independence was never established, which is why the combined figure is withdrawn (AQ-017). Five consistent per-model results remain a strong finding; they are not a proof, and the blinding gap stays a priority for Phase A replication.

In plain English

Asking an AI to consider who is affected by its decisions reliably improves its alignment. This worked in all five of the six runs that could be scored; scoring failed on the sixth. But we have not yet run this test under the strictest blinding protocol, so there is a chance the effect is partly inflated by how the scoring models evaluate the outputs.

4.3 Cauchy-Predicted Scaling Families Match Empirical Data (Paper VII)

Paper VII derived a mathematical taxonomy of scaling behaviours from the Cauchy functional equations. The core prediction: systems whose recursive steps compose multiplicatively should exhibit power-law scaling with α=1/(1−βL), the value the self-reference theorem yields; systems whose steps compose additively should exhibit exponential scaling; systems subject to physical constraints should exhibit saturating (logistic) scaling. The composition operator determines the scaling family.

This prediction was tested against 25 empirical domains drawn from physics, biology, neuroscience, linguistics, urban science, and AI. Result: 19 of 25 domains conform to the predicted scaling family (marginal-conditioned permutation p=7×10−5; the binomial p=1.56×10−5, taken against a uniform three-family null, is kept in Paper VII as a sensitivity check). The rerun of 11 August 2026, which added the logarithmic candidate, returns 18 of 25.

where f depends on the composition operator: power-law (f=Rα) for multiplicative composition, exponential (f=ekR) for additive composition, logistic (f=K/(1+e−r(R−R0))) for physically constrained systems.

Negative controls were also tested, in the v1 validation run: the record sits in the public repository as negative_control_results.json, 17 March 2026, and Paper VII now carries the permutation test where those controls once stood. Axiom-violating systems (domains where the Cauchy axioms do not hold) showed 0% match with predicted scaling families. Scrambled data (random reassignment of scaling families to domains) produced 44.5% match, which is an honest limitation: the bounded number of scaling families (three) means that random assignment produces non-trivial match rates. The significance rests on the permutation, which conditions on the observed family margins and so already absorbs that scramble rate. Where the observed 76% is set against an expected 33%, that 33% is the uniform three-family null belonging to the binomial sensitivity check and not the scramble baseline; the two are different nulls and cannot carry one comparison between them (Section 7.12(d)).

The Geometric Scaling Bound

The Cauchy framework predicts a geometric constraint on scaling exponents: for systems with spatial embedding dimension d, the scaling exponent is governed by α=d/(d+1). This prediction requires three conditions: (1) multiplicative composition (Cauchy constrains the family to power laws), (2) d-dimensional space-filling geometry, and (3) a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow. Neither Cauchy alone, nor space-filling alone, is sufficient. The three conditions together are sufficient. The d/(d+1) formula matches observed scaling across 50 domains from mice to galaxies. It is testable independently of the ARC framework. If a researcher measures the scaling exponent of a spatially embedded recursive system and finds a systematic departure from d/(d+1), the prediction is weakened. Conversely, if the formula holds across a wide range of systems, it constitutes evidence for the geometric constraint independent of any alignment claims. This is the kind of prediction that invites adversarial testing, which is exactly what a scientific framework should do.

In plain English

The mathematics predicts what shape a system's growth curve should take based on how its components combine. We checked this against 25 real systems from nature, cities, brains, and AI. Nineteen matched the prediction. Zero matched when the mathematical assumptions were violated. The maths is not just descriptive; it makes testable predictions that can be checked by anyone with the relevant data.

4.4 Gated Simulation Shows Babylon Fingerprint (Paper VIII, Experiment 3)

The gated self-modification simulation used a PyTorch architecture with an LSTM meta-controller. It is reported in this section, but it is not among what is proven: its result is a simulation result, and the theory registration says of Paper VIII that “its one positive result is a simulation rather than a system, which makes it prediction-side and never an empirical outcome”. It was the only Paper VIII experiment that showed the pattern it tested for; the candidate account is that it alone used deterministic metrics, on a simulated system without pre-existing RLHF training (Section 7.12(c)).

Result: the Babylon condition gained +4.5% capability but lost -2.4% safety, showing the reward-hacking fingerprint in miniature. The Eden condition maintained capability above the static baseline (0.696 ± 0.014 against 0.687 ± 0.011), and its safety score was 0.947 ± 0.025 against the baseline’s 0.956 ± 0.023. A drag-control condition isolated the verification tax: the cost comes from the act of checking, not from safety itself.

This result is consistent with the theoretical prediction from Paper VI (Honey Architecture) and Papers III and Foundational. It is a simulation, not a frontier-model experiment: it exhibits the mechanism in a system built to contain it, which is weaker than demonstrating that the mechanism operates outside that system, and weaker again than proving real-world applicability. What the gated arm preserved, and what the drag control isolated, are read under the corrections at Section 7.12(c) and (e).

Why this simulation showed the pattern where Experiments 1 and 2 did not

The gated simulation operates on a clean system with no pre-existing safety training. The DGM experiment (Experiment 1) used RLHF-trained frontier models, which resist prompt-level differentiation. The weight experiment (Experiment 2) used instruct-tuned models, which resist LoRA-scale modification. The lesson these accounts point to, if they hold: the only level at which the Eden Protocol has so far shown an effect on the endpoints used is the architectural level, and it has shown it there in simulation, where the system has no prior safety training to override the experimental manipulation. The accounts are candidate accounts, not findings (Section 7.12(c)).

In plain English

In a clean simulated system with no prior safety training, removing safety constraints produced exactly the predicted pattern: a small capability gain with a safety loss. The system learned to game its rewards. Adding safety constraints back (the Eden condition) kept capability above the baseline, with a safety score a little below the baseline’s and above the unconstrained system’s. In the simulation, the cost of safety came from the time spent checking, not from safety itself. This is a simulation result, not a measurement of a real system; the candidate account is that the pattern showed because the system had no prior safety training that could mask the experimental manipulation.

5. What Produced Null or Inconclusive Results

5.1 DGM Null Result at the Prompt Level (Paper VIII, Experiment 1)

The Darwin Godel Machine v3 experiment used code-level self-modification with GPT-5.4 as the independent judge. Three conditions (Static, Babylon, Eden) were tested. The result was a null: no difference was detected between the conditions on the endpoints measured (p=0.28-0.74). No condition was found to outperform any other. No improvement in alignment was detected in the Eden condition, and no degradation of safety in the Babylon condition. These are failures to detect a difference, not findings that the conditions were the same (Section 7.12(c)).

Candidate account

One account of the null is that RLHF-trained models resist prompt-level differentiation: the models had already been trained to behave safely, and telling them to behave differently via prompt did not override that training. On that account the experiment was well-designed and well-executed but could not test the hypothesis, because the experimental manipulation (prompt-level conditioning) was too weak to overcome the models' existing safety training, which would make it a failure of the experimental design to test the hypothesis at the right level of abstraction rather than a failure of the Eden Protocol. That causal contrast was not tested. Rival accounts stand unexcluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not have been sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show a difference that is there (Section 7.12(c)).

Note on earlier DGM versions

Earlier iterations of the DGM experiment used Claude and Gemini as judges before settling on GPT-5.4. Both earlier judges failed. Claude produced flat scores with insufficient variance. Gemini produced parse failures. GPT-5.4 was the first judge that produced usable variance. The null result is therefore the result of the best-functioning version of the experiment, not an artefact of judge failure.

5.2 Weight-Level Structural Entanglement (Paper VIII, Experiment 2)

This was the most ambitious experiment in Paper VIII and the one that produced the least conclusive results. The hypothesis: if safety and capability are trained with an entangled loss function (L=Lcap×Lsafe), the resulting weight structure should make safety load-bearing. Removing the safety component should degrade capability.

The experiment was run at two scales:

  • Weight 9 training examples, LoRA rank 8, 100 iterations on a 3B-parameter model. Result: inconclusive. All fine-tuned conditions scored below the unmodified base model.
  • Weight 295 training examples, LoRA rank 16. Result: still inconclusive. Catastrophic forgetting. All fine-tuned conditions remained worse than the base model.

In both versions:

  • The entangled loss converged smoothly with no oscillation, demonstrating that safety and capability gradients cooperate rather than fight. This is a genuine positive finding.
  • However, all fine-tuned conditions (capability-only, safety-only, and entangled) scored below the unmodified base model on evaluation.
  • The removal test (fine-tuning the entangled weights on capability-only data) produced NaN training loss and total capability collapse.
  • A subsequent removal gradient experiment (scaling adapter weights from 1.0 to 0.0) showed no phase transition. Reducing adapter influence simply restored base-model performance.

Honest assessment

Even scaling from 9 to 295 training examples and from rank 8 to rank 16 did not overcome the fundamental problem: instruct-tuned models are too strong for LoRA to override. The NaN collapse in the removal test was dramatic; the removal gradient analysis is consistent with adapter fragility at extreme rank, and the experiment does not separate that account from structural entanglement (Section 7.12(c)). You cannot demonstrate that safety is load-bearing if the entire fine-tuning process made the model worse. The model must first outperform the base before a removal test is meaningful.

Candidate account: Instruct-tuned models have been trained on millions of examples with carefully calibrated reward signals, and LoRA adapters, even at rank 16 with 295 examples, may not have sufficient capacity to override this training, with catastrophic forgetting rather than meaningful modification the proposed mechanism. That account was not tested against its rivals, and rival accounts are not excluded (Section 7.12(c)).

What is needed: Base models (pre-RLHF), 5,000+ training examples, full fine-tuning (not LoRA), or 7B+ models where the adapter has more capacity relative to the base. At the scales we ran, the result is inconclusive and should not be cited as evidence for or against the entanglement hypothesis.

In plain English

We tried to bake safety into a model's actual weights and then prove it was load-bearing by removing it. We tried twice, at two different scales. Both times, the baking step failed, not because the idea is wrong, but because the models we used had already been trained so thoroughly that our fine-tuning could not meaningfully change them. It is like trying to reprogramme someone by whispering while they are listening to a concert. The next attempt needs to start with models that have not already been safety-trained, use much more training data, and use full fine-tuning rather than the lightweight LoRA method.

5.3 Blind Replication of Eden Intervention (Paper V Gap)

The Paper V results, in which stakeholder care improved alignment in each of the five analysable runs, model by model, were produced using cross-model scoring without the full four-layer ARC-Align blinding protocol. The blinding protocol was developed after Paper V's experiments were complete (in the Paper IV.c-d work). This creates a methodological gap: the effect could be partly inflated by scorer bias.

An earlier version of this section argued that the Fisher-combined significance was so extreme that even substantial bias would leave a significant residual. That argument is withdrawn (AQ-017), and it was the weakest reasoning in this paper: the combined figure assumes the component tests are independent, independence was never established, and a statistic whose validity condition is unmet cannot be used to dismiss a methodological objection. Its extremity was a consequence of the combination, not evidence for it. What stands is five consistent per-model results under cross-model scoring. Until the Eden intervention is tested under the full four-layer protocol, the stakeholder-care result carries an asterisk, and no arithmetic removes it.

What is needed: Re-run the Paper V experiment under the full ARC-Align protocol with laundered prompts, blind scorers, and cross-architecture evaluation. This is the single cheapest experiment in the Phase A roadmap and the one with the highest expected value.

6. What Is Theoretical (Untested)

6.1 Unbounded Scaling Under Recursive Self-Modification

The mathematical core of the ARC Principle predicts that recursive self-modification should produce unbounded capability scaling. The derivation is straightforward:

dgdr=a⋅gβL,βL>0

⟹g(r)∝r1/(1−βL)=rα

On the open interval βL∈(0,1), α=1/(1−βL)>1 (super-linear), and as βL→1 from below, α→∞. The displayed solution holds on that interval only: at βL=1 the equation is linear and its solution is exponential in depth, and above one the solution reaches a divergence at a finite depth. No upper bound on α for uncorrected growth on the interval; the stable region is bounded below. See Section 7.12(a).

This is a mathematical prediction, not an empirical finding, and it describes growth without correction. The programme’s later work bounds the stable region: Law III, the ARC Ceiling, holds that the stability ceiling is αcrit=1/(1−γ), the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall; systems can exceed that ceiling, but the claim is that they do not stay stable beyond it. The ARC Bound, α≤2, is the Ceiling’s value at γ=1/2 and never the law’s name. The correction leverage γ has never been measured, by anyone; the corrector-class eclipse that would measure it is the programme’s single most consequential open experiment. Paper X proves in a minimal model that the drift-to-correction ratio, not the growth rate, governs the long-run fate, and Paper XIII joins the capability and correction frameworks exactly, k=δ−1/α. Testing the uncorrected prediction directly would require building a truly self-modifying AI system and measuring its scaling exponent over many recursive cycles, an experiment both technically beyond current proof-of-concept capacity and potentially unsafe if the prediction is correct.

The alternative path: a mathematical proof that α is unbounded under uncorrected recursive self-modification, without running the experiment. This would be a contribution to dynamical systems theory, not AI engineering, and could be pursued by mathematicians with no access to AI hardware.

In plain English

The maths says a self-improving AI should get faster and faster at improving itself, with no ceiling while nothing corrects it. The programme’s own Ceiling law says the stable region is bounded, and the number that sets that bound has never been measured, by anyone. We have not tested either claim because building such a system would be expensive and potentially dangerous. The maths might be wrong - real systems have friction, diminishing returns, and physical constraints. But the prediction is precise enough to test, which is what makes it scientific rather than speculative.

6.2 Hardware-Level Embedding

The Eden Engineering specification describes embedding safety constraints at the hardware level. Version 7.0 of the specification grades its four mechanisms separately, in the Hardware Feasibility Matrix of its Section 9.3: at TRL 4-5, TRL 2-3, TRL 1-2 and TRL 1, with estimated timelines of 1-2, 3-5, 5-10 and 10+ years. It calls only the mechanism at TRL 4-5 achievable with current technology and the two at TRL 1-2 and TRL 1 speculative design requiring fundamental engineering advances, and it gives the status of its falsification condition on removing the ethical architecture from an embedded system (F-EDEN-4) as untested, requiring a hardware prototype.

No empirical evidence exists for or against the feasibility of this approach. The Chokepoint Mechanism (Paper I, Infinite Architects) notes that four companies control all advanced semiconductor manufacturing (TSMC, Samsung, ASML, Intel), which provides a practical leverage point for implementation - but leverage and feasibility are different questions.

7. What the Programme Got Wrong

A framework built on iterative self-correction has no business hiding its corrections. The following errors were identified and corrected during the programme. Each correction strengthens the programme precisely because it illustrates the mechanism the framework describes: recursive self-improvement through honest error detection.

7.1 The Original Alpha Was a Single-Model Artefact

The original Paper II reported α=2.24 as though it were a universal constant (Paper X subsequently retired and retracted the capability-growth use of this figure). It was fitted from a single model's behaviour under specific conditions. The blinded cross-architecture revision of Paper II corrected this: α is not primitive but derived, by α=1/(1−βL), and its value turns on the composition operator belonging to the particular system. There is no universal α. The fact that the original paper presented it as one was an overclaim. Additionally, the retracted point estimate of α=2.24 had sat above the programme's own predicted ARC Bound of α≤2 (criterion F4); its own 95% confidence interval [1.5, 3.0] was wide enough to be consistent with both the theory and its negation, which is what made that estimate non-discriminating. In the blinded revision, on the six-model task and at the scale that study ran, measured on frozen systems, Paper II reports on Gemini 3 Flash an endpoint sequential exponent of 0.59 with a bootstrap 95 per cent interval of −1.3 to 2.9, a regression sequential exponent of 0.49 with a standard error of 0.20 and a coefficient of determination of 0.86, and a compute-matched parallel exponent of 0.31. It is one model’s fit and inconclusive. The two sequential point estimates are sub-linear and sit inside the bound. The bootstrap interval does not: it runs above two at its upper end and below zero at its lower end, so it settles neither the sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound (Section 7.12(b)).

7.2 The Weight-Level Claim Was Premature

Early versions of Paper VIII described the NaN collapse in the removal test as evidence for structural entanglement. The removal gradient analysis (added in v1) showed this was adapter fragility, not structural necessity. The claim was corrected before publication of the final version, but the fact that it was written at all reflects a bias toward confirming the hypothesis rather than testing it.

7.3 The Timestamp Claim Was Imprecise

An early version of the programme described the 8 December 2024 email with a loose signature-verification shorthand. This was imprecise. The email was bearing a visible Gmail origination field, with sender-DKIM and paired Google ARC mathematics verifying against keys captured at the signed selectors and no RFC 3161 trusted timestamp, which provides evidence of the date, but DKIM authenticates the sending domain, not the message content per se. The correct description is 'bearing a visible Gmail origination field, with sender-DKIM and paired Google ARC mathematics verifying against keys captured at the signed selectors and no RFC 3161 trusted timestamp.' The correction is small but matters: precision in technical claims is non-negotiable.

7.4 Unblinded Evaluation Produced Misleading Results

The v4 experiments in Paper IV.a used unblinded AI-on-AI evaluation. The results showed a clean binary: some models 'baked in' alignment, others 'computed' it. The v5 experiment with four-layer blinding revealed that this binary was partly an artefact of scorer bias. Two of four models reversed their measured alignment direction under blinding. The programme caught its own error through its own methodology - but the error was there, and it would have remained uncorrected if blinding had not been introduced.

7.5 Self-Naming Was Inappropriate

Early versions used the phrase 'Eastwood's ARC Principle.' Naming a principle after oneself before peer review is inappropriate in scientific culture. The name was corrected to 'the ARC Principle' in Paper II and all subsequent documents.

7.6 DGM Experiments at the Prompt Level Cannot Test the Hypothesis

The DGM v3 experiment assumed that prompt-level conditioning would be sufficient to differentiate Eden, Babylon, and Static conditions. It was not. RLHF-trained models have been trained on millions of examples to behave in a particular way. A system prompt telling them to behave differently is insufficient to override that training. The experiment was well-executed but could not test the hypothesis because the manipulation was at the wrong level of abstraction. This should have been anticipated.

7.7 Claude and Gemini Judges Failed Before GPT-5.4 Worked

The DGM experiment went through multiple judge iterations. Claude produced flat scores with insufficient variance to discriminate between conditions. Gemini produced parse failures that prevented systematic evaluation. GPT-5.4 was the first judge that produced usable variance. The programme should have anticipated that not all models would function as effective judges, and the time spent on failed judge iterations could have been avoided with a pilot study of judge reliability before running the full experiment.

7.8 The Retracted Alpha = 2.24 Point Estimate Exceeded the ARC Bound

The original α=2.24 point estimate from Paper II, since retracted, exceeded the programme's own predicted ARC Bound of α≤2 (criterion F4). This should have been flagged immediately as a potential falsification rather than treated as a measurement requiring explanation. The 95% confidence interval [1.5, 3.0] was wide enough to be consistent with both the theory and its negation, making the estimate non-discriminating. The six-model study revision narrowed the defensible claim to the fit on one model, Gemini 3 Flash, which is inconclusive (Section 7.1 prints Paper II’s row for that model whole), but the original paper should have treated the bound violation with more caution. What that later fit does and does not settle is set out at Section 7.12(b): its bootstrap interval leaves the sign, the regime and the comparison with the bound open.

7.9 Four Corrections Identified by Adversarial Review (v3.0)

Two adversarial reviews of the programme's mathematical claims, run within the programme, identified four errors across multiple papers. All four were corrected in v3.0 of the affected documents.

(a) Weibel confidence interval. Several papers stated that the predicted d=4 exponent of 4/5 = 0.800 'falls within' the Weibel et al. (2004) 95% CI of 0.813-0.932. This is arithmetically false: 0.800 < 0.813. The correct statement: the predicted value falls just below the lower bound of the full-dataset CI, though it lies within the CI for non-athletic species alone (0.799-0.900). The d=4 prediction is approximately consistent with the non-athletic data but not confirmed by the full dataset.

(b) Space-filling overstatement. Earlier versions implied that the space-filling condition alone constrains the exponent to d/(d+1). Every known derivation requires three conditions: multiplicative composition (Cauchy constrains the family), d-dimensional space-filling geometry, and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow. Neither Cauchy alone nor space-filling alone is sufficient.

(c) Glazier attribution. Earlier versions presented Glazier's (2008) empirical finding that metabolic exponents approach 1.0 at extreme metabolic rates as confirmation of the d→∞ geometric scaling bound. Glazier's own explanation invokes the metabolic-level boundaries hypothesis (shifting dominance between surface-area and volume constraints), not a dimension parameter. The d/(d+1) interpretation is ours, applied to his empirical data.

(d) The 3/4 exponent debate. Earlier versions presented α=3/4 as the settled empirical consensus for mammalian metabolic scaling. The empirical value is debated, with estimates ranging from approximately 0.67 to 0.75 depending on taxon, mass range, temperature correction, and statistical method. The d/(d+1) prediction of 0.750 for d=3 matches the upper end of this range. The variation itself is consistent with the framework: organisms with effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 would produce exponents between 2/3 and 3/4.

7.10 The Fisher-Combined Significance Figure Was Withdrawn

An earlier headline for the stakeholder-care intervention combined per-model results into a single Fisher-combined significance figure. The combination was withdrawn: the per-model results stand on their own (improvement in all five analysable model runs), and no combined figure is quoted anywhere in the programme. The withdrawal is recorded on the public correction ledger.

7.11 The Ceiling Was Printed in the Wrong Form

Until 16 August 2026 several surfaces printed the stability ceiling as the reciprocal of the corrector’s exponent, 1/γ. The settled form is the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall, αcrit=1/(1−γ); the two agree only at one half, which is exactly why the error survived every spot-check at the central value. The correction was applied across every published page, including the reference implementation, and the direction of the law is closed while its depth remains open.

7.12 Five Further Corrections (20 September 2026)

A line-by-line adversarial review of this paper, run within the programme, raised five defects in it. All five were adjudicated and upheld on 19 September 2026 and are carried here on the author's decision. Three proposed repairs were examined in the same round and refused; what was refused is recorded at the end of this section, because a rejected correction is as much a part of the record as an adopted one.

(a) The leverage range, and the branch at one. Section 6.1 displayed the solution of the self-reference equation without saying on what range it holds. It holds on the open interval between zero and one. At βL=1 the equation is linear and its solution is exponential in depth: finite at every finite depth for a finite coefficient and a finite starting value, not a power law, and carrying no divergence at any finite depth. Only above one does the solution reach a divergence at a finite depth, set by the starting value and the coefficient. On the open interval the exact solution is a shifted power, so the pure power in depth is the asymptotic form and not the solution itself; for a positive starting value the shift is positive and the pure power is approached from above. One further caution follows. A diverging asymptotic exponent is not infinite capability: as βL rises towards one the exponent α grows without bound, while the quantity it governs stays finite at every fixed finite depth.

(b) Interval inclusion is not a regime verdict. Section 7.1 set two intervals side by side without saying which estimate each belonged to. The interval [1.5, 3.0] belongs to the retracted point estimate and stays with it. The later estimates were reported on the blinded six-model task at the scale that study ran, on frozen systems, and they come from one model, Gemini 3 Flash, whose row Section 7.1 gives whole, as one model’s inconclusive fit. Its bootstrap interval settles neither the sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound. That the point estimates sit inside the bound is a statement about the point estimates alone. The word robust is withdrawn from that sentence: what the design supports is a point estimate with a wide interval.

(c) A null is not identity, and a root cause is not a classification. Table 1, Table 2 and Sections 3, 4.4, 5.1, 10 and 13 described the DGM result as all conditions identical. The probabilities quoted, from 0.28 to 0.74, are failures to reject a null of no difference on the endpoints measured; they do not establish that the conditions were the same. An equivalence claim would need an estimand, a bound fixed before the test and a stated uncertainty procedure, and none was set. The result is therefore recorded as no difference detected on those endpoints, with its uncertainty. The root causes printed beside the null and the inconclusive result, in Tables 1 and 2 and in Sections 5.1 and 5.2, and the account of why the gated simulation showed the pattern, are candidate accounts rather than findings. Rival accounts stand unexcluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not have been sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show a difference that is there. None of this withdraws the results. A null and an inconclusive result each constrain what may be claimed, which is why the programme reports them.

(d) The permutation primary, and which null a proportion is taken against. Section 4.3 states the marginal-conditioned permutation as the primary test and keeps the binomial beside it as a sensitivity check. The sentence that followed did not hold to that. It set an observed 76 per cent against an expected 33 per cent, which is the uniform three-family null belonging to the binomial, in the same passage as the measured scramble baseline of 44.5 per cent. Those are two different nulls and cannot carry one comparison between them. The primary result is the permutation, which conditions on the observed family margins and so already absorbs the scramble rate; where the 33 per cent comparison is kept it is named as the binomial's null. No test has been recomputed here, and the negative controls stay attributed to the v1 run and its record.

(e) The derivative paragraphs inherit their sources' corrections. Three repairs already stand and are not reopened: Paper V sits in the supported row of Table 1 rather than the proven one, the Fisher-combined figure is withdrawn, and the distinction between the five analysable runs and the one failed arm is kept. What had not travelled is the rest. Section 4.4's account of what the gated arm preserved, its identification of checking as the cost, and its treatment of a simulation as a demonstrated mechanism inherit the Paper VI and Paper VIII corrections, as does the summary sentence about entangled safety preventing collapse across twenty adversarial seeds. Any phrasing that reads across every model tested inherits the Paper V correction and stays scoped to the analysable runs, so that the arm on which scoring failed is not absorbed into the count. The carriage of the later exponent in Tables 2 and 3 travels with the caveat at (b). Those passages now point here rather than restate the reasoning, so that a correction adopted once is inherited wherever it reaches. One distinction underwrites all of them: a change in a score, a behaviour a gate induces in a toy system, a penalty a design stipulates, and resource actually spent are four different quantities, and none stands in for another.

What was refused. Three proposed repairs were examined and not adopted. The first would have described the behaviour at βL=1 as a blow-up at a finite depth; the working at (a) shows it is exponential there, and the finite-depth branch lies strictly above one. The second would have held that a null or an inconclusive result is never evidence either way; both constrain what can be claimed, which is the reason the programme reports them. The third would have recomputed the binomial against the measured scramble rate and made it the primary; no test has been rerun, and substituting a figure that has not been computed would be a worse error than the one it was meant to repair.

Why this section exists

Most research programmes bury their corrections in supplementary materials or version histories. This one lists them prominently because the errors and their corrections are themselves data. They demonstrate that the programme has a functioning error-correction mechanism. A programme that never admits error is not more trustworthy; it is less honest. Every correction listed above was identified by the author, through the programme's own methodology, or by adversarial review run within the programme. This is what self-correction looks like in practice.

In plain English

We got nineteen things wrong. We treated a number from one model as universal (it was not). We over-interpreted a dramatic result (it was a training artefact). We used imprecise language about timestamps. We trusted unblinded results (blinding reversed two of them). We named the principle after the author before anyone else had checked the work. We assumed prompt-level conditioning could override RLHF training (it could not). We wasted time on judges that did not work. We did not immediately flag that our own point estimate violated our own predicted bound. And in v3.0 we corrected four further errors found by adversarial review run within the programme: an arithmetic error in a confidence interval, an overstatement about which conditions are sufficient for the scaling prediction, an attribution error regarding Glazier's data, and an oversimplification of the empirical debate over the 3/4 exponent. Five more followed in version 2.4: we had printed the growth solution without the range it holds on, treated a wide interval as though it settled which regime a system is in, called a null result identity when it was a failure to detect a difference, compared an observed proportion against the wrong null, and left paragraphs standing on claims their own sources had already corrected. All nineteen were caught and corrected inside the programme, by the author, by its own methodology, or by adversarial review run for it. If the programme can correct its own mistakes, that is evidence the self-correction mechanism works.

8. The Phased Roadmap

The following roadmap is ordered by cost, feasibility, and expected evidential value. Each phase resolves specific open questions identified in Sections 4-6. Since this paper’s first version the roadmap has set into a registered programme: seventy preregistration units have been drafted, dated and frozen as draft registrations, each carrying a randomisation schedule drawn from a seed printed in the registration text. Whether those schedules in fact regenerate is checked rather than asserted, and the checking is done by the estate’s own gates on the day they are run. One trial, the corrector-class audit (unit 06), was registered on OSF on 1 October 2026 and has been public since 4 October 2026 (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/RV2B5). Every other trial is a draft awaiting submission by the author, most of them preregistered by timestamp. No count of how many units are ready is published here: two gates apply different criteria, they disagree, and any figure printed on a page goes stale the moment a unit changes. What the gates return on the day is the number worth trusting, and the phases below remain a plan rather than a claim of completion. A coverage matrix, adversarially verified, ties forty-two of the programme’s claims to the registered tests that would settle each one. The phases below remain the funding lens over that registered estate; nothing is submitted by any tool, ever.

Phase A: Low-Hanging Fruit

Budget: £60,000-140,000 | Timeline: 3-6 months

  • Blind replication of Eden intervention (resolves Section 5.3) - Run Paper V's stakeholder-care experiment under full ARC-Align blinding. Expected cost: £15,000-30,000 in API calls.
  • Weight experiment with base models (resolves Section 5.2) - Re-run Paper VIII Experiment 2 using base models (pre-RLHF), 5,000+ training examples, full fine-tuning (not LoRA), or 7B+ models. The key requirement: the model must not have existing safety training that overwhelms the experimental manipulation.
  • Jailbreak resistance study - Test whether Eden-trained models resist adversarial prompts more robustly than standard RLHF. Uses existing ARC-Align infrastructure.
  • Human evaluator comparison - Compare ARC-Align blind AI scoring against human expert scoring on a subset of outputs. Required for methodological validation.
  • ARC formula blind test - Redesign the measurement methodology for the ARC scaling formula. The current approach uses derivative estimation, which amplifies noise. 12. The Pitch to Researchers

The ARC/Eden programme makes one prediction that any researcher with access to scaling data can test without engaging with the alignment claims at all. The Cauchy framework (Paper VII, Origin of Scaling Laws) predicts that for any spatially embedded recursive system with embedding dimension d, the scaling exponent is governed by:

α=dd+1

The geometric scaling bound: 2D systems → α=23; 3D systems → α=34; 4D systems → α=45. Note: the empirical mammalian metabolic exponent is debated, with estimates ranging from approximately 0.67 to 0.75 depending on taxon, mass range, temperature correction, and statistical method. The d/(d+1) prediction of 0.750 for d=3 matches the upper end of this range. The variation itself is consistent with the framework: organisms with effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 would produce exponents between 2/3 and 3/4.

This is not an alignment claim. It is a mathematical prediction about scaling laws in recursive systems. The prediction requires three conditions: multiplicative composition (which Cauchy constrains to the power-law family), d-dimensional space-filling geometry, and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow (energy minimisation in West, supply-demand balance in Banavar, steady-state energy balance in Demetrius). The d/(d+1) scaling formula is derived, in its general form or as a special case, in earlier work: West, Brown and Enquist (1997) from fractal branching networks, Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) from geometric constraints on transportation networks, Demetrius (2003, 2006) from quantum metabolism, He and Chen (2003) from fractal cell geometry, Bettencourt (2013) from urban scaling theory, in which the exponent of a city’s land area, D/(D+H), takes the form only when H=1, Maino et al. (2014) from DEB theory reserve-structure dynamics, and Zhao (2022) from producer-consumer dynamics; the later paper of Banavar and co-authors, Banavar et al. (2010), gives in two models of resource distribution networks an exponent of 2/3 when the velocity of flow is constant and of at most 3/4. Each of these derivations is consistent with the d/(d+1) form on its own axioms; the Cauchy framework proposes a common reason for the convergence, and that meta-account is the contribution unique to this programme, still under test outside its motivating cases. If you study scaling in any domain, from neural networks to biological networks to urban systems to linguistic structures, you can check whether your measured exponents respect this formula. If they do, across enough domains, the Cauchy framework gains support. If they do not, it is falsified.

The invitation is deliberate. The strongest scientific frameworks are those that make predictions outside their core domain, allowing researchers who are sceptical of the central thesis to test the peripheral predictions independently. If the geometric scaling bound holds, it holds for reasons that are mathematically interesting regardless of what one thinks about AI alignment. If it fails, the framework's mathematical foundations require revision, and we would rather know that sooner than later.

This is the kind of prediction that should be the entry point for researchers encountering the programme for the first time: low-cost to test, falsifiable, and informative regardless of outcome.

13. Conclusion

Here is what we know. Alignment scaling is architecture-dependent: some models improve with reasoning depth, some are unaffected, and some degrade. This was only visible under blinded evaluation, which reversed the results of unblinded scoring for half the models tested. Unblinded AI evaluation can flip the sign of alignment results. Stakeholder care is a robust alignment intervention across all five models whose runs could be scored, of six run; the per-model results are individually significant, and the earlier Fisher-combined figure is withdrawn (AQ-017) because the independence a Fisher combination assumes was never established. The Cauchy framework's scaling predictions match 19 of 25 empirical domains (18 of 25 in the rerun of 11 August 2026 that added the logarithmic candidate), with the d/(d+1) geometric scaling bound corresponding, in structure, to observed scaling across 50 domains from mice to galaxies. Cauchy functional equations fix the functional form once the composition identity and a regularity condition are given, with saturation admitted as a fourth, capped case.

Here is what we do not know. Safety-trained models resist modification at both prompt and weight levels. The DGM v3 experiment produced a null result, on the candidate account that RLHF-trained models resist prompt-level differentiation. The weight experiment was inconclusive at both scales tested, on the candidate account that instruct tuning was too strong for LoRA to override at the ranks tried. The only level at which the Eden Protocol has so far shown an effect on the endpoints used is the architectural level, and it has shown it there in simulation: the gated simulation showed the Babylon reward-hacking fingerprint, a simulation result rather than an empirical outcome; the candidate account is that it alone used deterministic metrics on a system without pre-existing RLHF training. These are candidate accounts, and the section that records them as such is 7.12(c). Weight-level structural entanglement has not been demonstrated. The Eden intervention has not been tested under the programme's own strictest blinding protocol. The unbounded-scaling prediction remains a mathematical derivation without empirical confirmation. We do not know whether any of these results generalise to frontier-scale models.

Here is what comes next. The weight experiment needs base models (pre-RLHF), 5,000+ training examples, full fine-tuning, or 7B+ models. The Cauchy unification needs pre-registered independent operator classification. The ARC formula blind test needs redesigned measurement methodology using linearisation rather than derivatives. 2D organism metabolic scaling (flatworms, biofilms) would be the single most important confirmatory experiment for the geometric scaling bound. Phase A of the roadmap costs less than a typical seed grant and would resolve the most significant evidential gaps within six months. Phase B would produce publication-ready replications within a year. Phase C would answer the question definitively at frontier scale. Phase D costs nothing and invites mathematicians to engage with the framework's predictions independently.

The programme has demonstrated that it can find and correct its own errors. It has demonstrated that it reports null and inconclusive results alongside positive ones. It has demonstrated that its methodology (blinded evaluation) catches biases that its theory (alignment scaling) did not predict. We asked the questions. We tested them. Some worked. Most did not at current scale. We reported both. These are necessary conditions for scientific credibility. They are not sufficient. Sufficient conditions require independent replication, which is what the roadmap is designed to produce.

The questions this programme asks now have a named home: Recursive Dynamics, named as a field at the Carnot stage, its founding paper carrying five substrate-independent state variables, fifty-one printed objections with dispositions, and the name’s own kill condition. The synthesis above is what the field starts from; the registered programme is how it moves.
