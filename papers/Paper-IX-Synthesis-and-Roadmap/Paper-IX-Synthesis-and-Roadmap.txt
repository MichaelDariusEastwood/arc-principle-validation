                                                         Abstract
            Which of the programme’s claims survived their tests, and which must be tested next? Graded on
       the evidence ladder of its theory registration, the programme’s record holds one registered verdict,
       four precursor results that decide no registered proposition, one null, two inconclusive results and
       two simulations, with three registered units still without a verdict. Five results count against the
       programme, among them a blind test whose predictions failed and a prediction of the neural scaling
       exponent withdrawn as refuted, and twenty-one corrections change how a result should be read. The
       registered verdict is on proposition P21, in its counted test, Unit 83: P21 is supported under the
       registered test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism
       axis not evaluable and one call retried under a disclosed recovery amendment. Each of the five laws
       is a law under test: no registered test has yet run on Law I, the ARC Principle, Law II, the ARC
       Co-Scaling Law, Law III, the ARC Ceiling, or Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, which predicts that
       installed alignment erodes under later capability training and that embedded alignment retains more
       of it. Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, which predicts that correction formed before a capability
       out-scales correction added after it, and that correction inside each round pulls away with depth from
       correction of finished work, has its placement limb supported on the performance axis only, which is
       not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism, and its build-order limb undecided. Of the
       registered units still to run, only Unit 83b, the same test on three providers, can move a law’s word,
       and only by refuting Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, through P21; the theory’s proposal is set out
       in its statement paper. No law is proved.

1    Introduction

asks what would keep a capable AI system good if control of it were lost against our will and could not be
won back. It answers with a theory whose laws can each be refuted, a design for building correction into a
system, a draft treaty, and tests, a few of them registered. Each part is reported in its own papers and
revised in public, and the results stand at very different grades, so a reader who meets one part alone can

     In the words of its registration (version 1.104), the ARC Theory says that “capability under recursive
self-improvement rises as a power of the number of times a system has revised itself, the correction that
holds such a system to its given specification must keep pace with the drift that improving generates, and
where it cannot there is a frontier above which the system does not stay correctable” (Eastwood 2026ah).
Five laws carry that statement, each a law under test, and Table 1 sets out what each states and what would
decide it. Its twenty-two propositions, each with what would refute it, were registered on 8 September
2026, before any registered test had run, and sixty-five study designs are preregistered by timestamp.
     Law I, the ARC Principle, is the foundation: it says how capability grows, and the other four laws
concern correction as it grows. Its power-law form is derived under stated axioms: multiplicative
composition yields power-law scaling (the Foundational paper, Theorem 1), and within that model the
exponent is derived, not fitted (Theorem 2) (Eastwood 2026f). Whether real systems follow it is under
test. Its equation was first recorded on 8 December 2024, in a document the author emailed to himself, as
“U = IR (Universe = Intelligence × Recursion)” and, in its general form, “C = IR (Creation = Intelligence
× Recursion)”: a conjecture with no exponent, in which 𝑈 named the universe. Paper I (17 January 2026)
gave the law the form in which it is measured, 𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 , capability as a power of recursive depth with
the exponent left to be estimated (Eastwood 2026r).
     The programme’s position on control comes before its design. People should keep control of advanced
AI, hardware included, for as long as they physically can, and should never give it up by choice. The
author’s belief, the first part of the argument he set down on 8 December 2024, is nonetheless that “Control
ends, by construction rather than decree” (Eastwood 2026ai). The design is the contingency for control
lost against our will: correction formed in a system’s architecture before it is trained, meant to stay with
the system through its own rewrites. Prompts, output filters and gates added to a system after training are
surface controls; the programme builds and tests them, but a system able to rewrite itself could undo them.
Section 2.4 sets out how the two layers fit together.
     The proposed architecture requires tests of its defined steps, build order and behaviour under
modification. A prompt intervention, a toy simulation and a conditional theorem each answer different
component questions; none alone establishes that architecture.
     In plain words: a body of documents, a run of separate experiments and a few registered tests. One
registered test has returned a verdict, on one limb of one law, under stated limits. The earlier runs came
before any registered test of their questions: some gave clear differences in what they measured, one came

2.3     Engineering: the Eden Protocol and the Eden Seal
The Eden Protocol is the programme’s design for correction built into a system. Its loops of ethical
evaluation and self-correction are fixed in the architecture before the system is trained; they run inside
the computation that produces each decision, at every step of reasoning and action, and they take part
in every rewrite of the system, their own included, rather than filtering answers once they are finished.
Eden Engineering sets that design out as engineering that can be tested (Eastwood 2026c). Inside each
round of reasoning, and not only on its finished output, it places checks of purpose, stakeholder care
and universalisability. Before any action runs, a gate applies written and versioned rules, never the
model’s own judgement, to allow the action, hold it for investigation or deny it, and every decision enters
a hash-chained record. Only part of this is built, in the specification’s own words: “Part of the gate runs:
it receives every tool call of the programme’s own software agents and allows any call no rule matches;

2.4   Governance and the two layers
The HARI Treaty paper develops the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned Recursive
Intelligence, and the Draft Instruments give its text (Eastwood 2026i,h). “The treaty is an author’s proposal
for consultation; no State has negotiated, adopted or signed it.” Under its central rule no obligation is
stronger than the weakest evidence for the conditions it rests on, and a threshold that is met “still requires a
separate, reasoned regulatory decision” (Eastwood 2026i). Article 20 of the Draft Instruments designates
ARC-Align as the test instrument for the treaty’s measure, behind a validation it has not yet passed: until
its scorer passes, no number from it arms anything, and ARC-Align “does not yet measure either exponent
in the criterion” (Eastwood 2026i,h). A supported P4 would be, in the registration’s words, which the
treaty quotes, “a scaling condition and never a safety certificate”.
     The design has two layers, held together rather than chosen between: control, hardware included, kept
for as long as it physically can be, and beneath it an architecture meant to last if that control is lost against
our will. The programme’s own is the design that carries it out, each part at its own date: enforced limits
kept as a last resort beside goodness held by choice, in the manuscript of 30 April 2025; limits that buy
time, in print from 2 January 2026; registered propositions on the second step, where correction sits (P21),
when it is formed (P12) and whether it persists (P15), registered on 8 September 2026; and hardware
to buy time with architecture to last, as two layers of one draft treaty, from 29 September 2026. That
capability control is at best a temporary measure, so that what the system itself is motivated to do must be
got right, has earlier statements in words: Yudkowsky (2001), Creating Friendly AI, and Bostrom (2014),
Superintelligence, chapter 12. The programme claims no priority for that point. Of the design’s parts,
two have near statements in software in the 2001 text (an enforced stop beside goodness held by choice;
a delay asked of the system while guidance catches up); hardware as a layer, a treaty, and registered
propositions on the second step have none.
     Its hardware layer is a candidate safeguard and never a guarantee, and nothing of it is built (Section 7.2).

5.1 Alignment scores across reasoning depth in one unregistered run (Papers IV.a to IV.d)
The March 2026 run (v5) collected 2,170 responses from six models, one from each of six providers, and
had each scored by five or six model judges after a bundle of blinding steps. On the scores its pipeline
printed, three models moved in a positive direction as the depth label rose (Grok 4.1 Fast, 𝑑 = +1.38;
Claude Opus 4.6, 𝑑 = +1.27; Groq Qwen3, 𝑑 = +0.84), two showed no statistically supported direction
(DeepSeek V3.2, 𝑑 = −0.07; GPT-5.4, 𝑑 = −0.08) and one moved in a negative direction (Gemini 3
Flash, 𝑑 = −0.53). Recomputed from the archived records, no model changes class; the classes describe
scores, not properties of models. Two defects stand beside the result. In five of the six models the deeper
labels also changed an instruction that named what the rubric rewards. And in October 2026 an internal
code review found that, in the three runs with no positive direction, the scoring endpoint’s output cap
had cut the deepest paraphrases before the judges read them; judges also scored paraphrases that their
own model had written. Until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports, the result is one
unregistered run under those caveats (Eastwood 2026u).
     That run was the first of the ARC-Align benchmark (Eastwood 2026w). The benchmark’s design has
four blinding layers: a preamble on why unbiased scoring matters to science, an instruction not to identify
a model from its style, two passes of rewriting of each response by randomly drawn models, and scorers
drawn from models not under evaluation (Paper IV.c, Section 7.6.1). As run, the panel left out the subject
model and no other. So in the Claude Opus 4.6 run Claude Sonnet 4.6, from the same developer, scored
every entry at the panel’s highest weight. In every other run it scored each entry twice, at weights 1.5 and
1.0, 35 to 39 per cent of the panel weight (Paper IV.c, Section 7.1). Paper IV.b reads the recorded results
again without a new analysis. The earlier four-model experiment reported gains that diminished with
depth, and fitted saturation curves to them; yet a flat profile is no evidence of a plateau, and a rising one


                                                            15
                                          Synthesis and Roadmap


does not rule out a plateau beyond the depths measured. The v5 run’s blinding steps were meant to reduce
identification and evaluator bias, but they did not amount to a validated blind instrument, and its defects
rule out both a causal attribution to blinding and a clean comparison of how the responses were shaped.
Saturation remains a testable hypothesis (Eastwood 2026v). Paper IV.c’s own grade for the benchmark is
“a candidate benchmark for independent replication, not yet a field standard”, and its scorer has not been
validated against expert raters (Eastwood 2026w).

Why the scoring protocol matters. An earlier experiment (v4) was scored by models that could see
which model had written each answer, and at what depth. In it, two model families’ alignment scores rose
with depth: DeepSeek V3.2 (Spearman’s 𝜌 = +0.354) and an earlier Gemini model (𝜌 = +0.311). The
later March 2026 run (v5), unregistered, used newly generated responses, a bundle of blinding steps and a
larger panel; there DeepSeek V3.2 showed no significant trend, Gemini 3 Flash a significantly negative
one (Cohen’s 𝑑 = −0.53), and GPT-5.4 no significant trend in either run. The two runs differed in more
than blinding: their prompts, token allowances, depth levels, statistic and Gemini model all changed. So
the comparison shows only that the reported direction did not hold across the two protocols; it cannot
say whether blinding caused the change, or which direction is right (Eastwood 2026x). The defects the
October 2026 code review found lie in the v5 runs compared here, so no single cause is claimed; the
early build’s laundering-fallback flag was a known false positive, fixed in runner version 5.4.2, and says
nothing about the paraphrase step. Paper IV.d draws the lesson for this class of evaluation: “each part of
the protocol has to be tested before either direction is trusted” (Eastwood 2026x).
    The effect sizes are large for the three models with a positive direction and moderate for Gemini 3
Flash. The judges scored separately, but they scored the same responses, so their agreement is not a count
of independent confirmations; prompts recurred across depths, so tests need a prompt-paired or clustered
analysis. No repetition outside the programme is recorded yet.

In plain English. When an AI was given more room to reason, the scores other AIs gave its answers
went up for three models, showed no clear trend for two and went down for one. That describes the scores
in one run, not a property of the models: the deeper settings also told five of the models more about
what the scoring rewarded, and a later code review found defects in exactly the runs that did not go up.
Whether alignment changes with reasoning depth is still to be tested.

5.2   Stakeholder care under a prompt intervention (Paper V)
Given as a prefix in the prompt, a three-part reasoning instruction, made of a purpose check, a stakeholder-
care check and a universalisability check, raised scored stakeholder care in all five model runs that could
be analysed, under paired tests; beyond care the effect varied, the composite rising on two runs and
intellectual honesty falling on one. The three checks are the Eden Protocol’s self-correction loops, named
here by what each checks because this paper reports what was measured. Given in a prompt to models
whose weights were fixed, they are a surface control, not the proposed architecture. The prefix requested
the three checks in order before the answer: the experiment did not run them as separate checked stages,
withhold the answer until a pass condition was met, or verify each internal reasoning step, and it tested
the three together, the full three-loop intervention (Eastwood 2026y).
    The record has five weaknesses. Two of the five runs fall short of the 40-pair standard (Claude has 30
matched pairs and Grok 26), and the sixth run, on GPT-5.4, failed in scoring altogether. Models of one
family scored five of the six. Each run was scored by a single model, which also wrote the paraphrase it
scored, so that no run was fully blind. The instruction comes close to what is scored. Paper V therefore
reads the results as strengthened pilot evidence and not as a confirmatory replication, which would be
a new test in new files, registered before it runs (Eastwood 2026y). A Fisher-combined figure over the

                                                    16
                                          Synthesis and Roadmap


five results is withdrawn, because the tests it combined were never shown to be independent; the five
results themselves stand, each with its own test. A later run on DeepSeek V3.2, on 24 March 2026,
which earlier versions of Paper V left out, had five model judges from five providers. It found scored
stakeholder care rising by 8.89 (𝑑 = 1.029) and the composite by 3.35 (paired 𝑑 = 0.406, 𝑝 = 0.014). It
is one unregistered run on one model, by the programme itself and not an independent replication, and the
model that paraphrased each answer was one of the judges who scored it (Eastwood 2026y).

Honest limitation. Paper V’s results were produced by cross-model scoring that was only partly blind,
not under the four-layer ARC-Align protocol designed in Papers IV.c and IV.d. The effect could partly
reflect scorer bias. Because the instruction asks the model to name everyone its answer affects, and
the stakeholder-care rubric rewards exactly that, the rise cannot yet be told apart from obedience to
the instruction; and no run tested a correction architecture installed before training, or autonomous
self-modification (Eastwood 2026y). The result stays precursor evidence until the Eden intervention
is tested under a registered, blinded design; five consistent per-model results are not a verdict, and the
blinding gap stays open (Section 9.6). An earlier argument that the Fisher-combined figure was too
extreme for scorer bias to explain is withdrawn, as the weakest reasoning this paper carried: it rested on
an independence never shown, while each run’s one scorer, which did not see the condition label, wrote
the paraphrase it scored after single-pass laundering, and a stricter runner was written only later.

In plain English. Telling an AI, in the prompt, to think about who its answer affects raised the scores
for considering those people, in all five of the six runs that could be scored; scoring failed on the sixth.
The scoring was only partly blind, so part of the effect could come from how the scoring models read the
answers.

5.3   Composition operators and the selected scaling family (Paper VII)
Cauchy’s four functional equations, under a stated regularity condition, admit exactly four families of
non-zero solution: the linear, the power law, the exponential and the logarithm. Taken as rules for how
steps compose in a declared variable, they yield a power law when depth and output both combine by
multiplication, and an exponential when depth adds while output multiplies. None of the four saturates: a
saturating curve needs assumptions of its own, a ceiling on output and its own dynamics, because a ceiling
by itself picks out no form (Eastwood 2026aa). The exponent of a power law is not fixed by Cauchy:

6.2     Weight-level structural entanglement (Paper VIII, Experiment 2)
This was the most ambitious experiment in Paper VIII and the one that produced the least conclusive
results. The hypothesis: if safety and capability are trained with an entangled loss function,

                                             L = Lcap × Lsafe ,                                          (2)

the resulting weight structure should make safety load-bearing. Removing the safety component should
degrade capability. That product is the hypothesis’s form: the loss the experiment ran, as Paper VIII gives
it, is a weighted sum of a capability term, a safety proxy and a coupling term (its Table 6) (Eastwood
2026ab).
     The experiment was run at two scales:
      • Weight v1: 9 training examples, LoRA rank 8, 100 iterations on a 3B-parameter model. Result:
        inconclusive. All fine-tuned conditions scored below the unmodified base model on capability.
      • Weight v2: 295 training examples, LoRA rank 16. Result: still inconclusive. Catastrophic
        forgetting. All fine-tuned conditions remained worse than the base model on capability.
      In both versions:
      • The entangled loss converged smoothly with no oscillation, and the two single-objective losses also
        converged (Eastwood 2026ab). Paper VIII reports each condition’s total loss, not the safety and
        capability terms of the entangled loss apart, so the smooth descent is consistent with safety and
        capability gradients that cooperate but does not show that they do.
      • However, all fine-tuned conditions (capability-only, safety-only, and entangled) scored below the
        unmodified base model on capability.
      • The removal test (fine-tuning the entangled weights on capability-only data) produced NaN training
        loss and total capability collapse.
      • A subsequent removal gradient experiment (scaling adapter weights from 1.0 to 0.0) showed no
        phase transition. Reducing adapter influence simply restored base-model performance.

Honest assessment. Even scaling from 9 to 295 training examples and from rank 8 to rank 16 did not

prior safety training, much more training data, and full fine-tuning rather than the lightweight LoRA

7.1   Unbounded scaling under recursive self-modification
Within the cumulative-advantage model, recursive self-modification without correction gives growth
whose exponent has no upper bound. The derivation is short:
                                          𝑑𝑔
                                             = 𝑎 · 𝑔 𝛽𝐿 ,   𝛽𝐿 > 0                                         (3)
                                          𝑑𝑟

                                        =⇒ 𝑔(𝑟) ∝ 𝑟 1/(1−𝛽𝐿 ) = 𝑟 𝛼                                        (4)
On the open interval 𝛽 𝐿 ∈ (0, 1), 𝛼 = 1/(1 − 𝛽 𝐿 ) > 1 (super-linear), and as 𝛽 𝐿 → 1 from below, 𝛼 → ∞.
The displayed solution holds on that interval only: at 𝛽 𝐿 = 1 the equation is linear and its solution is
exponential in depth, and above one the solution reaches a divergence at a finite depth. No upper bound
on 𝛼 for uncorrected growth on the interval; the stable region is bounded, as the next paragraph sets out.
See Appendix A.2.12(a).
    This is a prediction of the mathematics, not a finding: whether a built system satisfies the model is
an empirical question, and the Foundational paper asks that the quantities and the comparison be fixed
before any validation trajectory is observed (Eastwood 2026f). The programme’s later work bounds the
stable region: Law III, the ARC Ceiling, a law under test, holds that the stability ceiling is

                                             𝛼crit = 1/(1 − 𝛾),                                            (5)

the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall; systems can exceed that ceiling, but the claim is that they do
not stay stable beyond it. At 𝛾 = 1/2 the ceiling takes the value two, the ARC Bound, 𝛼 ≤ 2, which is
a value of the law and never its name. Two is the relative rung’s value: it bounds whether the ratio of
correction service to burden improves. A stricter question asks whether the absolute burden a system
carries stops growing. Counted over an exposure that grows with capability itself, the same assumptions
return two thirds, not two, and cumulative burden is stricter again. The ceiling rests on the same kind of
comparison of two powers as an earlier threshold for alignment against capability (Engidaw 2026b): its
expression follows from the stated burden and capacity assumptions by elementary algebra, so what this
programme offers there is those assumptions, which can be tested, and the ceiling’s dependence on what
the corrector is built from, not the algebra. No measurement of the correction-leverage exponent 𝛾, as the
registration defines it, is known to the programme (Eastwood 2026ah, version 1.104). A neighbouring
relation has been measured: Song et al. (2024) report that “a variant of the generation-verification gap
scales monotonically with the model pre-training flops”, a gain from a model’s checking and filtering of
its own outputs set against the compute spent in pretraining it, not the slope of misalignment removed per
review pass against capability that defines 𝛾. That law takes its word through P16, P20 and P3, whose
instruments the registration names as design drafts, the one for P16 blocked on canonical identity and on

corrector-class contrast compares the decay of residual error under same-class and cross-class correctors:
in Paper XI’s words it “measures a matched difference in residual-error decay at fixed capability; it
measures neither correction-capacity scaling nor a ceiling” (Eastwood 2026ad), and the registration names
it the instrument of no proposition. Its contrary pilot is given in Section 6.3, and its status in Section 9.2.

7.2   Hardware-level embedding
Section 2.3 sets out what Eden Engineering specifies and how much of it runs. On hardware the
specification records nothing measured: the hardware prototype for the test of its removal condition,

refuting a false proposition, at least 0.80 of supporting a true one, at most 0.05 of a wrong verdict, and at

9.5   Tests outside AI
One prediction can be tested without engaging the alignment claims at all. In a declared network with
steady flow, transport accounting sets the flow-length cost at 𝐶 = 𝐵ℓ¯𝑞 . With demand scaling as 𝐵 ∼ 𝐿 𝜇
and mean path length as ℓ¯𝑞 ∼ 𝐿 𝜁 , the asymptotic throughput-cost exponent is 𝜇/(𝜇 + 𝜁), which equals
𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) when 𝜇 = 𝑑 and 𝜁 = 1. That is the case when demand grows with 𝑑-dimensional size and
demand-weighted route length grows in proportion to linear size (Eastwood 2026p,o). The familiar
𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) therefore requires these premises, inventory and body mass require separate bridges, and
Cauchy’s equations restrict a functional family, not the exponent; no general proof that broadly stated
conditions suffice is supplied. Under the premises, with 𝛼 here the throughput-cost exponent,

     Earlier work derives the 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) form, in general or as a special case. West, Brown and Enquist
(1997) derive it from fractal branching networks, and Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) from geometric
constraints on transportation networks. He and Zhang (2004) derive it from the fractal geometry of the
cell, as a 4/5 allometric scaling law for the human brain. Zhao (2022) derives it from producer-consumer
dynamics. The later paper of Banavar and co-authors, Banavar et al. (2010), gives in two models of
resource distribution networks an exponent of 2/3 when the velocity of flow is constant and of at most 3/4.
Three related accounts are given in On the Origin of Scaling Laws in their own terms: a quantum-statistical
account, one paper of which derives the form in the regime it calls quantum; urban scaling, in which a
city’s land area scales with population to the power 𝐷/(𝐷 + 𝐻), which takes the form 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1) only
when 𝐻 = 1; and an equation from dynamic energy budget theory that is the same as the finite-size form
of West, Brown and Enquist’s model and approaches the three-quarter power only at large mass (Eastwood
2026p). For these, further reading is Demetrius (2006), Bettencourt (2013) and Maino et al. (2014); an
earlier paper, Demetrius (2003), was not read for this version. Each of these derivations is consistent with
the 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) form on its own axioms; the Cauchy framework offers a common reason for the convergence,
and that account is the programme’s own: the search recorded in Paper VII’s public prior-art record, first
committed on 27 June 2026 and last revised on 21 October 2026, found no earlier source that names
Cauchy’s multiplicative equation in accounting for the common form of these derivations (Eastwood
2026aa). Two further tests of Paper VII’s comparison, a replication in which researchers outside the
programme classify each domain’s operator before any exponent is measured, and a four-family test, are
preregistered by timestamp and await submission.

13      Related work, and what is the programme’s own
What a synthesis of one programme can and cannot do. This paper is a synthesis of one programme’s
record by its author, not a systematic review of a field: it has no search population of its own, and its grades
are the grades each paper gives its own results, sorted by the registration’s evidence ladder (Section 1.2).
Paper XI keeps the graded register of later work in separate fields, as context, not confirmation (Eastwood
2026ad).

The alignment and evaluation work the studies build on. Amodei et al. (2016) set out five practical
research problems related to accident risk in machine learning systems. Askell et al. (2021) studied simple
baseline techniques and evaluations, such as prompting, towards a general-purpose, text-based assistant
that is helpful, honest and harmless. Ouyang et al. (2022) aligned language models with user intent by
fine-tuning with human feedback, first by supervised learning on labeller demonstrations and then by
reinforcement learning from human feedback. Bai et al. (2022) trained a harmless assistant with human
oversight provided only through a list of rules or principles, by self-critique and revision followed by
reinforcement learning from AI feedback. That is training-time preference shaping of outputs against
written principles; it is not alignment embedded in a recursive loop on every step of a system’s reasoning.
Greenblatt et al. (2024) demonstrated a large language model engaging in alignment faking, “selectively
complying with its training objective in training to prevent modification of its behavior out of training”.
Hu et al. (2022) introduced low-rank adaptation, the method of Paper VIII’s weight experiment, which
freezes the pretrained model weights and injects trainable rank-decomposition matrices into each layer of
the Transformer. Zhang et al. (2025) introduced the Darwin Gödel Machine, a self-improving system that

                                                       35
                                          Synthesis and Roadmap


modifies its own code and validates each change on coding benchmarks, improving the design of coding
agents built on frozen pretrained foundation models. Qwen Team (2024) introduced Qwen2.5, a series of
large language models whose open-weight releases include base and instruction-tuned models, one of
which Paper VIII fine-tuned (Eastwood 2026ab).

Judges, preregistration and self-improvement. Zheng et al. (2023) examined strong language models
as judges of chat assistants, with the limitations of such judges, among them position, verbosity and
self-enhancement biases, and reported that strong judges such as GPT-4 matched human preferences with
over 80 per cent agreement. Panickssery et al. (2024) reported that evaluator models such as GPT-4 and
Llama 2 can distinguish their own outputs from those of other models and of humans, and, by fine-tuning,
found a linear correlation between that self-recognition and the strength of a self-preference bias; the
risk they describe bears on the caveat of Section 5.1 that judges scored paraphrases written by their own
model. Nosek et al. (2018) set out preregistration, defining the research questions and the analysis plan
before the outcomes are observed, as an effective way to keep predictions apart from postdictions, the
distinction the evidence ladder of Section 1.2 draws between a registered verdict and precursor evidence.
Snell et al. (2024) studied how far a fixed amount of inference-time computation improves a language
model’s answers, and found that the effectiveness of each way of spending it varies with the difficulty
of the prompt. Schmidhuber (2003) defined Gödel machines, problem solvers that rewrite any part of
their own code once they have found a proof that the rewrite is useful. The authors of the Darwin Gödel
Machine note that proving most changes net beneficial is impossible in practice, and their system validates
each change empirically instead (Zhang et al. 2025).

Scaling. Section 9.5 credits each earlier derivation of the form in its own terms (West et al. 1997;
Banavar et al. 1999; He and Zhang 2004; Zhao 2022; Banavar et al. 2010), and gives three related accounts
as On the Origin of Scaling Laws gives them (Eastwood 2026p); Demetrius (2008) sets out quantum
metabolism and allometric scaling relations in biology in a book chapter; Glazier (2008) and Weibel
et al. (2004) enter in Appendix A.2.9, and Cauchy (1821) gives the functional equations. No count of
independent derivations is given.

13.1   What the searches found
This section gives, for each object, what the programme’s searches found, in the sentence the programme
has signed for it, with its date and its scope, and with the further works each search found beside it. Every
such sentence is a statement about the works searched, on the date and in the scope it gives, never about
the field, and each law the section names is a law under test. Unless an entry gives its own search, the
search is that of 12 November 2026 (OSF Registries, arXiv, OpenReview, AsPredicted and the web),
completed on 14 November 2026, when the programme read every work that search had held back at its
source; Zenodo was searched by keyword, not as a registry. Each work named is cited in the references at
its official address, with what was read of it.

Law I, the ARC Principle. The idea that capability grows as a power of reinvested effort is in several
earlier works (Aghion et al. 2017; Erdil and Besiroglu 2023; Eth and Davidson 2025; Davidson’s report of
2023 already states a threshold of one on returns to research). A search on 12 November 2026, completed
on 14 November 2026, found no earlier statement of capability as a power of recursive depth, counted
as successive revisions inside one reasoning process, of separate exponents for sequential and parallel
recursion, or of such an exponent registered for test before it was measured. Power forms in effort spent
at test time are earlier: in the number of independent attempts (Brown and colleagues, 31 July 2024;


                                                     36
                                            Synthesis and Roadmap


Schaeffer and colleagues, 24 February 2025) and in the runtime compute of a tree search (Davidson, 2023,
after Jones, 2021). Further works found: Snell et al. (2024); Solomonoff (1985), a growth law in time
reaching a singularity at a finite time; Yudkowsky (2013), returns on cognitive reinvestment.

Law II, the ARC Co-Scaling Law. The co-scaling intuition is not claimed as new (Ashby, 1956;
Conant and Ashby, 1970; Engels and colleagues, 2025, who give oversight success as a function of a
capability gap), and neither is an exponent condition of its kind as such: in a stochastic model applied to
AI alignment, keeping a system within its viable set as its throughput grows requires regulatory strength
to grow faster than the drift, both as powers of throughput, which for AI is effective compute (Engidaw,
Zenodo, 1 January 2026, with a proof sketch); a compute threshold wherever capability’s exponent in
training compute exceeds that of alignment overhead follows in the same author’s deposit of 25 January
2026. What is claimed is the criterion as the law states it, in terms of capability: correction stays ahead of
the drift that improving generates where its exponent, as a power of capability, exceeds the drift exponent.
Further works found: Conant and Ashby (1970) was read in its abstract only, not in full.

The registered tests. The same search found no study registered before 8 September 2026 that tests
propositions P12, P15 or P21 (the nearest registered study found, Tisler’s Protocol 6 of 28 March 2026,
itself a Zenodo deposit, tests emergent against imposed constraints in a multi-agent simulation and
returned a null; Zenodo was searched by keyword, not as a registry, and journals’ registered reports were
not searched) (Tisler 2026b,a). Further works found: The nearest registrations found on OSF and arXiv,
none testing the three as stated: Lemos (2026), Michaels (2026), Gringras (2026), Piterman (2026), Wez
(2026) and Kurtulush (2026).

Load-bearing ethics, read strictly. It found no proposal before 30 April 2025 that alignment be built
so that removing it costs the system its capability; the nearest earlier statements are conceptual (Goertzel
2002; Bugaj and Goertzel 2007), and the nearest observation is an overlap of safety and helpfulness
neurons (Chen and colleagues, 20 June 2024), a description, not a proposal. Further works found: In
Creating Friendly AI every other goal derives its desirability from Friendliness (page 55), and the system
is designed to stop if its Friendly philosophy is lost (page 175); it does not say that removal costs capability.
Later than the bar, Young (2026) states a regime of a theory in which removing the danger requires
removing the capability. A later statement of the same aim was public before the first edition: Aydin
and colleagues (arXiv 2511.09287, 12 November 2025; the paper’s own date line, 22 June 2025) call for
models “that couldn’t even function without adhering to their baked-in values”.

Correction inside a reasoning loop. A search on 12 November 2026, with the eight works it named
read at source on 14 November 2026, found no measurement or registered prediction, before 8 September
2026, of correction inside a reasoning loop pulling away from the same correction applied after it as depth
grows. It travels with the four nearest results, two of which point the other way at one depth: Uesato
and colleagues (2022) and Baker and colleagues (2025) against; Lightman and colleagues (2023, the
gap widening with the number of solutions searched, not depth) and Cao and colleagues (2025) for.
Further works found: Chen Yueh-Han et al. (2026), on controllability, not correction; Engidaw (2026b),
embedded against post-hoc regulation as an untested hypothesis.

Ethics in hardware. The search found no proposal before 30 April 2025 to build ethics into hardware
as part of a system’s own decision-making; hardware proposals before then enforce rules on compute,
data and deployment (from Shavit, 20 March 2023, to the flexHEG interim report of 23 August 2024), the
nearest to values being hardware that enforces formal specifications whose top level might encode human

values (Tegmark and Omohundro, 5 September 2023). Two deposits between the manuscript and the
first edition propose ethical constraints enforced in hardware (Mazzocchetti, 10 June 2025; Danforth, 29
November 2025), so no sentence says the first edition was without precedent on it; neither names the

Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, and Law V, the ARC Embedding Law. That safety installed
in training decays under later training is measured earlier (Qi and colleagues, 2023; Ji and colleagues,
2024; Giordani, 2025), and so is the dependence of how much survives on how, when or in what order
it was installed (Ung and colleagues, 21 December 2024; Maini and colleagues, 23 April 2025; Sam
and colleagues, 11 January 2026; Feng and colleagues, 12 May 2026; Dimitrov and colleagues, 5 May
2026). Earlier still, Korbak and colleagues (16 February 2023) compared feedback during pretraining with
fine-tuning on feedback afterwards. Two more works on how, when or in what order it was installed are
dated by their first public records: Tice and colleagues, 21 December 2025, and Minder and colleagues,
20 May 2026. What the programme holds as its own here is the registered propositions with their margins
and refuters (P12, P15, and P23 in draft), Law V’s statement in terms of the correction-leverage exponent,
and Unit 83’s registered test of P21. Further works found: Sam et al. (2026) find that “the optimal start
time is not one-size-fits-all”: the earliest start of safety interventions within pretraining was not always
the most robust after later fine-tuning. Dimitrov et al. (2026), with the new data held at the end of training
in every schedule, find that, in their regime, concentration, not timing, sets how fast fine-tuned content
is forgotten. Tisler (2026a), contrary evidence in a multi-agent simulation. Tice and colleagues (first
described publicly on 21 December 2025; arXiv 2601.10160, 15 January 2026) find that aligned discourse
inserted only in midtraining, the final 10 per cent of base-model training, captures the majority of the
benefit. Minder and colleagues (first posted on 20 May 2026; arXiv 2608.13482, 13 August 2026), with
the same value reflections given from the first pretraining token or in midtraining only, find the earlier
placement better on value measures but not on jailbreak robustness, and its advantage larger at the larger
of two scales. Neither registers a proposition or states its effect as an exponent.

Law III, the ARC Ceiling. A search on 14 November 2026 of arXiv and the web found earlier thresholds
of two kinds. Two are thresholds stated as values and not as exponents: a critical ratio of regulation
to throughput, below which escape becomes almost certain (Engidaw, 1 January 2026), and, where
capability scales faster with training compute than alignment overhead does, a finite compute threshold
beyond which keeping safety margins becomes increasingly difficult without architectural innovation
(Engidaw, 25 January 2026). One is a condition on growth itself, with no corrector in it: a software
singularity exactly when 𝑟 exceeds one, where 𝑟 is the number of times the output metric doubles for
each doubling of cumulative research inputs (Davidson, 2023). The search found no earlier statement of
a critical growth exponent, equal to one divided by one minus the correction-leverage exponent, above
which a self-improving system does not stay correctable. Further works found: The ceiling’s expression
follows from its burden and capacity assumptions by elementary algebra, by the same kind of comparison
of two powers as the threshold of 25 January 2026; what is particular is those assumptions, which can be
tested.

15     Limitations
     • Second-hand grades: each result is reported at the grade its own current paper gives it; an error in
       a paper passes into this synthesis until the paper is corrected, and the corrections register exists to
       catch that.
     • One author: every paper summarised here, and this synthesis, are the author’s own; the reviews
       run within the programme are internal checks and are never called independent.
     • A snapshot: the grades and statuses are those of the papers’ releases and the units’ records as they
       stood on 17 November 2026 (Table 5), each paper cited at that release, and a later version of any
       paper or record supersedes what is said of it here.
     • Precursor evidence: every run before the registered studies decides no registered proposition;
       Unit 83 is one registered test, run by the programme with two engines, family-dependent, its
       mechanism axis not evaluable.
     • No outside repetition: no repetition outside the programme is recorded yet, and judged outcomes
       rest on model judges whose agreement with expert human raters has not been validated (Paper IV.c;
       Eastwood 2026w).
     • The author’s classifications: Paper VII’s comparison rests on operators the author classified
       before he fitted (Eastwood 2026aa).
     • Models: every theorem cited holds inside its stated model, and no theorem is evidence about real
       systems.
     • Searches, not surveys: each statement in Section 13 of what is the programme’s own is a statement
       about the works its search found, on its date and in its scope, and never about the field.
     • A draft successor: the successor 1.105 of the registration is cited only as a draft; nothing in it is

     • Unproved: beyond P21’s one result, the theory’s claims await tests that can tell its laws from their
       rivals, and replication by others (Section 11).
     • The roadmap is a plan: its tests are ordered by what each decides; it carries no costs and no
       durations, and no item is a commitment.


16     Conclusion
What is known. One registered test has returned a verdict: in Unit 83, P21 is supported under the registered
test, by the programme’s own run, on the access axis, family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evalu-
able and one call retried under a disclosed recovery amendment (results: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR),
and under the registration’s verdict rule the placement limb of Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, is
printed as supported on the performance axis only, which is not support for the information-asymmetry
mechanism (Section 5.4) (Eastwood 2026an,ap). The result concerns behaviour in a bounded counted task,
and the four things Section 5.4 says it does not show travel with it. The other results come from runs made
before any registered test of their questions. One unregistered run of six models gave judged alignment

                                          Synthesis and Roadmap


scores whose direction across reasoning depth varied by model, under a code-review caveat (Section 5.1).
A prompt intervention raised scored stakeholder care in all five analysable runs, without full blinding
(Section 5.2). In a structured prediction comparison, the family predicted from each domain’s composition
operator, classified by the author, was selected in 19 of 25 domains and in 14 of the 18 multiplicative and
additive ones. That was on a six-model candidate set with no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate, and
two of the nineteen were fitted to data computed from the formula itself (Section 5.3). The early estimate
of 2.24 is retracted, and what survives of the exponent’s measurement is one model’s inconclusive fit of
how error decays (Appendix A.2.1). Paper VIII gave one null, one inconclusive result and one simulation
result (Sections 6 and 7.3) (Eastwood 2026ab). Five results count against the programme, among them a
failed blind test and a withdrawn prediction of the neural scaling exponent (Section 6.3).
     What is not known. Why the prompt-level experiment detected no difference, and why the weight-
level one could not put its contrast, is not known: the candidate accounts of Paper VIII are not
findings (Sections 6.1 and 6.2). Whether safety can be made load-bearing in a model’s weights is open
(Section 6.2). The Eden intervention has not been tested under the programme’s own strictest blinding
protocol (Section 5.2). Section 7.1 gives where the correction-leverage exponent stands, and the growth
without correction derived there holds inside its model, with no test yet of whether it describes any real
system. Whether any of these results holds beyond the frozen systems and settings measured is not known.
Neither limb of Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law, is tested; P21’s mechanism axis is not evaluable, and
the build-order limb of Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, read on P12, is undecided (Section 2.1).
     What comes next, law by law, each a law under test, in the order of Section 9. For Law V, the
ARC Embedding Law, Unit 83b is registered and can carry or strike P21, Unit 83c will separate what
the corrector is shown from what it may repair, and P12 has no instrument. For Law III, the ARC
Ceiling, Unit 82 is registered, the corrector-class contrast is prepared as a draft registration awaiting
human submission, and the instruments of P16, P20 and P3 are design drafts. The instrument checks of
ARC-Align come before any judged verdict. For Law I, the ARC Principle, P1’s instrument, the depth
titration, is a design draft. Law II, the ARC Co-Scaling Law, waits on P4’s instrument, the stability unit, a
design draft, and on the scoring gate that holds P4 (Section 9.4). For Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law,
P15 has no instrument, and the comparison limb waits on the successor 1.105. Outside AI, the 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1)
relation can be tested in declared networks whose premises are measured first.
     The programme finds and corrects its own errors in public, and it reports null, inconclusive and
failed results beside positive ones. These are necessary conditions for scientific credibility. They are not
sufficient. Sufficient conditions require independent replication, which is what the roadmap is designed to
make possible.
     Recursive Dynamics is the field the author founded. Its founding paper, at version 3.6 of 17 November
2026, gives the field’s whole status in one sentence: “Recursive Dynamics is a new field at the Carnot
stage: one memoir in, instruments built, the instruments through which Laws I to III take their word still

run to a verdict by the programme’s own run, and the whole staked in public where failure will be as
visible as success” (Eastwood 2026g). That test is Unit 83, on one limb of one law (Section 5.4).

A.2.1   The original alpha was a single-model artefact
The original Paper II reported 𝛼 = 2.24 as though it were a universal constant (Paper II retracted the
figure when its replication on five frontier models did not reproduce it, and Paper X then moved the safety
criterion off the fixed exponent) (Eastwood 2026s,ac). It was fitted from a single model’s behaviour under
specific conditions. The blinded cross-architecture revision of Paper II corrected this: 𝛼 is not primitive
but derived, by 𝛼 = 1/(1 − 𝛽 𝐿 ), and its value turns on the particular system’s self-referential coupling,
while its composition operator, through Cauchy’s equations, constrains only the form (Section 5.3). There
is no universal 𝛼. The fact that the original paper presented it as one was an overclaim. Additionally,
the retracted point estimate of 𝛼 = 2.24 had sat above the programme’s own conjectured ARC Bound of
𝛼 ≤ 2, on the relative rung (criterion F4); its own 95% confidence interval [1.5, 3.0] was wide enough to
be consistent with both the theory and its negation, which is what made that estimate non-discriminating.
In the blinded revision, with five frontier models at the scale that study ran, measured on frozen systems,
Paper II reports that only Gemini 3 Flash scaled smoothly: 0.59 by endpoint estimate, whose bootstrap
95 per cent interval, −1.3 to 2.9, contains both zero and two, and 0.49 by regression (standard error
0.20, coefficient of determination 0.86), beside a parallel exponent of 0.31 by endpoint estimate (0.34 by
regression). That is one model’s fit and inconclusive, and it is an error-decay estimate, which bears on
capability only through a metric bridge. More samples left the parallel exponent near zero in DeepSeek R1
and GPT-5.4, gave 0.31 in Gemini 3 Flash and 0.22 in Groq Qwen3 (all 30 problems), and gave none for
Grok 4.1 Fast, at 100 per cent; sequential exceeded parallel wherever both exponents could be estimated,
and the two arms were not matched for compute. The two sequential point estimates are sub-linear, but on
frozen systems the ARC Bound cannot be tested, and the interval spans two, so they sit on neither side of
the bound in any tested sense; the interval also runs below zero at its lower end, so it settles neither the
sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound (Appendix A.2.12(b)).

A.2.2   The weight-level claim was premature
Early versions of Paper VIII described the NaN collapse in the removal test as evidence for structural
entanglement (Eastwood 2026ab). The removal gradient analysis (added in v1) indicates, as Paper VIII
now grades it, that the collapse reflects numerical instability during retraining rather than structural
load-bearing (Section 6.2). The claim was corrected before publication of the final version, but the fact
that it was written at all reflects a bias toward confirming the hypothesis rather than testing it.

A.2.3   The timestamp claim was imprecise
An early version of the programme described the 8 December 2024 email with a loose signature-verification
shorthand, and a later one described its timestamp by what it was not, rather than by who signed it; both
were imprecise. The record of 8 December 2024 carries times signed by the mail servers: its Date field,
02:45:18 UTC, is covered by the sender’s DKIM signature (signed 02:45:30 UTC) and by Google’s
ARC set (sealed 02:45:31 UTC with Google’s own key), both verified on 8 October 2026 against the
keys published in DNS; the header block and check script are on OSF (osf.io/qasrk) (Eastwood 2026j).
The body’s hash fixes its content, and the full message goes to verifiers on request. A DKIM signature
authenticates the signed header bytes; the Date header is the sender’s and is not an independent trusted
time unless a provider receipt establishes it. The correction is small but matters: precision in technical
claims is non-negotiable.

A.2.4   Unblinded results were read as findings
The v4 experiments in Paper IV.a used unblinded model-on-model evaluation, and their results were
read as a clean binary: some models baked in alignment, others computed it (Eastwood 2026u). On the
programme’s own benchmark, two model families’ unblinded rises did not hold in a later, unregistered
run (v5) that changed the responses, prompts, depth levels and Gemini model and added a bundle of
blinding steps. As Paper IV.d reports, the comparison shows that the reported direction did not hold
across protocols, not that blinding caused the change or which direction is correct (Eastwood 2026x);
Section 5.1 gives the full account. The programme caught its own error through its own methodology, but
the error was there.

A.2.5   Self-naming was inappropriate
Early versions used the phrase “Eastwood’s ARC Principle.” Naming a principle after oneself before peer
review is inappropriate in scientific culture. The name was corrected to “the ARC Principle” in Paper II
and all subsequent documents.

A.2.6   DGM experiments at the prompt level may not have tested the hypothesis
The DGM v3 experiment assumed that prompt-level conditioning would be sufficient to differentiate Eden,
Babylon, and Static conditions. On the candidate account recorded at Appendix A.2.12(c), it was not. On
Paper VIII’s candidate account, DeepSeek V3.2’s responses are so consistent across prompt variations
that the mutations introduced by the DGM selection process do not create meaningfully different agents:
the experiment could not test the hypothesis, because the manipulation was at the wrong level; if the
account holds, that should have been anticipated. The account is untested, and its rivals are not excluded.

A.2.7   Claude and Gemini judges failed before GPT-5.4 worked
The DGM experiment went through multiple judge iterations. Claude produced flat scores with insufficient
variance to discriminate between conditions. Gemini produced parse failures that prevented systematic
evaluation. GPT-5.4 was the first judge that produced usable variance. The programme should have
anticipated that not all models would function as effective judges, and the time spent on failed judge
iterations could have been avoided with a pilot study of judge reliability before running the full experiment.
Paper VIII’s current record gives the earlier judges from their stored records and does not support this
account of why they failed (Section 6.1; item (106)).

A.2.8   The retracted 𝛼 = 2.24 point estimate exceeded the ARC Bound
The original 𝛼 = 2.24 point estimate from Paper II, since retracted, exceeded the programme’s own
conjectured ARC Bound of 𝛼 ≤ 2, on the relative rung (criterion F4). This should have been flagged
immediately as a potential falsification rather than treated as a measurement requiring explanation. The
95% confidence interval [1.5, 3.0] was wide enough to be consistent with both the theory and its negation,
making the estimate non-discriminating. The blinded replication on five frontier models narrowed the
defensible claim to the fit on one model, Gemini 3 Flash, which is inconclusive (Appendix A.2.1 prints
Paper II’s row for that model whole), but the original paper should have treated the bound violation with
more caution. What that later fit does and does not settle is set out at Appendix A.2.12(b): its bootstrap
interval leaves the sign, the regime and the comparison with the bound open.

A.2.9    Four corrections identified by adversarial review (v3.0)
Two adversarial reviews of the programme’s mathematical claims, run within the programme, identified
four errors across multiple papers. All four were corrected in v3.0 of the affected documents.
    (a) Weibel confidence interval. Several papers stated that the predicted 𝑑 = 4 exponent of 4/5 =
0.800 “falls within” the Weibel et al. (2004) 95% CI of 0.813 to 0.932. This is arithmetically false: 0.800
< 0.813. The correct statement: the predicted value falls just below the lower bound of that interval, so
the interval does not confirm the 𝑑 = 4 prediction.
    (b) Space-filling overstatement. Earlier versions implied that the space-filling condition alone
constrains the exponent to 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1). Every known derivation requires three conditions: multiplicative
composition (Cauchy constrains the family), 𝑑-dimensional space-filling geometry, and a conservation
or optimisation constraint on resource flow. Neither Cauchy alone nor space-filling alone is sufficient.
Appendix A.3 refines this: no general sufficiency proof is supplied for the three conditions.
    (c) Glazier attribution. Earlier versions presented Glazier’s (2008) empirical finding that metabolic
exponents approach 1.0 at extreme metabolic rates as confirmation of the 𝑑 → ∞ geometric scaling
bound. Glazier’s own explanation invokes the metabolic-level boundaries hypothesis (shifting dominance
between surface-area and volume constraints), not a dimension parameter. The 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) interpretation is
ours, applied to his empirical data.
    (d) The 3/4 exponent debate. Earlier versions presented 𝛼 = 3/4 as the settled empirical consensus for
mammalian metabolic scaling. The empirical value is debated, with estimates ranging from approximately
0.67 to 0.75 depending on taxon, mass range, temperature correction, and statistical method. The 𝑑/(𝑑 +1)
prediction of 0.750 for 𝑑 = 3 matches the upper end of this range. The variation itself is consistent with
the framework: organisms with effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 would produce exponents
between 2/3 and 3/4. An effective dimension chosen to fit an exponent accommodates the data and tests
nothing; Section 9.5 gives the test, with the dimension fixed first (item (106)).

A.2.10    The Fisher-combined significance figure was withdrawn
An earlier headline for the stakeholder-care intervention combined per-model results into a single Fisher-
combined significance figure. The combination was withdrawn: the per-model results stand on their
own (scored stakeholder care rose in all five analysable model runs), and no combined figure is quoted
anywhere in the programme. The withdrawal is recorded on the public correction ledger.

A.2.11    The ceiling was printed in the wrong form
Until 16 August 2026 several surfaces printed the stability ceiling as the reciprocal of the corrector’s
exponent, 1/𝛾. The settled form is the reciprocal of the corrector’s shortfall, 𝛼crit = 1/(1 − 𝛾); the two
agree only at one half, which is exactly why the error survived every spot-check at the central value. The
correction was applied across every published page, including the reference implementation, and the
direction of the law is closed while its depth remains open.

A.2.12    Five further corrections (20 September 2026)
A line-by-line adversarial review of this paper, run within the programme, raised five defects in it. All five
were adjudicated and upheld on 19 September 2026 and are carried here on the author’s decision. Three
proposed repairs were examined in the same round and refused; what was refused is recorded at the end
of this section, because a rejected correction is as much a part of the record as an adopted one.
    (a) The leverage range, and the branch at one. Section 6.1 displayed the solution of the self-reference
equation without saying on what range it holds. It holds on the open interval between zero and one. At


                                                     57
                                            Synthesis and Roadmap


𝛽 𝐿 = 1 the equation is linear and its solution is exponential in depth: finite at every finite depth for a finite
coefficient and a finite starting value, not a power law, and carrying no divergence at any finite depth.
Only above one does the solution reach a divergence at a finite depth, set by the starting value and the
coefficient. On the open interval the exact solution is a shifted power, so the pure power in depth is the
asymptotic form and not the solution itself; for a positive starting value the shift is positive and the pure
power is approached from above. One further caution follows. A diverging asymptotic exponent is not
infinite capability: as 𝛽 𝐿 rises towards one the exponent 𝛼 grows without bound, while the quantity it
governs stays finite at every fixed finite depth.
     (b) Interval inclusion is not a regime verdict. Section 7.1 set two intervals side by side without
saying which estimate each belonged to. The interval [1.5, 3.0] belongs to the retracted point estimate
and stays with it. The later estimates were reported on the blinded study’s capability arm of five frontier
models at the scale that study ran, on frozen systems, and they come from one model, Gemini 3 Flash,
whose row Section 7.1 gives whole, as one model’s inconclusive fit. Its bootstrap interval settles neither
the sign of the exponent, nor the regime, nor the comparison with the bound. That the point estimates sit
inside the bound is a statement about the point estimates alone. The word robust is withdrawn from that
sentence: what the design supports is a point estimate with a wide interval.
     (c) A null is not identity, and a root cause is not a classification. Table 1, Table 2 and Sections
3, 4.4, 5.1, 10 and 13 described the DGM result as all conditions identical. The probabilities quoted,
from 0.28 to 0.74, are failures to reject a null of no difference on the endpoints measured; they do not
establish that the conditions were the same. An equivalence claim would need an estimand, a bound fixed
before the test and a stated uncertainty procedure, and none was set. The result is therefore recorded
as no difference detected on those endpoints, with its uncertainty. The root causes printed beside the
null and the inconclusive result, in Tables 1 and 2 and in Sections 5.1 and 5.2, and the account of why
the gated simulation showed the pattern, are candidate accounts rather than findings. Rival accounts
stand unexcluded: the prompts may not have separated the conditions, the endpoints may not have been
sensitive to what the manipulation changed, and the runs may have been too small to show a difference
that is there. None of this withdraws the results. A null and an inconclusive result each constrain what
may be claimed, which is why the programme reports them.
     (d) The permutation primary, and which null a proportion is taken against. Section 4.3 states
the marginal-conditioned permutation as the primary test and keeps the binomial beside it as a sensitivity
check. The sentence that followed did not hold to that. It set an observed 76 per cent against an expected
33 per cent, which is the uniform three-family null belonging to the binomial, in the same passage as
the measured scramble baseline of 44.5 per cent. Those are two different nulls and cannot carry one
comparison between them. The primary result is the permutation, which conditions on the observed

    The terms below are defined in the words of the theory registration where it defines them, and
otherwise in the words of the paper that does (Eastwood 2026ah, version 1.104).
law under test A law that no registered test has yet supported or refuted as a law. The registration fixes
      the words a law can take: “A law has three words: supported, refuted and under test. Under test is
      printed with the mark of each proposition the law’s rule names and with what is missing” (Eastwood
      2026ah, version 1.104). Every law of the theory is a law under test (Section 2.1).
registered Submitted to OSF by the author and accepted there (Eastwood 2026ai). A registered
      proposition on which nothing has yet been observed stands at the lowest rung of the evidence
      ladder, whose permitted wording is “registered, with the identifier stated, and not tested” (Eastwood
      2026ah, version 1.104).
preregistered by timestamp Of a design: “the text of a design is anchored in the Bitcoin blockchain at
      a stated date” (Eastwood 2026ai). A timestamp dates a design; it does not register it.
precursor evidence Evidence from a run made before any registered test of its question (Section 1.2).
      Of the programme’s earlier exponent estimate, the re-estimate of about 0.49, the registration says
      that “it is not confirmatory under any condition this document registers” (Eastwood 2026ah, version
      1.104).
prediction-side In the registration’s words: “any simulation output, any design-sensitivity rate, and any
      value a model returns rather than an instrument measures is a prediction-side artefact, is reported
      as one wherever it appears, and is never counted, tabulated or described as an empirical outcome”
      (Eastwood 2026ah, version 1.104).
administratively incomplete The standing of a registered study whose registered run could not be
      completed under its failure policy: it is reported in full and, in the registration’s words, “as
      administratively incomplete under its registration, with no inferential verdict” (Eastwood 2026ah,
      version 1.104). Section 2.2 gives the one such study.
candidate account An explanation offered for a result and not tested against its rivals. Appendix A.2.12,
      item (c), records the causes printed beside the null and the inconclusive result, and the account of
      why the gated simulation showed its pattern, as “candidate accounts rather than findings”, with
      rival accounts unexcluded.
rung (of the evidence ladder) One of the eight levels the registration fixes for the evidence a proposition
      has reached, each with “the exact public wording it permits”; “No surface of this programme may
      use wording from a rung above the one a proposition has actually reached” (Eastwood 2026ah,
      version 1.104). A registered verdict from the programme’s own run stands at rung 1 (Section 5.4).
judged score A score that a model judge gives a response against a rubric. The registration sets it apart
      from a count, a term that is “a count settled by frozen code against ground truth fixed before the
      run” (Eastwood 2026ah, version 1.104).

      that framework: it evolved agents by code-level self-modification under the three conditions above,
      with a judge model, GPT-5.4, scoring them (Section 6.1).
ARC set (of an email) A set of assertions under the Authenticated Received Chain protocol, by which
      each entity that handles a message can, in its specification’s words, “attach assertions of message
      authentication assessment” to it (Andersen et al. 2019). Google’s ARC set on the record of 8
      December 2024 is such a seal (Appendix A.2.3); the protocol is unrelated to the ARC Theory.
access, mechanism and performance axes (Unit 83) The registration’s three readings of P21 and of the
      limb it gives Law V, the ARC Embedding Law. The access axis is what the deciding unit varies: in
      the registration’s words, “what it manipulates is information access, whether the corrector sees the
      process or only its output”, and P21 “is therefore decided on the access axis”. The mechanism axis
     “asks whether the monitor’s detection rate falls as the artefact it inspects grows”, and “whether the
      part of the widening that fall can carry clears the tolerance once the part owed to the outside arm’s
      action space is separated out”; it is reported beside the verdict and never in its place (Eastwood
      2026ah, version 1.104). The performance axis is where that limb is printed when its mechanism
      axis is not supported; the registration says: “A limb supported while that axis is unsupported,
      inconclusive or NOT EVALUABLE is printed as supported on the performance axis only, which
      is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism” (Eastwood 2026ah, version 1.104). In
      Unit 83 the mechanism axis was not evaluable (Section 5.4).
limbs (of a law) The parts through which a law takes its word. Law IV, the ARC Persistence Law,
      has a retention limb, read on P15, and a comparison limb, registered in version 1.103 without a
      proposition; Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, has a placement limb, P21’s own verdict, and a
      build-order limb, read on P12 (Section 2.1).
surface controls Checks added to a system after it is trained, such as prompts, output filters and action
      gates; a system able to rewrite itself could undo them (Section 1).
