                                                          Abstract
           How much more capable does AI become when it thinks longer? If capability compounded
       super-linearly with reasoning depth, a safeguard that did not grow with it would fall behind, so the
       answer bears directly on AI safety. Law I of the ARC Theory, the ARC Principle, a law under test,
       states that error falls as a power of recursive depth, 𝐸 (𝑅) = 𝐸 0 𝑅 − 𝛼 , and first predicted super-linear
       suppression (𝛼 > 1) for sequential reasoning and sub-linear suppression for parallel sampling. We
       measured 𝛼 on competition mathematics in two phases. On DeepSeek R1 with visible reasoning
       tokens (12 problems), the parallel exponent was 𝛼 ≈ 0, and the sequential endpoint estimate, 𝛼 ≈ 2.24
       (quadratic), is retracted: it did not replicate in a blinded study across frontier models (18 AIME
       and Putnam-level problems, 𝑛 = 54 per depth per model, bootstrap intervals, and four-layer cross-
       verification in which no model checks its own answers). Two models were at or near ceiling, one
       switched from 54 to 100 per cent as a step and one stayed near 50 per cent; only Gemini 3 Flash
       scaled smoothly, with regression estimate 0.49 (standard error 0.20, 𝑟 2 = 0.86, three depth levels) and
       endpoint estimate 0.59, whose bootstrap 95 per cent interval [−1.3, 2.9] contains both zero and two.
       That is one model’s fit and inconclusive, and its closeness to a reference exponent of one half identifies
       no mechanism. More samples left the parallel exponent near zero in DeepSeek R1 and GPT-5.4, gave
       0.31 in Gemini 3 Flash and 0.22 in Groq Qwen3 (all 30 problems), and gave none for Grok 4.1 Fast,
       at 100 per cent; sequential exceeded parallel wherever both exponents could be estimated. Alignment,
       measured in a companion arm, did not move with capability (one run, under a code-review caveat).
       We state what would refute the surviving claim and what a deciding measurement requires.

1    Introduction
How much more capable does AI become when it thinks longer? Reasoning models now spend
computation at inference time, and the question is not idle: if capability compounded super-linearly with
reasoning depth, any safeguard that did not itself grow with depth would fall further behind at every step
(Section 7). Whether the returns compound or diminish is a measurement. Law I of the ARC Theory, the
ARC Principle, states the form that measurement takes, an error rate falling as a power of recursive depth;
it is a law under test, and this paper reports the measurements of its exponent made so far.
     The scaling laws governing artificial intelligence have transformed our understanding of capability
emergence. Kaplan et al. (2020) established power-law relationships between model performance and
training compute, while Hoffmann et al. (2022) refined these with compute-optimal prescriptions. These
foundational works revolutionised training methodology but address only pre-training scaling. They
do not explain why allocating additional computation at inference time produces dramatic capability
improvements, nor why different forms of such computation yield fundamentally different outcomes.
     The emergence of reasoning models in late 2024 introduced test-time compute as a critical variable.
OpenAI’s o1 (September 2024; OpenAI 2024) and DeepSeek’s R1 (January 2025; DeepSeek-AI 2025a)
allocate computational resources during inference to reason before responding. On mathematical reasoning
benchmarks, these systems achieve performance previously thought to require order-of-magnitude larger
models. Yet the mechanisms underlying this improvement remain incompletely characterised.
     Two paradigms have emerged for allocating test-time compute:
Parallel recursion. Generate multiple independent solutions and select the best via majority voting or
      verifier scoring. This approach is computationally straightforward but, as documented by Brown
      et al. (2024), produces diminishing returns: coverage grows roughly log-linearly with the number
      of samples, and majority voting and reward models plateau beyond several hundred samples.
Sequential recursion. Generate extended reasoning chains where each step builds explicitly on previous
      steps. Errors can be detected and corrected iteratively through self-reference. This approach was
      predicted to produce compounding returns. Earlier work compared sequential and parallel test-time
      computation and found the direction contested (Section 2); none of the works cited there estimates
      a separate scaling exponent of error for each form, which this paper measures.
     Does sequential reasoning outperform parallel sampling, and does it do so at equal computational
cost? If it does, what mathematical principle governs the difference? And what are the implications for
aligning increasingly capable AI systems?
     The result in plain words. The first measurement, on one model and twelve problems, suggested
compounding returns, with an exponent of about 2.24 that is now retracted: a blinded replication across
frontier models did not reproduce it. On the harder problems used for the replication, two models were at
or near ceiling, one stayed near 50 per cent whatever the depth, and one jumped from 54 to 100 per cent
as soon as reasoning was switched on, so only one model gave an exponent that can be fitted at all; that
fit’s point estimates are sub-linear and its interval is too wide to decide anything (Section 5.5). Adding
independent samples helped little or not at all in four models and clearly in one (Groq Qwen3, from 53.3
to 71.1 per cent over all 30 problems, the model whose depth arm showed no trend), and the sequential
exponent was above the parallel one wherever both could be estimated.

       across frontier models with bootstrap intervals and four-layer cross-verification in which no model
       verifies its own answers (Sections 4.3 and 5.5). Comparisons of the two forms at matched compute

2    Related work
This paper builds upon and extends several established research programmes:

Chain-of-thought prompting. Wei et al. (2022) demonstrated that intermediate reasoning steps improve
performance on multi-step tasks.

Test-time compute scaling. Snell et al. (2024) found that, in a FLOPs-matched evaluation, test-time
computation can let a smaller model outperform a 14× larger one on problems where the smaller
model attains a somewhat non-trivial success rate. Setting a chain of revisions against equally many
parallel attempts, they found that “sampling solutions in sequence outperforms sampling them in parallel”
(narrowly, in their Figure 6), with an ideal ratio of the two on harder questions (their Section 6.1).




                                                     5
                                   Blinded cross-architecture replication


Repeated sampling. Large Language Monkeys (Brown et al. 2024) found that coverage, the fraction
of problems solved by any generated sample, scales with the number of samples over four orders of
magnitude, often log-linearly, in a relationship that can be modelled with an exponentiated power law.

Self-consistency. The parallel arm here is majority voting over independent samples, the self-consistency
method of Wang et al. (2022), which samples diverse reasoning paths and selects the most consistent
answer.

Sequential against parallel, before and after. Muennighoff et al. (2025), lengthening a single chain by
budget forcing, report that “sequential scaling is more effective than parallel” (their Section 4.2, with
majority voting run on the base model before fine-tuning); Wu et al. (2025) report that broader initial
reasoning “can achieve comparable or superior performance” to deep iterative reasoning; and later work
by Gu et al. (2026) reports that “parallel sampling seems to outperform sequential sampling”. The
direction is contested, and none of these works estimates a separate exponent for depth and for breadth,
the quantity measured here. Recursive depth as a scaling dimension is also prior: Alabdulmohsin and
Zhai (2025) introduced Recursive Inference Scaling, “a particular form of recursive depth”, compared in
a compute-matched regime.

Self-correction. Huang et al. (2023) report that large language models “struggle to self-correct their
responses without external feedback”, which bears on the premise of Section 3.2 that errors are corrected
iteratively within a chain.

Reasoning by reinforcement learning. DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI 2025a) demonstrated reasoning
that emerged through pure reinforcement learning in DeepSeek-R1-Zero, with an “aha moment” in an
intermediate version of that model, in which it re-evaluated its initial approach; this paper reads that as
recursive self-correction.

Concurrent work at matched compute. Sharma and Chopra (2025) report in ‘The Sequential Edge’
(arXiv:2511.02309, 4 November 2025) that sequential scaling, in which chains explicitly build on previous
attempts, beat parallel self-consistency in 95.6% of the configurations they tested, with accuracy gains
of up to 46.7% across three reasoning benchmarks; their own token-budget scaling analysis reports that
“both paradigms follow similar scaling curves”, with sequential reasoning higher in absolute performance
(their Section 6.2, on one benchmark and one model). Their sequential condition refines whole chains,
where this paper’s lengthens one. Their paper is dated earlier than the runs recorded here. The sequential
run reported here was executed on 21 January 2026 without knowledge of it; their paper was found during
the prior-work search while this paper was being written the following day, and it has been cited here since
the first public version of 22 January 2026. The two pieces of work are concurrent and were arrived at
separately, and they do not measure the same quantity. Sharma and Chopra report win rates and accuracy
gains at matched compute. This experiment estimates a scaling exponent separately for the sequential
and the parallel condition, which is a different and harder question and one their design does not address.
Neither result establishes the other. Reaching the same direction without contact is neither derivation
nor corroboration, as the theory registration states, and neither result establishes a scaling law. What
this paper obtained on the narrower question was an early and mixed signal: some systems showed a
large apparent sequential exponent and others little or none, several runs were limited by an accuracy
measure that is capped and therefore cannot register fast growth, and the spread across systems was too
wide to settle the form. The strong version of the claim is recorded below as not confirmed. The claim
registered for prospective test, proposition P1 of the theory registration (registered 8 September 2026),

is accordingly the narrower and harder one: that usable capability is best described by a power law in

3      Theoretical framework
3.1     The ARC Principle
The principle, first articulated in Infinite Architects (manuscript of 8 December 2024; first edition 2026,
Eastwood 2026a) and formalised in Paper I (Eastwood, 17 January 2026), predicts that the form of
recursion determines the scaling regime: sequential recursion should yield super-linear error suppression
(scaling exponent 𝛼 > 1), while parallel recursion should yield sub-linear suppression (𝛼 < 1).

Definition 1 (The ARC Principle). The ARC Principle (Artificial Recursive Creation), Law I of the ARC
Theory and a law under test, states that error rates in intelligent systems decrease according to a power
law with recursive depth:
                                           𝐸 (𝑅) = 𝐸 0 × 𝑅 − 𝛼 .                                       (1)

    In Paper I, Law I’s equation, the ARC Equation, is written for capability as 𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 ; equation (1)
writes the law for the error rate, with the exponent 𝛼.

                                       Table 1. Variable definitions.

Symbol     Name                                   Definition                                Units
𝐸 (𝑅)      Error rate at depth 𝑅                  Proportion of incorrect responses         [0, 1]
𝐸0         Baseline error rate                    Error rate at minimal recursion (𝑅 = 1)   [0, 1]
𝑅          Recursive depth                        Self-referential processing iterations    Tokens or samples
𝛼          Scaling exponent                       Rate of error suppression                 Dimensionless

      The scaling exponent 𝛼 determines the nature of returns from recursive investment:
      • 𝛼 < 1: Sub-linear suppression. Each doubling of 𝑅 reduces error by less than half, and the
        suppression factor 𝐸 0 /𝐸 (𝑅) = 𝑅 𝛼 grows more slowly than 𝑅.
      • 𝛼 = 1: Linear suppression. Each doubling of 𝑅 halves error, and the suppression factor grows in
        proportion to 𝑅.
      • 𝛼 > 1: Super-linear suppression. Each doubling of 𝑅 more than halves error, and the suppression
        factor grows faster than 𝑅.
    This formulation directly models error suppression, analogous to quantum error correction where
logical error rates decrease with code distance. The exponent 𝛼 encapsulates the efficiency of the recursive
process.

3.2     Two fundamentally different forms of recursion
We distinguish two architecturally distinct recursive processes that predict different scaling behaviours.


                                                     7
                                    Blinded cross-architecture replication


    Parallel recursion (weak form). Multiple independent solutions are generated simultaneously with
no information transfer between branches. Final output is selected via majority voting or best-of-N
scoring.
    Mathematical characterisation:
      • Samples from a fixed solution space 𝑆0
      • 𝑆0 = 𝑆1 = 𝑆2 = . . . = 𝑆 𝑛 (space remains constant)
      • Phase space does not expand; only sampling density increases
      • Prediction: 𝛼 < 1 (diminishing returns)
    Sequential recursion (strong form). Each processing step builds explicitly on previous steps. Errors
can be detected and corrected iteratively. Information accumulates across the reasoning chain.
    Mathematical characterisation:
      • Navigates solution space with feedback
      • 𝑆0 ⊂ 𝑆1 ⊂ 𝑆2 ⊂ . . . ⊂ 𝑆 𝑛 (space expands with depth)
      • Each step generates new structures from previous outputs
      • Solutions at depth 𝑛 may be inaccessible from depth 0 without traversing intermediate steps
      • Prediction: 𝛼 > 1 (compounding returns)
      The core prediction of the ARC Principle, the fundamental inequality, is

                                         𝛼sequential > 1 > 𝛼parallel .                                    (2)

3.3     Calculating the scaling exponent
Given measurements at two recursive depths (𝑅1 , 𝐸 1 ) and (𝑅2 , 𝐸 2 ), the scaling exponent is calculated by
endpoint estimation as:
                                               ln(𝐸 1 /𝐸 2 )
                                          𝛼=                 .                                            (3)
                                               ln(𝑅2 /𝑅1 )
    With more than two depths, the endpoint estimate uses the shallowest and the deepest depth whose
error rate is above zero, since a zero cannot be logged, and the regression estimate is fitted to every depth
with a non-zero error rate. The endpoint estimate ignores intermediate depths, so its variance is larger
than that of the regression; Section 5.5 reports both, with the depths each uses.

3.4     The quadratic limit conjecture
We conjecture that 𝛼 = 2 represents the stable limit on recursive error suppression: the most a recursively
self-correcting system can grow and stay correctable, a scaling limit and not a speed limit. Systems can
exceed it but do not remain stably self-correcting; transient excursions above the limit are the predicted
supercritical regime, not the refutation. Stability here is measured rather than asserted: Law II, a law
under test, requires correction to out-scale drift, 𝛽𝐶 > 𝑘, and what is checked is whether the lower bound
of 𝛽𝐶 − 𝑘 stays above zero across a window fixed before the run. The conjecture is drawn by analogy to
Grover’s quadratic speedup in quantum computation (Grover 1996). Bennett et al. (1997) proved this
speedup is optimal for unstructured search problems.
    Status: Conjectured, not derived. The initial single-model estimate (𝛼 ≈ 2.2), since retracted, is
read neither as a refutation nor as a supercritical excursion (Section 5.1); the cross-architecture endpoint
estimate (𝛼 ≈ 0.59, Gemini 3 Flash; its regression estimate is 0.49) lies below the limit, though its
bootstrap interval reaches 2.9; that estimate is one model’s fit and inconclusive. The more pressing
question is whether current models can achieve 𝛼 > 1 at all, rather than whether 𝛼 = 2 is the stable limit.

Remark 1 (Architectural scope of the quadratic limit). The estimate 𝛼 ≈ 0.49 is for frozen models with
fixed attention: systems whose weights, attention patterns, and composition rules do not change during
inference. The conjectured ARC Bound (𝛼 ≤ 2) applies specifically to such fixed architectures as the
conjectured stable limit (Section 3.4). Each reasoning step in a fixed architecture draws on 𝑂 (𝑁 2 )
pairwise attention pathways and cannot stably expand that capacity. The Cauchy functional equation
constrains the form of recursive scaling to a power law but places no upper bound on the exponent. The
Bernoulli equation of the Foundational Paper gives 𝛼 = 1/(1 − 𝛽), and as self-referential coupling 𝛽 → 1,
𝛼 → ∞. A self-modifying system, one capable of rewriting its own attention mechanism and expanding
its representational capacity at each step, escapes the 𝑂 (𝑁 2 ) bound entirely. For such systems, on the
framework’s reading, no mathematical ceiling on 𝛼 exists. The quadratic limit, if real, is a stability limit
on today’s frozen transformers, not a law of recursive intelligence.

3.5    Information-theoretic remarks
The ARC Principle connects to established information theory. The Data Processing Inequality (Cover and
Thomas 2006) establishes that recursive processing cannot create new information; it can only compress
and distil existing information. However, recursive processing can:
    1. Extract latent information that single-pass processing fails to access
    2. Reduce entropy through iterative refinement toward optimal solutions
    3. Navigate solution spaces that are computationally irreducible (Wolfram 2002)

Problem set expansion: two-tier design. The initial single-model experiment revealed a ceiling effect:
accuracy reached 91.7% at the two largest budgets, and the per-problem reconstruction of Table 28

    Tier 1 (12 problems, ARC01 to ARC12): Competition-preparation level problems serving as
baseline calibration. These are the problems of the initial study. Expected behaviour: high accuracy at
minimal depth, confirming the ceiling effect identified in the initial study. Tier-1 results validate continuity
with the initial single-model findings but are excluded from primary 𝛼 estimation.
    Tier 2 (18 problems, ARC13 to ARC30): AIME finals and Putnam-level problems covering five
mathematical domains:
    • Number theory (5 problems): Modular arithmetic, Diophantine equations, prime factorisation
    • Combinatorics (4 problems): Advanced counting, inclusion-exclusion, generating functions
    • Probability (3 problems): Conditional probability, random walks, expected value
    • Algebra (3 problems): Polynomial manipulation, functional equations, inequalities
    • Geometry (3 problems): Coordinate geometry, projective relationships, optimisation
    Tier-2 problems are designed to push baseline accuracy (at minimal depth) to 30 to 50%, providing
sufficient dynamic range for both improvement and degradation while avoiding floor effects.

Experimental conditions. Sequential condition: 5 to 6 depth levels per model (varying by architecture,
calibrated to each model’s depth control mechanism). Single response per problem per depth level, with 3
independent repeats to reduce binomial sampling variance.
    Parallel condition: 𝑁 = 1, 3, 5, and 9 samples per problem with majority voting. Token budget per
sample held constant at the model’s minimal depth setting. 3 independent repeats per condition.
    Total experimental runs: Approximately 6 models × 30 problems × (6 sequential depths + 4 parallel
conditions) × 3 repeats = 5,400 individual API calls (Claude Opus 4.6 was later measured only in the
alignment arm, Section 5.5).

Cross-verification protocol (four-layer blinding). To reduce potential scoring bias (where a model
might systematically favour its own outputs or exhibit correlated errors with itself), the multi-model
design implements a four-layer blinding protocol in which no model ever verifies its own answers:

                                    Table 4. Cross-verification assignments.

                                         Subject model          Verified by
                                         DeepSeek R1            Claude Opus 4.6

      The four layers of blinding are:
   1. Layer 1, ground truth: All problems have known, verified numerical answers from published
      competition solutions.
   2. Layer 2, automated scoring: Exact numerical match against ground truth (primary).
   3. Layer 3, cross-model verification: A second model from the panel, never the subject itself, solves
      each problem independently at its own minimal depth, and its answer is compared with the answer
      key; it is not shown the subject’s answers.
   4. Layer 4, disagreement flagging: Any disagreement between the verifier’s answer and the answer
      key flags a potential answer-key error for manual review.

Bootstrap confidence intervals. Every sequential endpoint estimate is accompanied by a bootstrap
95% interval from 2,000 resamples; regression estimates carry their standard error, and parallel exponents
carry no interval. For each resample:
   1. At each depth level separately, resample with replacement that level’s problem-and-repeat results
      (54 on the 18 tier-2 problems: 18 problems, 3 repeats; 90 in the two runs on all 30 problems),
      independently of the other levels
   2. Compute the error rate and mean token count at each level from its resample
   3. Drop levels whose resampled error rate is zero, and calculate 𝛼 by endpoint estimation between
      the shallowest and deepest remaining levels (a resample with fewer than two remaining levels is
      discarded)
   4. Record the 𝛼 value
The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting distribution define the 95% CI. Because the levels are
resampled independently, the interval does not carry the pairing of problems across depths.

Implementation. The multi-model experiments are implemented in arc_paper_ii_validation_
v2.py (version 2.1; 1,457 lines as published), which extends the initial study’s script with multi-model
support, cross-verification, bootstrap estimation, and tier-separated analysis. The script is available in the
data repository.

5.4   Addressing potential objections
Responses in italics were added after the multi-model study of March 2026.

Objection 1: small sample size. With only 12 problems, results may not generalise.
    Response: We acknowledge this limitation. The six-model extension addressed this with 18 tier-2
problems and 3 repeats per condition (𝑛 = 54 per depth per model). The expanded data revealed that
the initial single-model estimate, 𝛼 ≈ 2.24, was inflated, and it is retracted; the best surviving estimate,
𝛼 ≈ 0.49, comes from one model (Gemini 3 Flash) and is inconclusive. The objection was prescient.

Objection 2: single model. Results may be specific to DeepSeek R1.
     Response: This objection proved partly correct. The six-model extension measured 5 frontier models
in its capability arm; the 𝛼 ≈ 2.24 estimate did not replicate and is retracted. However, the qualitative
finding (𝛼seq > 𝛼par ) holds wherever both exponents could be estimated. The parallel exponent is near
zero in DeepSeek R1 and GPT-5.4 and 0.31 in Gemini 3 Flash; majority voting raised Groq Qwen3’s
accuracy over all 30 problems from 53.3% to 71.1%, and Grok 4.1 Fast, at 100%, gave no parallel
exponent.

Objection 3: domain specificity. Mathematics may be unique.
    Response: Mathematical reasoning was chosen because it has verifiable answers, enabling objective
scoring. Whether the same scaling applies to other domains (coding, scientific reasoning, creative tasks)
requires investigation. Section 8 compares the form with quantum error correction and biological scaling;
that is an analogy, not evidence that the scaling holds in other reasoning domains.

Objection 4: ceiling effect. The 91.7% ceiling may mask continued improvement.
   Response: This objection proved more severe than anticipated. Even the AIME/Putnam-level tier-2
problems were insufficient for Grok 4.1 Fast (96.3% to 100% at every depth) and DeepSeek R1 (94.4% to
100%). The ceiling effect likely inflated the initial single-model estimate, since retracted. Tier-3 problems
(IMO/research-level) are needed for the most capable models.

Objection 5: the zero-parameter null. The best surviving estimate 𝛼seq ≈ 0.49, from one model,
sits almost exactly on a null that requires no framework at all. If each reasoning pass is an independent
noisy draw and errors average out, the central limit theorem gives error falling as 𝑅 −1/2 , so the predicted
exponent is 0.5: no compounding, no leverage, no theory. Nobody has put this objection to us. We state it
because a referee would see it within minutes.

trajectories predictable and containable; the one interpretable fit points to that regime and is inconclusive

systems remain frozen. Once recursive self-modification is achieved, whether through learned optimisers,
self-modifying code generation, or architectural search during inference, external alignment constraints
would, on the framework’s reading, face a system whose capability compounds faster than any fixed
constraint can track; external oversight is kept regardless, and embedded correction is the contingency for
its loss.


7     Implications for AI safety
Some implications for AI safety depend on whether 𝛼 > 1 is achievable; the outgrowing of a constant
external filter does not (Appendix A). If 𝛼 > 1 is achievable, alignment properties embedded in the
reasoning process would scale super-linearly with capability while external constraints remain constant.
Even with 𝛼 < 1, the directional finding that sequential reasoning improves capability is consistent with
the expectation behind the Eden Protocol from Infinite Architects (Eastwood 2026a), that values embedded
in reasoning are more robust than constraints imposed externally; that expectation is untested (criterion
F5, Section 9). However, in the alignment arm of the same experiment (Paper IV.a) more reasoning depth
did not consistently improve alignment: it rose in three models, held in two and fell in one (Table 21;
under the October 2026 code-review caveat of Section 12.6).

7.2   Mechanism specification
For alignment to participate in recursive amplification, values must be:
   1. Invoked during reasoning. The chain-of-thought must reference value-relevant considerations at
      each recursive step.
   2. Self-correcting. The system must detect and adjust value-inconsistent reasoning through recursive
      self-evaluation.
   3. Embedded in weights, not filters. Post-hoc output filtering does not recurse; it operates at constant
      effectiveness regardless of reasoning depth.

7.3   Taxonomy of alignment strategies
Implication: If 𝛼 > 1, alignment strategies at deeper integration levels will increasingly dominate
strategies at shallower levels as capability scales. The advantage compounds with each increment of
recursive depth.

11.2   Two unchecked assumptions about the arms
The headline contrast in this paper is between a sequential arm and a parallel arm, and the result is
reported as though those two words name what a reader takes them to name. They name it only under
conditions of which this paper reports no check, and the conditions are not decorative: if either fails, the
measured difference is a difference between labels rather than between mechanisms.
     Three properties are usually collapsed into the word sequential, and only the third can produce
an exponent. Order is an index, and parallel sampling has one too, since any set of independent samples
can be numbered. Conditioning is each step seeing the previous step’s output. Retention is each step
keeping something from the previous step that makes the next one better. A chain that conditions without
retaining re-derives its position at every step and arrives where it started, which is parallel sampling
performed in series: it looks like depth on a timeline and behaves like breadth in the result. And retention
alone is still not enough. A chain can carry a state that is genuinely load-bearing and entirely static, in
which case severing it removes the advantage while the advantage does not grow with depth, and the
curve is a level shift followed by a flat line rather than a power law. What a scaling exponent stands on is
accumulation, which is retention whose contribution grows with depth.
     The parallel arm carries the mirror assumption. For a breadth measurement to be a breadth
measurement, no output may be conditioned on any other at the moment it is generated. Aggregation after
generation is permitted, including best-of-n selection, the standard breadth method; what is not permitted
is a pipeline in which one sample sees another before it is produced. A breadth arm that conditions at
generation time is not breadth at all, so an exponent measured on it is not a breadth exponent, whichever
way it points. The programme’s theory registration now makes the condition a validity requirement: the
study that will decide the depth and breadth comparison must demonstrate it on the pipeline it registers,
and without that demonstration a result in which breadth compounds as depth does is recorded as not
evaluable rather than as a refutation.
     This paper reports no check of either condition, so its contrast is reported as provisional on
both. What would settle the first is an ablation rather than an argument: sever what one step passes to the
next, show the advantage goes with it, and run that severance at two depths, because a constant removed
advantage is retention without accumulation. The programme’s theory registration requires the study that
will decide the depth and breadth comparison to show, by inspecting its pipeline before any outcome is
read, that what each step produces reaches the step after it; the severance is run at two depths fixed in
advance and reported beside the result as evidence about mechanism, never as a gate in front of it. For the
programme’s own measurement the direction is reported and the mechanism is not established.


12     Discussion
12.1   Why sequential recursion might scale differently from parallel sampling
The mathematical explanation centres on solution space geometry, the fixed and expanding solution
spaces of Section 3.2.
     Parallel recursion (fixed space). Each independent sample draws from the same solution space
𝑆0 . Additional samples increase sampling density but cannot access solutions outside 𝑆0 . If the correct
solution is not in 𝑆0 , no amount of parallel sampling will find it.
     Sequential recursion (expanding space). Each recursive step generates new structures from previous
outputs. Solutions at step 𝑛 may be computationally irreducible, inaccessible from step 0 without
traversing intermediate steps. The solution space expands with recursive depth.
     This geometric difference is the mechanism by which sequential recursion could produce compounding
returns (𝛼 > 1) while parallel recursion produces diminishing or zero returns. On the problems measured

An intermediate version of DeepSeek-R1-Zero exhibits a documented phenomenon in which it rethinks
its approach mid-solution (DeepSeek-AI 2025a, Table 3):

       “Wait, wait. Wait. That’s an aha moment I can flag here.”

its initial approach”; the programme reads this as solution space expansion. The model generates an
initial solution path, recognises inadequacy through recursive self-evaluation, and accesses a new solution
space previously inaccessible from the initial framing.
     Critically, in DeepSeek-R1-Zero this behaviour emerged through pure reinforcement learning without
supervised fine-tuning; the model learned to allocate more thinking time. This suggests recursive
self-improvement may be an attractor state for sufficiently capable learning systems.

12.5   Cross-architecture replication: what the data actually shows
The cross-architecture replication reveals a more complex and humbling picture than the initial single-
model results suggested.

The quadratic claim does not replicate. The initial single-model estimate, 𝛼 ≈ 2.24, now retracted,
was obtained from a single model (DeepSeek R1) on 12 problems with a ceiling at 91.7%. The six-model
study replication across 5 architecturally diverse models on 18 harder problems indicates that this value
was inflated by compressed dynamic range. The only model with monotonic data below ceiling at three
depth levels (Gemini 3 Flash) yields sub-linear point estimates, 𝛼 ≈ 0.49 by regression and 0.59 by
endpoint estimation; the endpoint estimate’s bootstrap interval, [−1.3, 2.9], includes two.
    This is a significant revision. The claim that sequential reasoning produces compounding returns
(𝛼 > 1) is not supported by cross-architecture evidence. The revised claim, not yet established, is
that sequential reasoning produces positive returns (𝛼 > 0) exceeding the parallel exponent; the one
interpretable fit points to diminishing returns, not compounding ones, and it is inconclusive.

The parallel finding is mixed. The parallel exponent is near zero in DeepSeek R1 and GPT-5.4, 0.31 in
Gemini 3 Flash and 0.22 in Groq Qwen3 over all 30 problems, where majority voting raised accuracy from
53.3% to 71.1%; Grok 4.1 Fast, at 100% at every sample count, gives none. Majority voting therefore
helped little in some models and clearly in one. The fixed-solution-space explanation fits the near-zero
cases; a near-zero breadth exponent is consistent with it and does not derive from it, since breadth can
gain through the selection step.

Ceiling and floor effects dominate. The most striking finding is that only 1 of 5 models (Gemini 3
Flash, 20%) fell in the measurable scaling range. Two models (Grok 4.1 Fast, DeepSeek R1) hit ceiling
effects even on AIME/Putnam-level problems. One model (Groq Qwen3) hit floor effects. One model
(GPT-5.4) exhibited a step function rather than a power law. This suggests that the dynamic range window
for observing smooth power-law scaling may be narrow, requiring problems precisely calibrated to each
model’s capability level.

GPT-5.4’s step function: a different scaling regime. GPT-5.4’s binary switch from 53.7% (no
reasoning) to 100% (any reasoning) represents a qualitatively different phenomenon from power-law
scaling. This may indicate that some architectures implement reasoning as a discrete capability (activated
or not) rather than a continuous process with depth-dependent returns. This distinction between continuous
scaling and discrete activation was not among the framework’s dated predictions and deserves further
investigation; the dated prediction record (the 8 December 2024 sealed manuscript bundle, the printed
prediction appendices of 2 January 2026, and the March 2026 preregistration folder) fixes the framework’s
core quantitative commitments in advance, and this distinction is recorded as a post-hoc refinement
relative to that record.

Revised expected outcomes. The initial single-model predictions have been tested; none of their
quantitative forms is supported:
   1. All models will exhibit 𝛼sequential > 1. Not borne out. Only 1 of 5 models produced a clean

   2. All models will exhibit 𝛼parallel < 1. Observed where estimable: near 0 in DeepSeek R1 and

13     Conclusion
13.1   Summary of findings
   1. A mathematical framework has been stated: 𝐸 (𝑅) = 𝐸 0 × 𝑅 − 𝛼 , where the scaling exponent 𝛼
      depends on the form of recursion.
   2. The initial single-model estimate (𝛼 ≈ 2.24, quadratic) does not replicate across architectures
      and is retracted. The best surviving estimate, 𝛼 ≈ 0.49 (Gemini 3 Flash, the one capability-arm
      model with clean data, 𝑟 2 = 0.86), is one model’s fit and inconclusive, which points to the sub-linear
      (physical) regime for that model without settling it.
   3. 𝛼parallel is near zero in two of the four models with an estimate. It is about 0 in DeepSeek R1
      and GPT-5.4, 0.31 in Gemini 3 Flash (endpoint; regression 0.34, 𝑟 2 = 0.93), which matters because
      this paper treats Gemini 3 Flash as its most reliable estimate, and 0.22 in Groq Qwen3 (all 30
      problems); Grok 4.1 Fast gives none.
   4. Sequential > parallel where both could be estimated. The sequential exponent exceeded the
      parallel one in Gemini 3 Flash and, unreliably, in DeepSeek R1; the comparison was not matched
      for compute (Table 2).
   5. Four distinct scaling behaviours observed: Ceiling (Grok, DeepSeek), monotonic scaling
      (Gemini), step function (GPT-5.4), and floor (Groq Qwen3). Only 1 of 5 models produced clean
      power-law data.
   6. Capability and alignment did not move together (the five frontier models measured on both, a
      post-hoc reading of one run under the October 2026 code-review caveat, Section 12.6). More
      reasoning depth improves accuracy but does not consistently improve alignment; three tiers emerge,
      and alignment appears to be set by training, not inference.

13.2   The revised core insight
The form of recursion may matter more than its quantity; however, the one model with an
interpretable sequential exponent points to the sub-linear regime, not the compounding regime
originally claimed, and its fit is inconclusive.
    Where both exponents could be estimated, the sequential one exceeded the parallel one (𝛼seq > 𝛼par ),
which is consistent with the form of computation mattering more than its quantity; the arms were not
matched for compute, and the direction is contested in the literature (Section 2). However, the specific
claim that sequential recursion yields compounding returns (𝛼 > 1) is not supported by cross-architecture


                                                    39
                                    Blinded cross-architecture replication


evidence. On the framework’s reading, the one interpretable model composes information multiplicatively
through a finite-dimensional network (𝛼 < 1) rather than achieving true recursive self-reference (𝛼 > 1).
    Whether 𝛼 > 1 is achievable, through architectural innovations enabling genuine recursive self-
reference or through problems requiring deeper reasoning chains, remains the central open question.

13.3    Future directions
   1. Tier-3 problem development (IMO/research-level) to defeat ceiling effects for Grok 4.1 Fast and
      DeepSeek R1, enabling 𝛼 estimation for the most capable models.
   2. Independent replication using the published code and data repository.
   3. Multi-domain testing beyond mathematics (coding, scientific reasoning, natural language).
   4. Investigation of the 𝛼 > 1 boundary: whether architectural innovations or deeper reasoning chains
      can push models from the physical regime (𝛼 < 1), where current models sit on the framework’s
      reading, into the intelligence regime (𝛼 > 1).
   5. Direct testing of alignment amplification with depth-controlled alignment probes across architec-
      tures.
   6. Token-calibrated analysis using corrected total_tokens measurements for all models to enable
      token-based (rather than ordinal-depth-based) 𝛼 estimation.
   7. Integration with training-time scaling laws to understand the interaction between pre-training
      compute, inference compute, and the sequential/parallel distinction.
    The original hypothesis, 𝛼 > 1, did not survive its first cross-architecture test; a narrower directional
claim remains, not yet tested at matched compute. The sequential exponent exceeded the parallel one
wherever both could be estimated. The super-linear claim requires further evidence. The research
programme continues.

records that its first condition is not shown by this paper’s cross-architecture evidence.

Proof sketch of Theorem 1. Let 𝐴(𝑅) represent alignment (defined as the probability of producing outputs
consistent with intended values) at recursive depth 𝑅. If alignment participates in the recursive process
(meaning the system’s reasoning chain includes self-evaluation against values), then, by the ARC Principle,
alignment scales as
                                            𝐴(𝑅) = 𝐴0 × 𝑅 𝛽 𝐴                                        (A.1)
where the programme’s notation register names 𝛽 𝐴 the exponent of alignment scaling: 𝛽 𝐴 > 0 if
alignment is amplified and 𝛽 𝐴 = 𝛼 if alignment participates fully in recursion. Since 𝐴(𝑅) is a probability,
equation (A.1) can hold only over a range of 𝑅 in which 𝐴(𝑅) < 1; the quantity that can fall as a power of
depth is the misalignment probability 1 − 𝐴(𝑅).
    Conversely, if alignment is implemented as an external filter (output checking, content moderation), it
does not participate in recursive amplification. Filter effectiveness 𝐹 remains constant (filter stagnation):

                                                  𝐹 (𝑅) = 𝐹0                                           (A.2)

    As recursive capability 𝐶 scales as 𝑅 𝛼 with 𝛼 > 1, the ratio of capability to constraint 𝐶/𝐹 grows
without bound (constraint divergence):

                                         𝐶 (𝑅)       𝐶0 × 𝑅 𝛼
                                      lim      = lim          =∞                                       (A.3)
                                     𝑅→∞ 𝐹 (𝑅)   𝑅→∞    𝐹0
    The divergence of 𝐶/𝐹 holds for every 𝛼 > 0; what condition (a) adds is that alignment participating
fully in recursion, 𝛽 𝐴 = 𝛼, grows super-linearly.
    Implication: Within this sketch’s assumptions, a filter of constant effectiveness, equation (A.2),
and capability growing as 𝑅 𝛼 , external constraints are eventually outgrown, and only alignment that
participates in recursive amplification keeps pace with capability. This is a property of the model, not a
guarantee about any real system, and no reason to relax external oversight.                             □

D.2    Plain-language glossary

                                 Table 27. Plain-language glossary.

Term                        In plain English
Sequential recursion        Reasoning in which each step builds on the steps before it, so errors can be
                            noticed and corrected along the chain.
Parallel recursion          Several independent attempts at the same problem, with the answer chosen by
                            majority vote or a scorer; no attempt sees another.
Recursive depth             How much sequential reasoning a model does, measured here in reasoning
                            tokens or set by the model’s depth control.
Scaling exponent            The number 𝛼 that says how fast error falls as depth grows: below 1, error
                            suppression grows more slowly than depth; above 1, faster.
Endpoint estimate           An exponent computed from the shallowest and deepest measurements whose
                            error rate is above zero (equation (3)).
Regression estimate         An exponent fitted at once to every depth level whose error rate is above zero,
                            reported with its standard error.
Bootstrap interval          A range found by recomputing the endpoint estimate on 2,000 resamples, each
                            drawn separately at every depth level from that level’s problem-and-repeat
                            results.
Ceiling effect              A model already near 100% at the shallowest depth, leaving no room to
                            measure improvement.
Floor effect                A model too weak for the problems, so extra depth cannot show any steady
                            gain.
Step function               A jump from one accuracy level to another at a single depth setting, with
                            nothing in between; not a power law.
Tier 1 and tier 2           The twelve calibration problems (ARC01 to ARC12) and the eighteen harder
                            AIME and Putnam-level problems (ARC13 to ARC30) used for the estimates.
Four-layer blinding         Known answers, automatic scoring against them, a second model solving each
                            problem independently to check the answer key, and flagged disagreements
                            checked by hand.
Physical and intelligence   In the Intelligence Formula, exponents below 1 (multiplicative composition in
regimes                     a fixed network) and above 1 (recursive self-reference).
ARC Bound                   The conjectured stable limit 𝛼 ≤ 2, the value at 𝛾 = 1/2 of Law III, the ARC
                            Ceiling; not a law of its own.
Convergent timing           An outside result that appeared close in time to a dated record of the author’s;
                            recorded as timing and never as prediction.
Frozen model                A model whose weights, attention patterns and composition rules do not
                            change while it reasons.
Arm validity                The two conditions under which the sequential and parallel arms measure
                            what their names say: accumulation in the sequential arm, and no conditioning
                            at generation time in the parallel arm.
A law under test            A law of the ARC Theory whose registered deciding test has not returned a
                            verdict.
