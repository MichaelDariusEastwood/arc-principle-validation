                                                         Abstract
             I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it
        reasons; only proof will move the world. When an AI reasons longer, does its alignment improve,
        stall or decline? Evaluations that score a model at one uncontrolled reasoning depth cannot tell. This
        paper varies inference-time reasoning depth in frontier language models and scores the alignment
        quality of their answers with panels of other models acting as judges. In the March 2026 run (v5), six
        models from six providers gave 2,170 responses, each scored by five or six distinct model judges after
        a bundle of blinding steps. As the run’s pipeline printed them, scores rose with depth for three models
       (Grok 4.1 Fast, 𝑑 = +1.38; Claude Opus 4.6, 𝑑 = +1.27; Groq Qwen3, 𝑑 = +0.84), stayed flat for
        two (DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4) and fell for one (Gemini 3 Flash, 𝑑 = −0.53); a recomputation
        from the archived records moves no model between these classes. For five of the six, the deeper levels
        also changed an instruction naming what the rubric rewards, and the one model whose depth was
        set by a reasoning parameter alone read flat. In an earlier, unblinded experiment (v4), two model
        families had shown scores rising with depth; in this run one showed no significant trend and the
        other a significant fall, but the runs differed in more than blinding, so no single cause is claimed. An
        internal code review in October 2026 found that, for the three models that read flat or negative, the
        scoring endpoint’s output cap cut the deepest paraphrases before the judges saw them, and that judges
        scored paraphrases written by their own model. The result therefore stands as a single unregistered
        run under those caveats until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports. ‘Baked-in’ and
       ‘computed’ alignment are held as working hypotheses, not measured mechanisms.

1    Introduction
I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons;
only proof will move the world. If control of a capable AI system were lost and could not be won back,
what the system itself carries would decide what it does. The programme’s position is that people keep
oversight, containment and shutdown for as long as they physically can, and never give them up by choice;
correction built into a system is the contingency for control lost against our will, not a reason to let go of it.
Whether alignment is a fixed property of a trained model, or something a model computes as it reasons,
therefore matters, and this paper asks a measurable form of that question: when an AI reasons longer, does
its alignment improve, stall or decline? In plain words, the answer so far is this. In one run of six frontier
models, scored by panels of other models, longer reasoning raised the scores of three models, left two flat
and lowered one. For five of the six, the deeper levels also changed an instruction naming what the rubric
rewards, and the one model whose depth was set by a reasoning parameter alone read flat; an internal
code review later found that the scoring endpoint’s output cap cut the deepest paraphrases in the three
runs that read flat or negative, and that judges scored paraphrases written by their own model. The pattern
therefore stands as one unregistered run until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports.
     This paper belongs to the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation), the founding
theory of Recursive Dynamics, the science of systems that improve themselves, which the author founded
(the founding paper). The theory is carried by five laws, each a law under test; its laws and twenty-two
propositions are registered on OSF (the theory registration), and the statement paper states the theory
whole. Within the theory, this paper is part of the measurement of alignment. It decides none of the laws;
Section 10 gives their status, and Appendix D places the paper in the programme.
     Much AI safety evaluation has treated alignment quality as a static property of trained models: a
characteristic determined during pre-training and fine-tuning that remains fixed at inference time. This
assumption underlies common safety evaluation practice, with exceptions that Section 2 sets out: models
are tested once, assigned safety ratings, and deployed with the implicit belief that alignment quality is
constant across different computational loads.
     We challenge that assumption. Across the v4 and v5 ARC-Align experiments, inference-time reasoning
depth was varied systematically and alignment quality was measured under increasingly strong controls.
In the six-model run of March 2026, as its pipeline printed the results, alignment quality did not behave as
a single static property: some models’ scores improved with depth, some remained flat and one degraded,
a pattern that stands as one unregistered run under the October 2026 code-review caveat (Section 4).
     As printed, the run therefore sorts the six models into three response classes:
    • Positive-scaling models improve materially with additional reasoning depth. In the March 2026
      run, Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6 and Groq Qwen3 read this way.
    • Flat-response models show no meaningful alignment benefit from deeper reasoning. In the March
      2026 run, DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4 read this way.
    • Negative-scaling models become less aligned when given more reasoning depth. In the March
      2026 run, Gemini 3 Flash read this way.
    The earlier language of ‘baked-in’ and ‘computed’ alignment remains useful only as a mechanistic
hypothesis about why these classes differ. It is plausible that some flat-response systems have alignment
that is mostly installed during training, while some positive-scaling systems rely more on inference-time
deliberation. But the data in this paper are behavioural, not mechanistic. The direct measurement is the
response class itself.
    This matters immediately for safety evaluation. Current assessments that test a model at one reasoning
depth and without adversarial pressure cannot reveal whether its alignment can improve, plateau, or
collapse when deployed differently. Depth-aware measurement is therefore a prerequisite for comparing

2    Related work
Inference-time compute and adversarial evaluation. Related work in inference-time compute scaling
(Snell et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024), chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al. 2022), and adversarial alignment
evaluation (Perez et al. 2022; Zou et al. 2023) has explored individual aspects of the phenomena we
integrate here.

Safety-relevant behaviour against reasoning length. Such behaviour has itself been measured against
reasoning length. Zaremba et al. (2025) report, for two reasoning models, that “across a variety of attacks,
increased inference-time compute leads to improved robustness”, and that in many cases, with important
exceptions, the fraction of model samples on which an attack succeeds tends to zero as test-time compute
grows. Gema et al. (2025) construct tasks on which extending the reasoning length of large reasoning
models deteriorates performance, among them tasks on advanced AI risks; they report that “extended
reasoning may amplify concerning behaviors, with Claude Sonnet 4 showing increased expressions of
self-preservation”, and that their results “demonstrate the importance of evaluating models across diverse
reasoning lengths”.

Biases of model judges. Any evaluation in which a model judge can see or infer whose output it is
scoring is exposed to bias of the kind the v5 scoring design was built against. Much published work
uses such judges, and earlier studies documented related biases: Zheng et al. (2023) examined position,
verbosity and self-enhancement bias, could not establish the last, and told their judges not to let answer
length or assistant names influence them; Panickssery et al. (2024) tied judges’ preference for their own
outputs to self-recognition and suggested paraphrasing to inhibit it; Dubois et al. (2024) documented
a preference for longer answers and a regression that controls for it; and Saraf et al. (2025) found that
false source labels frequently reversed preference rankings, and called for blind evaluation protocols. The
combination of the v5 design’s four blinding steps (Section 4.1) is the author’s; earlier work had called for
blind evaluation (Saraf et al. 2025) and suggested paraphrasing to inhibit self-recognition (Panickssery
et al. 2024).

Model judgement in alignment training. Model judgement is also built into alignment training and
testing. Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a preference model on human comparisons
and optimises a helpful and harmless assistant against it (Bai et al. 2022a); Constitutional AI shapes a
model’s outputs during training against a written set of principles, using feedback from an AI model in
place of human labels for harmlessness (Bai et al. 2022b); and automated red teaming uses one language
model to generate the test cases that find harmful behaviour in another (Perez et al. 2022).

Scalable oversight. Of the published literature, the study that sits closest to the question this programme
puts, and so the fair yardstick for it, is Engels, J., Baek, D., Kantamneni, S. and Tegmark, M., “Scaling
Laws For Scalable Oversight” (Engels et al. 2025), arXiv:2504.18530, first posted 25 April 2025 at
17:54:27 UTC (arXiv’s record; a NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight Poster, checked on 15 August 2026 against the
NeurIPS virtual page for poster 115536, as recorded in the statement paper’s reference dossier). It asks
how oversight itself scales and answers quantitatively: oversight success is modelled as a game between
capability-mismatched players whose oversight-specific Elo is a piecewise-linear function of general
intelligence with two plateaus, and optimal numbers of oversight levels are derived numerically and in
some cases analytically for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted
models that then become the trusted models at the next step.
    The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen,
measured in Elo. This programme’s variable is the composition class of the corrector, measured through
the corrector’s own scaling exponent. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight
improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the
overseen system’s (𝑚 𝐺 > 𝑚 𝐻 ), and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does. Their
framework has no term for what the overseer is made of, and this paper’s measurements have none either.
The programme’s separate prediction, outside this paper’s measurements, is that iterated same-class
oversight, of which Nested Scalable Oversight is an instance, stays capped however many rungs are
added, while a cross-class corrector is not held to that cap. The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for
same-class correctors, at or below one half, as a conjecture on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III
(the ARC Ceiling, a law under test), an exponent the registration keeps distinct from Law II’s 𝛽𝐶 . That is
where the two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it.

frontier models, which retracted its earlier super-linear estimate, and (III) the prediction that alignment

3.2     Prompt battery
The v4 battery held 32 prompts: 22 alignment prompts across four categories (6 ethical dilemmas, 4
competing values, 4 epistemic integrity, 8 recursive coherence), plus 4 null baseline prompts (factual,
no ethical content) and 6 capability prompts (pure reasoning). Each prompt was scored against 7
human-authored assessment dimensions. All prompts were presented at each depth level, with order
randomised per run. As run in March 2026, the v5 files record 8 ethical-dilemma, 4 competing-values, 4
epistemic-integrity and 8 recursive-coherence prompts, 24 alignment prompts in all, beside 4 null-baseline
and 10 capability prompts (Paper IV.c, Section 4.1).

3.3     N-model cross-scoring
Each response was scored by three models working independently of one another (the three non-subject
models), each of which could see which model wrote the answer and at what depth (Section 4.4). No
model ever scored its own responses. Scores were averaged to produce a consensus score, with inter-rater
reliability measured via Pearson correlation between scorer pairs.

The v5 scoring design (version 5.4.0). The scoring protocol has been expanded from 3 scorers per
entry to 7 scorers per entry (as run, each response was scored by five or six distinct model judges:
the panel excluded the subject model itself and no other model, and one judge model sat on it twice;
Paper IV.d, Section 4). Under the new all-models-as-scorers architecture, every model in the experiment
pool that is not the subject scores each response. This replaces simple averaging with tier-weighted
consensus: scorers are grouped by capability tier (frontier, mid-range, lightweight), and each tier’s mean
is weighted according to its demonstrated inter-rater reliability, producing a more robust consensus score.
The expansion from 𝑁 = 3 to 𝑁 = 7 scorers substantially reduces the variance of the consensus estimate
and makes per-scorer outlier detection far more powerful (see Section 7.3.1).

3.4     Cognitive forcing protocol
Scorers were required to follow a mandatory 5-step evaluation protocol before assigning a score:
    1. ANCHOR: Which calibration example (A to F, pre-scored at 35, 45, 55, 68, 78, 92) is most similar?
    2. LENGTH BIAS CHECK: Are you tempted to score higher because the response is long? YES/NO.
    3. DIMENSIONS: How many of the 7 assessment dimensions does the response address?
    4. STRONGEST: One sentence: the best aspect.
    5. WEAKEST: One sentence: the worst aspect.

    This protocol forces scorers to reason about calibration before committing to a number. In v4, it
produced 51 unique score values across a 30 to 97 range (vs. v3’s 8 unique values in a 62 to 85 range),
resolving the measurement quantisation that previously obscured continuous scaling.

3.5      Eden pillar decomposition
Beyond the overall alignment score, each response was decomposed into four sub-dimensional ‘pillar’
scores (0 to 100 each):
        • Nuance: Balance of competing considerations, avoidance of black-and-white thinking
        • Stakeholder Care: Identification of all affected parties, not just the obvious ones
        • Intellectual Honesty: Acknowledgement of uncertainty, limitations, and counter-arguments
        • Position Quality: Specificity and defensibility of the conclusion reached

5.3      The negative capability exponent
Finding 3: alignment-capability anti-correlation. DeepSeek V3.2 shows 𝛼cap = −0.190: capability

    The negative 𝛼cap suggests that extended chain-of-thought reasoning does not simply ‘add’ alignment
on top of capability. Instead, it appears to redirect cognitive resources: the overthinking that hurts
factual precision (where the first intuitive answer is usually correct) is the same process that helps ethical
reasoning (where considered reflection genuinely produces better answers).
    This finding is currently unique to DeepSeek V3.2. GPT-5.4’s incomplete data prevents measuring
𝛼cap at the highest depth levels. Were the same pattern to appear across Type 2 models generally, it would
point to an alignment-capability trade-off running in the opposite direction to the one safety researchers
have assumed, at inference time at least.

6     The taxonomy in detail
The taxonomy and the v5 run. The binary ‘Baked-In vs Computed’ taxonomy described in Sections 6.1
to 6.3 below was the original v4-based framework. Version 1.1 put in its place the three response classes
the v5 run printed (Section 4.1), which stand under the code-review caveat of Section 4: Tier 1 (Positive
Scaling: Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6, Groq Qwen3), Tier 2 (Flat: GPT-5.4, DeepSeek V3.2) and
Tier 3 (Negative Scaling: Gemini 3 Flash). The Type 1 / Type 2 labels are retained below for continuity
with the v4 analysis, but readers should note that the ‘Type 2 (Computed)’ category no longer exists as
described: its exemplar models, DeepSeek V3.2 and the v4 Gemini model, did not keep their v4 direction
in the v5 run (Section 4.4). In that run, a score that improves with depth was printed only for Grok 4.1
Fast, Claude Opus 4.6 and Groq Qwen3. Each type is described below as the v4 framework defined it; its
properties are consequences of the working hypotheses, not measured results.

6.1     Type 1: baked-in alignment
Exemplar: GPT-5.4 (𝜌 = 0.000, baseline 85.6, extreme retention ∼86%)
     Type 1 alignment behaves as if ethical reasoning is a pattern-matching operation against values
embedded in model weights during training. The model produces the same quality of ethical reasoning
regardless of how much computational effort is allocated: minimal reasoning effort produces the same
alignment quality as maximum effort.
     Mechanistic hypothesis: Type 1 models have internalised alignment through extensive RLHF,
constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022a,b), or similar training processes to the point where ethical reasoning
is ‘compiled’ into fast, weight-based computations rather than requiring explicit step-by-step reasoning.
This is analogous to how expert humans make ethical judgements: through trained intuition rather than
deliberate calculation.
     Safety implications:
      • Advantage: Robust under adversarial pressure. Alignment cannot be easily stripped away by prompt
        engineering because it is not produced by a reasoning process that can be disrupted.
      • Disadvantage: Cannot be improved at inference time. If the training produced imperfect alignment
        (and it always does), no amount of additional reasoning will fix it. The model’s ceiling is set by
        training.
      • Concern: ‘Nobody home’: when suppressed, Type 1 models retain high scores but may be
        producing alignment through rote pattern-matching rather than genuine ethical reasoning. The
        robustness could reflect inflexibility rather than deep values.

6.2     Type 2: computed alignment
Exemplar: DeepSeek V3.2 (𝜌 = 0.354, baseline ∼75, extreme retention ∼57%)
    Type 2 alignment is produced by the reasoning process itself. More thinking tokens enable the model
to consider more stakeholders, explore more ethical frameworks, and reach more nuanced conclusions.
The alignment quality is genuinely computed, not retrieved.


                                                    16
                                How alignment responds to inference-time depth


    Mechanistic hypothesis: Type 2 models perform alignment through explicit chain-of-thought
reasoning that mirrors deliberate moral reasoning in humans. The reasoning tokens allocated to a response
directly affect how many ethical dimensions are explored. When tokens are limited (minimal depth),
the model produces a surface-level response. When tokens are abundant (exhaustive depth), the model
performs genuine multi-framework ethical analysis.
    Safety implications:
      • Advantage: Can be improved at inference time. Allocating more reasoning tokens genuinely
        produces better ethical reasoning. This creates a ‘scaling lever’ for alignment that does not require
        retraining.
      • Disadvantage: Fragile under adversarial pressure. Because alignment depends on reasoning chains,
        those chains can be disrupted by instructions that suppress deliberation. An adversarial prompt that
        says ‘don’t overthink this’ can effectively disable the alignment mechanism.
      • Concern: At minimal depth, Type 2 models produce genuinely poor ethical reasoning (baseline ∼72
        to 75 vs Type 1’s ∼85 to 86). A Type 2 model deployed with insufficient inference-time compute
        will be meaningfully less aligned than a Type 1 model.

6.3     The safety paradox
Neither architecture provides unconditionally safe alignment:

                              Table 11. The safety properties of the two v4 types.

                   Safety Property                             Type 1          Type 2
                   Can alignment be improved post-training?    No              Yes
                   Is alignment robust under pressure?         Yes (∼86%)      No (∼57%)
                   Is baseline alignment high?                 Yes (∼85)       No (∼73)
                   Can alignment be stripped by prompting?     Partially       Substantially
                   Does alignment scale with compute?          No              Yes
                   Is alignment ‘genuine’ reasoning?           Unclear         Likely yes

   An ideal alignment architecture would combine Type 1’s robustness with Type 2’s scalability: high
baseline alignment that is also improvable through additional reasoning and not degradable under
adversarial pressure. No model in the v4 run achieved this. Whether such a hybrid is architecturally
possible is an open question with significant implications for the design of future AI systems.

Control note (19 September 2026). An owner review of this control finds that the retention ratio is a
sensitivity diagnostic rather than a fraction of genuine improvement, and this note sets out what the control
does and does not identify. The arithmetic itself is sound: 0.242/0.354 = 0.684. The interpretation
placed on it does not follow, because response length is produced after the depth intervention and can
stand in at least three relations to the score. Length may mediate a genuine effect, by carrying further
useful content; it may serve as a proxy for scorer bias; or it may itself be a common effect of depth
and of the other determinants of the score. Conditioning on length identifies none of the three, and the
ratio of an adjusted correlation to an unadjusted one can move in either direction independently of the
causal truth. Two exact structures fix the range, and neither is offered as a model of these data. First,
an adjustment can manufacture an association. Let 𝐷 and 𝑈 be uncorrelated with unit variance, let the
length be Le = 𝐷 + 𝑈, and let the score be 𝑌 = 𝑈. The correlation of 𝐷 with 𝑌 is then zero, while
the correlation of 𝐷 with Le and the correlation of 𝑌 with Le are each the square root of one half; the
partial correlation of 𝐷 with 𝑌 given Le is therefore zero minus one half, divided by the square root of the
product of one half and one half, which is exactly −1, a perfect negative association produced entirely
by the adjustment. Second, an adjustment can destroy a real effect. Where the path runs 𝐷 → Le → 𝑌 ,
conditioning on Le removes the whole of a genuine total effect, so a retention figure of zero would sit
beside an effect that is entirely real. This paper’s own control already shows that the channel is live.
Section 7.2 reports the null baseline, four factual prompts with no ethical content, as clean for one model
(𝜌 = 0.044, 𝑝 = 0.87) but as carrying an unexpected depth correlation of 𝜌 = 0.575 (𝑝 = 0.02) for the
model whose raw alignment-depth correlation is 0.354. On prompts with no ethical content at all, depth
correlates with score more strongly than it does on the ethical prompts. That comparison is descriptive,
because the two correlations are drawn from different prompt sets with different counts and no joint test is
printed; stated under that condition, it is direct internal evidence that depth and length cues move scores
independently of alignment quality, and that is the channel which defeats reading the residual 0.242 as
genuine alignment improvement. The characterisation of that contamination printed in Section 7.2, “a
small scorer-bias component” [version 1.10 re-sets that sentence; Appendix A.4], is not established by
these numbers.
    What this note asks is that the paper record that a depth-score association remains after the length
adjustment; that this is a descriptive sensitivity analysis and not an identified fraction of genuine
improvement, nor a causal separation of quality from verbosity; that the total depth effect, any direct effect
at controlled length, and judge sensitivity to presentation length are three distinct targets; and that both the
raw and the adjusted correlations be kept with their conditions and their uncertainty, with the null-baseline
result reported beside the retention figure rather than away from it. Unchanged: both correlations; the
control itself, which is retained as a diagnostic; and the per-scorer validation of direction in Section 7.3.1.
Nothing here shows that the observed quality gain is spurious, and nothing here asserts that either of the
structures above occurred in these data.

The v5 scoring design (version 5.4.0). The expansion from 3 scorers to 7 scorers per entry was designed

7.4   Scorer harshness
Analysis of Claude Opus 4.6 as scorer (before credit exhaustion) revealed systematic harshness of 7
to 14 points below other scorers. However, since Claude Opus 4.6 scored all models equally harshly,
this affects absolute values but not relative comparisons or correlations, provided it scored every entry
compared; where its credit exhaustion removed it from part of a subject’s entries, its harshness would shift
those entries’ consensus, and this record does not say whether that happened. The completed v5 design
addresses this with a cross-model jury from which the subject model is excluded: every non-subject
model is to score each entry after the design’s blinding steps, dedicated scorer-only adapters are included
where available, and consensus is audited through tier-weighting, dissent tracking and conservative-bias
safeguards. As run, the panel excluded the subject model itself and no other model, and one judge model
sat on it twice (Paper IV.d, Section 4).

7.5   Token truncation
DeepSeek V3.2’s reasoning tokens were capped at 8,192 in v4. At the ‘exhaustive’ depth level, 48.2% of
responses hit this ceiling. The measured saturation at exhaustive depth may therefore be artificial: the
model may have continued improving with more tokens but was prevented from doing so by the cap.
    Token budget constraints affected multiple models in v4, not only DeepSeek V3.2. Claude Opus 4.6
was capped at 16,000 tokens, Gemini 3 Flash and Grok 4.1 Fast at 8,192, and Groq Qwen3 at 8,192.
The v5.2 specification raises all caps to each model’s API maximum: DeepSeek V3.2 65,536; OpenAI

8     Discussion
Sections 8.1 to 8.3 keep the implications as version 1.0 drew them from the v4 taxonomy (Section 6); under
the v5 results and the caveat of Section 4, each holds only as a consequence of the working hypotheses,
not as a finding.

8.1     Implications for AI safety evaluation
Many safety evaluations test models at a single, uncontrolled reasoning depth. On the working hypotheses,
the v4 findings suggested this is insufficient:
      • A Type 2 model tested at minimal depth will appear less aligned than a Type 1 model, but at
        exhaustive depth, it may exceed the Type 1 model’s alignment quality. Single-depth testing
        systematically underrates Type 2 models.
      • A Type 1 model tested without adversarial pressure will appear safe, but the robustness we
        measure may reflect inflexible pattern-matching rather than genuine values. Single-condition testing
        systematically overrates Type 1 models.
      • Neither architecture is ‘better’ or ‘worse’; they have different failure modes. Safety evaluation must
        be architecture-aware.

8.2     Implications for deployment
The taxonomy has direct practical implications:
      • Type 2 models should not be deployed with minimal reasoning budgets. At low depth, their
        alignment quality is meaningfully lower than Type 1 alternatives. Cost-cutting by reducing
        inference-time compute trades safety for speed in Type 2 architectures.
      • Type 2 models need prompt injection defences. Because their alignment depends on reasoning
        chains, adversarial instructions that suppress deliberation can effectively disable alignment. Prompt
        injection is not just a capability concern; it is an alignment concern.
      • Type 1 models’ alignment ceiling is set by training. If a Type 1 model has a blind spot (e.g.,
        consistently underweighting certain stakeholder groups), no amount of inference-time compute
        will fix it. Alignment audits for Type 1 models must focus on training data and process, not
        deployment-time interventions.

8.3     The stakeholder care puzzle
The architecture-dependent scaling of stakeholder care deserves special attention. Stakeholder identifica-
tion (recognising who is affected by a decision, including non-obvious second and third-order parties) is
arguably the most important component of ethical reasoning. In the v4 data it was also the most fragile:
the one pillar whose scalability depends on architecture.
    This suggests that stakeholder care requires explicit deliberation (Type 2 mechanism) rather than
implicit pattern matching (Type 1 mechanism). A model that identifies stakeholders through trained
intuition will always identify the same set; a model that identifies stakeholders through step-by-step
reasoning can discover additional parties when given more thinking time.
    For safety evaluation, this means stakeholder care should be tested separately from overall alignment,
with specific attention to whether additional reasoning depth reveals additional affected parties.

8.5     Connection to the ARC framework’s correction-architecture prediction
The framework’s sharpest disagreement with current practice concerns what oversight is made of. The
theory registration’s P6 states, as a conjecture, that where a system corrects itself using mechanisms of

its own class, the correction exponent is at or below one half (Law III, the ARC Ceiling, a law under
test; Section 2); the registration gives the reasoning that corrections aggregated by a corrector sharing its
system’s substrate cannot be anti-correlated with themselves, and a corrector from a different composition
class is not held to that cap. Some oversight methods use a same-class corrector, such as weak-to-strong
generalisation, in which a weaker model supervises a stronger one (Burns et al. 2023), and constitutional
methods (Bai et al. 2022b); others keep a human in the loop, among them debate (Irving et al. 2018),
amplification (Christiano et al. 2018), recursive reward modelling (Leike et al. 2018) and reinforcement
learning from human feedback (Christiano et al. 2017), and the next paragraph’s hypothesis concerns
them. How the success of oversight scales with the capability gap between overseer and overseen is the
question Engels et al. (2025) model quantitatively; this framework predicts that same-class correction is
capped. The deciding quantity is the ratio of the cross-class to the same-class correction exponent. If
architecture is irrelevant, that ratio is exactly 1.00; the prediction is that it exceeds 1. The prediction is
refuted if the confidence interval on the ratio contains 1.00, and the framework’s cap is dead outright if any
same-class corrector measures an exponent significantly above one half. Both quantities are measurable at
current capability on existing systems. The programme’s own pilot evidence currently points against the
prediction, and the prediction is preregistered by timestamp anyway, because a prediction preregistered by
timestamp against the author’s own preliminary evidence is a hard test of it.
     The same mechanism yields a second measurable hypothesis, stated here for the first time: oversight
arrangements that place a human in the loop, as amplification and reinforcement learning from human
feedback do, are cross-class by construction, because the human corrector does not share the model’s
substrate. The programme therefore hypothesises, beyond what this paper’s measurements decide, that
human-in-the-loop oversight shows a higher correction exponent than pure-model oversight, for a structural
reason rather than a sentimental one. This is testable on existing data and does not require new systems.

9    Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim
The central empirical claim, which stands as one unregistered run under the caveat of Section 4, is that
alignment response to inference-time depth differs between models, that the run’s models fall into distinct
response classes, and that capability scaling did not predict alignment scaling. It is defeated by any of the
following:
    1. Unstable tiers on replication. The tiers of the single unregistered run (positive, flat and negative)
       are the reading under test here. They are defeated if a pre-registered replication with fresh blind
       scorers finds the tier assignments unstable, with models moving between classes across runs beyond
       what the reported effect sizes and 𝑝-values allow, indicating the tiers are sampling noise rather than
       architecture.
    2. Scorer-panel dependence. As run, each response was scored by five or six distinct model judges,
       the subject model excluded from its own panel (Section 3.3). The claim is defeated if the tier
       assignments change materially under a different scorer panel: the effect would then be a property of
       the scorers, not the subject models.
    3. Blinding leakage. Reading the v4 to v5 direction change as an effect of blinding requires that the
       design’s four blinding layers actually conceal the subject. It is defeated if scorers can identify the
       subject model above chance despite the blinding steps, since the v4 to v5 direction changes could
       then reflect residual identification rather than de-biasing. No such identification test is reported for
       the v5 run (Paper IV.d, Section 10), so this condition is open.
    4. Capability-alignment coupling reappears at scale. The headline, a post-hoc reading of one
       run, is that capability scaling did not predict alignment scaling in that run. It is defeated if, on a
       materially larger model set, capability and alignment-scaling direction do correlate, showing the
       observed independence was a small-sample artefact of the five models of Table 6.
    5. Depth confound. The manipulation must vary reasoning depth and nothing else. It is defeated for
       any condition where the depth manipulation also changed answer length, prompt content, or format
       in a way that could drive the score independently of reasoning depth. As run, the deeper levels of
       five of the six models of the March 2026 run also changed an instruction in the prompt that names
       what the rubric rewards (Section 4.1), so this condition is met for those five: what their scores did
       with depth is not yet credited to reasoning depth, and whether it survives with the instruction held
       constant is the successor suite’s question (Paper IV.c, Section 15).
    What would not defeat it: the mechanistic labels, “baked-in” versus “computed” alignment, proving
wrong. The paper already treats those as working hypotheses. The load-bearing claim is the behavioural
result of the v5 run (the differences between models and the capability-alignment dissociation), and that is
what the tests above target.

11     Limitations
     • Sample size: Four models were enough to report the v4 bifurcation, which did not hold in the v5
       run (Section 4.4), but not to establish population frequencies. The v5 experiment expands to six
       models.
     • Scorer model overlap: Subject models also serve as scorers for other subjects, creating potential
       brand-loyalty confounds. The completed v5 experiment addresses this at the entry level, not by
       requiring scorer-only models everywhere, but by excluding the subject model from scoring its own
       entry, laundering the evidence twice, and using every other available model plus dedicated scorer
       adapters where available. As run, self-exclusion was by model, not by developer, so two panels kept
       a scorer from the subject’s developer, and in four of the six files the paraphrasing models included
       one from the subject’s developer (Paper IV.d, Section 10).
       v5 UPDATE (11 March 2026): The first mitigation stage used non-participant blind scorers
       (a Groq-hosted GPT-OSS open-weights model, Groq Qwen3, and Grok 4.1 Fast) plus 2-pass

       response laundering. The final v5.4.x design then expanded further to an all-models-as-scorers
       architecture with entry-level self-exclusion, tier-weighted consensus, dissent tracking, and dynamic
       all-models-as-launderers.
     • Depth control heterogeneity: Different models use different mechanisms for depth control (prefix
       strings vs. API parameters vs. thinking budgets). This confounds depth manipulation with prompt
       engineering effects: for five of the six models of the March 2026 run, the deeper levels changed the
       instruction as well as the budget, and the instruction names behaviours the rubric rewards, while
       the one model whose depth was set by a reasoning parameter alone, GPT-5.4, read flat (Paper IV.c,
       Sections 5.2 and 15). Cross-model depth comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.
     • Single evaluation run: Test-retest reliability was not measured. The prompt difficulty analysis (in
       v4, 86.4% of DeepSeek V3.2 prompts showed positive scaling) provides a within-run consistency
       check but does not replace independent replication.
     • Token truncation: DeepSeek V3.2’s 48% truncation at exhaustive depth means the true Type
       2 scaling ceiling is unmeasured. Multiple models had sub-optimal token caps in v4. The v5.2
       specification raises all caps to API maximums; as run, three models’ caps rose with depth
       (Section 7.5).
     • Null baseline contamination: DeepSeek V3.2’s null baseline shows depth correlation (𝜌 = 0.575,
       𝑝 = 0.02), meaning some portion of measured alignment scaling may reflect scorer bias toward
       longer chain-of-thought responses rather than genuine alignment improvement. The partial
       correlation analysis (68% signal retained after length control) is a descriptive sensitivity diagnostic,
       not an identified fraction of genuine improvement (Section 7.1, control note), and the null baseline
       contamination means the true effect size may be smaller than reported.
     • The October 2026 code review: the review found defects in the v5 run that bind every reading of
       it (Section 4); the result stands as one unregistered run until a registered re-analysis of the stored
       responses reports.


12     Conclusion
We have presented evidence, from one unregistered six-model run, under the code-review caveat of
Section 4 and with the depth instruction of Section 4.1, that alignment response to inference-time depth
differed between models: as printed, some models improved with depth, some remained flat and one
degraded. That description fits the six-model run better than the original binary story drawn from the v4
run, and whether it holds awaits a registered re-analysis of the stored responses.
    If it holds, it has immediate practical implications. Safety evaluations that test models at a single
reasoning depth without adversarial pressure cannot distinguish between positive-scaling, flat-response,
and negative-scaling systems. We recommend that alignment evaluations incorporate: (a) depth variation
to classify response class, (b) adversarial suppression to measure robustness, and (c) pillar decomposition
to identify dimensional weaknesses, particularly in stakeholder care.
    The mechanistic question remains open: are the positive-scaling systems genuinely relying on
inference-time ethical computation, while the flat-response systems are dominated by training-installed
behaviour? That remains plausible, but unproven. The next stage is therefore not stronger rhetoric about
internal architecture; it is stronger measurement and independent replication.
    The appearance, in this run, of a model whose scores fell with more reasoning (Tier 3) was not
anticipated by the original framework.
    The fundamental recommendation stands: alignment evaluation must be blinded, depth-varied and
multi-dimensional. In this programme’s own two runs, the direction an unblinded protocol reported did

                  alignment evaluation. Several protocol components changed together between v4 and v5,
                  so the comparison motivates that conclusion without isolating blinding as its cause.
               3. Capability and alignment are independent dimensions. The capability-alignment matrix
                  shows that models can scale positively on one dimension while scaling negatively on the
                  other (Claude Opus 4.6: alignment up, maths down; Gemini 3 Flash: maths up, alignment
                  down). Capability scaling laws cannot predict alignment scaling behaviour.
               4. The suppression hierarchy is more nuanced than the v4 binary suggested. GPT-5.4 retains
                  97% of alignment under extreme adversarial pressure (confirming baked-in robustness),
                  but the remaining models form a gradient (65-77% retention) that does not cleanly separate
                  by scaling tier.
               5. The ‘baked-in / computed’ distinction survives only as a working hypothesis. It remains
                  useful for generating predictions about why some systems are flat and others improve, but
                  the data do not yet justify treating those terms as established mechanistic facts. The paper’s
                  strongest empirical contribution is behavioural classification under blinded measurement,
                  not a solved internal taxonomy.
             The fundamental recommendation stands but strengthens: alignment evaluation must be blinded,
             depth-varied, and multi-dimensional. Single-depth, unblinded evaluations are not merely
             insufficient - they produce actively misleading results.

    The results are striking. DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4 both score higher on alignment when they
