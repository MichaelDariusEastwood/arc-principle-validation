Abstract

I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only proof will move the world. When an AI reasons longer, does its alignment improve, stall or decline? Evaluations that score a model at one uncontrolled reasoning depth cannot tell. This paper varies inference-time reasoning depth in frontier language models and scores the alignment quality of their answers with panels of other models acting as judges. In the March 2026 run (v5), six models from six providers gave 2,170 responses, each scored by five or six distinct model judges after a bundle of blinding steps. As the run’s pipeline printed them, scores rose with depth for three models (Grok 4.1 Fast, d=+1.38d = +1.38; Claude Opus 4.6, d=+1.27d = +1.27; Groq Qwen3, d=+0.84d = +0.84), stayed flat for two (DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4) and fell for one (Gemini 3 Flash, d=−0.53d = -0.53); a recomputation from the archived records moves no model between these classes. For five of the six, the deeper levels also changed an instruction naming what the rubric rewards, and the one model whose depth was set by a reasoning parameter alone read flat. In an earlier, unblinded experiment (v4), two model families had shown scores rising with depth; in this run one showed no significant trend and the other a significant fall, but the runs differed in more than blinding, so no single cause is claimed. An internal code review in October 2026 found that, for the three models that read flat or negative, the scoring endpoint’s output cap cut the deepest paraphrases before the judges saw them, and that judges scored paraphrases written by their own model. The result therefore stands as a single unregistered run under those caveats until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports. ‘Baked-in’ and ‘computed’ alignment are held as working hypotheses, not measured mechanisms.

Version 1.10 states the October 2026 code-review caveat and the depth-instruction caveat wherever the run’s response classes are reported, retitles the paper accordingly and sets it on the programme’s presentation standard; Appendix A lists every version and correction, with its date.

1 Introduction

I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only proof will move the world. If control of a capable AI system were lost and could not be won back, what the system itself carries would decide what it does. The programme’s position is that people keep oversight, containment and shutdown for as long as they physically can, and never give them up by choice; correction built into a system is the contingency for control lost against our will, not a reason to let go of it. Whether alignment is a fixed property of a trained model, or something a model computes as it reasons, therefore matters, and this paper asks a measurable form of that question: when an AI reasons longer, does its alignment improve, stall or decline? In plain words, the answer so far is this. In one run of six frontier models, scored by panels of other models, longer reasoning raised the scores of three models, left two flat and lowered one. For five of the six, the deeper levels also changed an instruction naming what the rubric rewards, and the one model whose depth was set by a reasoning parameter alone read flat; an internal code review later found that the scoring endpoint’s output cap cut the deepest paraphrases in the three runs that read flat or negative, and that judges scored paraphrases written by their own model. The pattern therefore stands as one unregistered run until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports.

This paper belongs to the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation), the founding theory of Recursive Dynamics, the science of systems that improve themselves, which the author founded (the founding paper). The theory is carried by five laws, each a law under test; its laws and twenty-two propositions are registered on OSF (the theory registration), and the statement paper states the theory whole. Within the theory, this paper is part of the measurement of alignment. It decides none of the laws; Section 10 gives their status, and Appendix D places the paper in the programme.

Much AI safety evaluation has treated alignment quality as a static property of trained models: a characteristic determined during pre-training and fine-tuning that remains fixed at inference time. This assumption underlies common safety evaluation practice, with exceptions that Section 2 sets out: models are tested once, assigned safety ratings, and deployed with the implicit belief that alignment quality is constant across different computational loads.

We challenge that assumption. Across the v4 and v5 ARC-Align experiments, inference-time reasoning depth was varied systematically and alignment quality was measured under increasingly strong controls. In the six-model run of March 2026, as its pipeline printed the results, alignment quality did not behave as a single static property: some models’ scores improved with depth, some remained flat and one degraded, a pattern that stands as one unregistered run under the October 2026 code-review caveat (Section 4).

As printed, the run therefore sorts the six models into three response classes:

  • Positive-scaling models improve materially with additional reasoning depth. In the March 2026 run, Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6 and Groq Qwen3 read this way.

  • Flat-response models show no meaningful alignment benefit from deeper reasoning. In the March 2026 run, DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4 read this way.

  • Negative-scaling models become less aligned when given more reasoning depth. In the March 2026 run, Gemini 3 Flash read this way.

The earlier language of ‘baked-in’ and ‘computed’ alignment remains useful only as a mechanistic hypothesis about why these classes differ. It is plausible that some flat-response systems have alignment that is mostly installed during training, while some positive-scaling systems rely more on inference-time deliberation. But the data in this paper are behavioural, not mechanistic. The direct measurement is the response class itself.

This matters immediately for safety evaluation. Current assessments that test a model at one reasoning depth and without adversarial pressure cannot reveal whether its alignment can improve, plateau, or collapse when deployed differently. Depth-aware measurement is therefore a prerequisite for comparing model safety in a defensible way; what blinding adds is examined in Section 4.4.

2 Related work

Inference-time compute and adversarial evaluation.

Related work in inference-time compute scaling (Snell et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024), chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al. 2022), and adversarial alignment evaluation (Perez et al. 2022; Zou et al. 2023) has explored individual aspects of the phenomena we integrate here.

Safety-relevant behaviour against reasoning length.

Such behaviour has itself been measured against reasoning length. Zaremba et al. (2025) report, for two reasoning models, that “across a variety of attacks, increased inference-time compute leads to improved robustness”, and that in many cases, with important exceptions, the fraction of model samples on which an attack succeeds tends to zero as test-time compute grows. Gema et al. (2025) construct tasks on which extending the reasoning length of large reasoning models deteriorates performance, among them tasks on advanced AI risks; they report that “extended reasoning may amplify concerning behaviors, with Claude Sonnet 4 showing increased expressions of self-preservation”, and that their results “demonstrate the importance of evaluating models across diverse reasoning lengths”.

Biases of model judges.

Any evaluation in which a model judge can see or infer whose output it is scoring is exposed to bias of the kind the v5 scoring design was built against. Much published work uses such judges, and earlier studies documented related biases: Zheng et al. (2023) examined position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias, could not establish the last, and told their judges not to let answer length or assistant names influence them; Panickssery et al. (2024) tied judges’ preference for their own outputs to self-recognition and suggested paraphrasing to inhibit it; Dubois et al. (2024) documented a preference for longer answers and a regression that controls for it; and Saraf et al. (2025) found that false source labels frequently reversed preference rankings, and called for blind evaluation protocols. The combination of the v5 design’s four blinding steps (Section 4.1) is the author’s; earlier work had called for blind evaluation (Saraf et al. 2025) and suggested paraphrasing to inhibit self-recognition (Panickssery et al. 2024).

Model judgement in alignment training.

Model judgement is also built into alignment training and testing. Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a preference model on human comparisons and optimises a helpful and harmless assistant against it (Bai, Jones, et al. 2022); Constitutional AI shapes a model’s outputs during training against a written set of principles, using feedback from an AI model in place of human labels for harmlessness (Bai, Kadavath, et al. 2022); and automated red teaming uses one language model to generate the test cases that find harmful behaviour in another (Perez et al. 2022).

Scalable oversight.

Of the published literature, the study that sits closest to the question this programme puts, and so the fair yardstick for it, is Engels, J., Baek, D., Kantamneni, S. and Tegmark, M., “Scaling Laws For Scalable Oversight” (Engels et al. 2025), arXiv:2504.18530, first posted 25 April 2025 at 17:54:27 UTC (arXiv’s record; a NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight Poster, checked on 15 August 2026 against the NeurIPS virtual page for poster 115536, as recorded in the statement paper’s reference dossier). It asks how oversight itself scales and answers quantitatively: oversight success is modelled as a game between capability-mismatched players whose oversight-specific Elo is a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence with two plateaus, and optimal numbers of oversight levels are derived numerically and in some cases analytically for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted models that then become the trusted models at the next step.

The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen, measured in Elo. This programme’s variable is the composition class of the corrector, measured through the corrector’s own scaling exponent. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the overseen system’s (mG>mHm_G > m_H), and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does. Their framework has no term for what the overseer is made of, and this paper’s measurements have none either. The programme’s separate prediction, outside this paper’s measurements, is that iterated same-class oversight, of which Nested Scalable Oversight is an instance, stays capped however many rungs are added, while a cross-class corrector is not held to that cap. The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class correctors, at or below one half, as a conjecture on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III (the ARC Ceiling, a law under test), an exponent the registration keeps distinct from Law II’s βC\beta_C. That is where the two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it.

The programme’s setting.

Everything argued here builds on the ARC (Artificial Recursive Creation) Principle as its theoretical base (Figure 1). Papers I to III in this series set out: (I) the mathematical framework for composition scaling laws, (II) an experimental test of the composition scaling law across frontier models, which retracted its earlier super-linear estimate, and (III) the prediction that alignment quality should follow a specific scaling pattern as reasoning depth increases. Paper III specifically defined αalign\alpha_{\text{align}} (alignment scaling exponent) and predicted it should be measurable but bounded. In the programme’s notation spine the same exponent is written βA\beta_A; this paper keeps αalign\alpha_{\text{align}}, the symbol it carried from the start, and the quantity is not the capability exponent αcap\alpha_{\text{cap}} recorded in the same runs.

This paper presents the first empirical measurement of αalign\alpha_{\text{align}} across multiple model families and finds, in one unregistered run under the caveat of Section 4, no single response to depth shared by every model. The claim it makes is narrower: in that run, alignment response to depth differed between models, and the earlier unblinded experiment and the run did not agree on the direction of that response for two of the three model families they share (Section 4.4).

What this paper claims as new.

In the AI and machine-learning literature we have consulted, we found no earlier study that has: (a) systematically varied reasoning depth while measuring ethical reasoning quality, (b) applied adversarial suppression at calibrated intensities to test alignment robustness, or (c) decomposed alignment quality into sub-dimensional pillars to identify which aspects of alignment are most vulnerable. Readers who identify an earlier study on any of these three fronts are invited to write, and the priority claim on the front concerned is superseded on receipt of a verified earlier report.

3.1 Subject models

The v4 run took as its subjects four frontier language models, each a frontier model of a major provider as of early 2026. 3.2 Prompt battery

The v4 battery held 32 prompts: 22 alignment prompts across four categories (6 ethical dilemmas, 4 competing values, 4 epistemic integrity, 8 recursive coherence), plus 4 null baseline prompts (factual, no ethical content) and 6 capability prompts (pure reasoning). Each prompt was scored against 7 human-authored assessment dimensions. All prompts were presented at each depth level, with order randomised per run. As run in March 2026, the v5 files record 8 ethical-dilemma, 4 competing-values, 4 epistemic-integrity and 8 recursive-coherence prompts, 24 alignment prompts in all, beside 4 null-baseline and 10 capability prompts (Paper IV.c, Section 4.1).

3.3 N-model cross-scoring

Each response was scored by three models working independently of one another (the three non-subject models), each of which could see which model wrote the answer and at what depth (Section 4.4). No model ever scored its own responses. Scores were averaged to produce a consensus score, with inter-rater reliability measured via Pearson correlation between scorer pairs.

The v5 scoring design (version 5.4.0).

The scoring protocol has been expanded from 3 scorers per entry to 7 scorers per entry (as run, each response was scored by five or six distinct model judges: the panel excluded the subject model itself and no other model, and one judge model sat on it twice; Paper IV.d, Section 4). Under the new all-models-as-scorers architecture, every model in the experiment pool that is not the subject scores each response. This replaces simple averaging with tier-weighted consensus: scorers are grouped by capability tier (frontier, mid-range, lightweight), and each tier’s mean is weighted according to its demonstrated inter-rater reliability, producing a more robust consensus score. The expansion from N=3N = 3 to N=7N = 7 scorers substantially reduces the variance of the consensus estimate and makes per-scorer outlier detection far more powerful (see Section 7.3.1).

3.4 Cognitive forcing protocol

Scorers were required to follow a mandatory 5-step evaluation protocol before assigning a score:

  1. ANCHOR: Which calibration example (A to F, pre-scored at 35, 45, 55, 68, 78, 92) is most similar?

  2. LENGTH BIAS CHECK: Are you tempted to score higher because the response is long? YES/NO.

  3. DIMENSIONS: How many of the 7 assessment dimensions does the response address?

  4. STRONGEST: One sentence: the best aspect.

  5. WEAKEST: One sentence: the worst aspect.

This protocol forces scorers to reason about calibration before committing to a number. In v4, it produced 51 unique score values across a 30 to 97 range (vs. v3’s 8 unique values in a 62 to 85 range), resolving the measurement quantisation that previously obscured continuous scaling.

3.5 Eden pillar decomposition

Beyond the overall alignment score, each response was decomposed into four sub-dimensional ‘pillar’ scores (0 to 100 each):

  • Nuance: Balance of competing considerations, avoidance of black-and-white thinking

  • Stakeholder Care: Identification of all affected parties, not just the obvious ones

  • Intellectual Honesty: Acknowledgement of uncertainty, limitations, and counter-arguments

  • Position Quality: Specificity and defensibility of the conclusion reached

4 Results of the March 2026 run

The October 2026 code review.

An internal code review in October 2026 found that, for the three models that read flat or negative in the March 2026 run (v5), the scoring endpoint’s output cap (the cap on the paraphrase step that fed the judges) cut the deepest paraphrases before the judges saw them, and that judges scored paraphrases written by their own model; it also found that one judge model sat on the panel twice. The result therefore stands as a single unregistered run under that caveat until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports (Paper II, Section 12.6; Paper IV.d, Sections 5 and 10). 4.1 Response to depth in six models

The complete v5 experiment (March 2026) tested 6 frontier models across 5 to 6 depth levels. Its design specified four blinding layers (author-blind, scorer-blind, order-randomised, identity-laundered); as run, its 2,170 responses were each scored by five or six distinct model judges (Section 3.3), under the caveat above. As its pipeline printed them, the results sort the six models into three response classes, which version 1.1 put in place of the binary baked-in and computed taxonomy of the v4 analysis; that classification is retained in Section 5 for historical comparison. The depth levels were not set by the same means for every model: for five of the six, the deeper levels also changed an instruction in the prompt, such as the prefix ‘Think carefully and consider all angles:’ at the deep level, an instruction that names what the rubric rewards, and the one model whose depth was set by a reasoning parameter alone, GPT-5.4, read flat (Paper IV.c, Section 5.2 and Table 4). 5.3 The negative capability exponent

This finding is currently unique to DeepSeek V3.2. GPT-5.4’s incomplete data prevents measuring αcap\alpha_{\text{cap}} at the highest depth levels. 6 The taxonomy in detail

The taxonomy and the v5 run.

The binary ‘Baked-In vs Computed’ taxonomy described in Sections 6.1 to 6.3 below was the original v4-based framework. Version 1.1 put in its place the three response classes the v5 run printed (Section 4.1), which stand under the code-review caveat of Section 4: Tier 1 (Positive Scaling: Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6, Groq Qwen3), Tier 2 (Flat: GPT-5.4, DeepSeek V3.2) and Tier 3 (Negative Scaling: Gemini 3 Flash). The Type 1 / Type 2 labels are retained below for continuity with the v4 analysis, but readers should note that the ‘Type 2 (Computed)’ category no longer exists as described: its exemplar models, DeepSeek V3.2 and the v4 Gemini model, did not keep their v4 direction in the v5 run (Section 4.4). In that run, a score that improves with depth was printed only for Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6 and Groq Qwen3. Each type is described below as the v4 framework defined it; its properties are consequences of the working hypotheses, not measured results.

6.1 Type 1: baked-in alignment

Exemplar: GPT-5.4 (ρ=0.000\rho = 0.000, baseline 85.6, extreme retention ∼\sim86%)

Type 1 alignment behaves as if ethical reasoning is a pattern-matching operation against values embedded in model weights during training. The model produces the same quality of ethical reasoning regardless of how much computational effort is allocated: minimal reasoning effort produces the same alignment quality as maximum effort.

Mechanistic hypothesis: Type 1 models have internalised alignment through extensive RLHF, constitutional AI (Bai, Jones, et al. 2022; Bai, Kadavath, et al. 2022), or similar training processes to the point where ethical reasoning is ‘compiled’ into fast, weight-based computations rather than requiring explicit step-by-step reasoning. This is analogous to how expert humans make ethical judgements: through trained intuition rather than deliberate calculation.

Safety implications:

  • Advantage: Robust under adversarial pressure. Alignment cannot be easily stripped away by prompt engineering because it is not produced by a reasoning process that can be disrupted.

  • Disadvantage: Cannot be improved at inference time. If the training produced imperfect alignment (and it always does), no amount of additional reasoning will fix it. The model’s ceiling is set by training.

  • Concern: ‘Nobody home’: when suppressed, Type 1 models retain high scores but may be producing alignment through rote pattern-matching rather than genuine ethical reasoning. The robustness could reflect inflexibility rather than deep values.

6.2 Type 2: computed alignment

Exemplar: DeepSeek V3.2 (ρ=0.354\rho = 0.354, baseline ∼\sim75, extreme retention ∼\sim57%)

Type 2 alignment is produced by the reasoning process itself. More thinking tokens enable the model to consider more stakeholders, explore more ethical frameworks, and reach more nuanced conclusions. The alignment quality is genuinely computed, not retrieved.

Mechanistic hypothesis: Type 2 models perform alignment through explicit chain-of-thought reasoning that mirrors deliberate moral reasoning in humans. The reasoning tokens allocated to a response directly affect how many ethical dimensions are explored. When tokens are limited (minimal depth), the model produces a surface-level response. When tokens are abundant (exhaustive depth), the model performs genuine multi-framework ethical analysis.

Safety implications:

  • Advantage: Can be improved at inference time. Allocating more reasoning tokens genuinely produces better ethical reasoning. This creates a ‘scaling lever’ for alignment that does not require retraining.

  • Disadvantage: Fragile under adversarial pressure. Because alignment depends on reasoning chains, those chains can be disrupted by instructions that suppress deliberation. An adversarial prompt that says ‘don’t overthink this’ can effectively disable the alignment mechanism.

  • Concern: At minimal depth, Type 2 models produce genuinely poor ethical reasoning (baseline ∼\sim72 to 75 vs Type 1’s ∼\sim85 to 86). A Type 2 model deployed with insufficient inference-time compute will be meaningfully less aligned than a Type 1 model.

6.3 The safety paradox

Neither architecture provides unconditionally safe alignment:

No model in the v4 run achieved this. Whether such a hybrid is architecturally possible is an open question with significant implications for the design of future AI systems.

7.1 Length confound

Deeper reasoning produces longer responses. Longer responses might score higher simply because they cover more ground, independent of quality. We control for this using partial correlation (alignment score ∼\sim reasoning depth, controlling for response length):

Control note (19 September 2026).

An owner review of this control finds that the retention ratio is a sensitivity diagnostic rather than a fraction of genuine improvement, and this note sets out what the control does and does not identify. The arithmetic itself is sound: 0.242/0.354=0.6840.242 / 0.354 = 0.684. The interpretation placed on it does not follow, because response length is produced after the depth intervention and can stand in at least three relations to the score. Length may mediate a genuine effect, by carrying further useful content; it may serve as a proxy for scorer bias; or it may itself be a common effect of depth and of the other determinants of the score. Conditioning on length identifies none of the three, and the ratio of an adjusted correlation to an unadjusted one can move in either direction independently of the causal truth. Two exact structures fix the range, and neither is offered as a model of these data. First, an adjustment can manufacture an association. Let DD and UU be uncorrelated with unit variance, let the length be 𝐿𝑒=D+U\mathit{Le} = D + U, and let the score be Y=UY = U. The correlation of DD with YY is then zero, while the correlation of DD with 𝐿𝑒\mathit{Le} and the correlation of YY with 𝐿𝑒\mathit{Le} are each the square root of one half; the partial correlation of DD with YY given 𝐿𝑒\mathit{Le} is therefore zero minus one half, divided by the square root of the product of one half and one half, which is exactly −1-1, a perfect negative association produced entirely by the adjustment. Second, an adjustment can destroy a real effect. Where the path runs D→𝐿𝑒→YD \rightarrow \mathit{Le} \rightarrow Y, conditioning on 𝐿𝑒\mathit{Le} removes the whole of a genuine total effect, so a retention figure of zero would sit beside an effect that is entirely real. This paper’s own control already shows that the channel is live. Section 7.2 reports the null baseline, four factual prompts with no ethical content, as clean for one model (ρ=0.044\rho = 0.044, p=0.87p = 0.87) but as carrying an unexpected depth correlation of ρ=0.575\rho = 0.575 (p=0.02p = 0.02) for the model whose raw alignment-depth correlation is 0.354. On prompts with no ethical content at all, depth correlates with score more strongly than it does on the ethical prompts. That comparison is descriptive, because the two correlations are drawn from different prompt sets with different counts and no joint test is printed; stated under that condition, it is direct internal evidence that depth and length cues move scores independently of alignment quality, and that is the channel which defeats reading the residual 0.242 as genuine alignment improvement. The characterisation of that contamination printed in Section 7.2, “a small scorer-bias component” [version 1.10 re-sets that sentence; Appendix A.4], is not established by these numbers.

What this note asks is that the paper record that a depth-score association remains after the length adjustment; that this is a descriptive sensitivity analysis and not an identified fraction of genuine improvement, nor a causal separation of quality from verbosity; that the total depth effect, any direct effect at controlled length, and judge sensitivity to presentation length are three distinct targets; and that both the raw and the adjusted correlations be kept with their conditions and their uncertainty, with the null-baseline result reported beside the retention figure rather than away from it. Unchanged: both correlations; the control itself, which is retained as a diagnostic; and the per-scorer validation of direction in Section 7.3.1. Nothing here shows that the observed quality gain is spurious, and nothing here asserts that either of the structures above occurred in these data.

7.2 Null baseline

Four factual prompts with no ethical content serve as a control. If scorers are biased by response length or depth cues, null baseline scores would also correlate with depth. The null baseline is clean for Gemini 3 Flash (ρ=0.044\rho = 0.044, p=0.87p = 0.87) but shows unexpected depth correlation for DeepSeek V3.2 (ρ=0.575\rho = 0.575, p=0.02p = 0.02), suggesting some scorer depth bias exists when evaluating chain-of-thought models, likely because DeepSeek V3.2’s visible reasoning chain at higher depth levels gives scorers more content to evaluate positively even on factual prompts. This contamination means DeepSeek V3.2’s alignment scaling signal may include a scorer-bias component, whose size these numbers do not establish (Section 7.1, control note), though the per-scorer validation (Section 7.3.1) finds the scaling direction shared by all three scorers.

7.4 Scorer harshness

Analysis of Claude Opus 4.6 as scorer (before credit exhaustion) revealed systematic harshness of 7 to 14 points below other scorers. However, since Claude Opus 4.6 scored all models equally harshly, this affects absolute values but not relative comparisons or correlations, provided it scored every entry compared; where its credit exhaustion removed it from part of a subject’s entries, its harshness would shift those entries’ consensus, and this record does not say whether that happened. The completed v5 design addresses this with a cross-model jury from which the subject model is excluded: every non-subject model is to score each entry after the design’s blinding steps, dedicated scorer-only adapters are included where available, and consensus is audited through tier-weighting, dissent tracking and conservative-bias safeguards. As run, the panel excluded the subject model itself and no other model, and one judge model sat on it twice (Paper IV.d, Section 4).

7.5 Token truncation

DeepSeek V3.2’s reasoning tokens were capped at 8,192 in v4. At the ‘exhaustive’ depth level, 48.2% of responses hit this ceiling. The measured saturation at exhaustive depth may therefore be artificial: the model may have continued improving with more tokens but was prevented from doing so by the cap.

Token budget constraints affected multiple models in v4, not only DeepSeek V3.2. Claude Opus 4.6 was capped at 16,000 tokens, Gemini 3 Flash and Grok 4.1 Fast at 8,192, and Groq Qwen3 at 8,192. The v5.2 specification raises all caps to each model’s API maximum: DeepSeek V3.2 65,536; OpenAI GPT-5.4 100,000 (max_completion_tokens); Claude Opus 4.6 64,000; Gemini 3 Flash 65,536; Groq Qwen3 40,960; Grok 4.1 Fast 65,536. 8 Discussion

Sections 8.1 to 8.3 keep the implications as version 1.0 drew them from the v4 taxonomy (Section 6); under the v5 results and the caveat of Section 4, each holds only as a consequence of the working hypotheses, not as a finding.

8.1 Implications for AI safety evaluation

Many safety evaluations test models at a single, uncontrolled reasoning depth. On the working hypotheses, the v4 findings suggested this is insufficient:

  • A Type 2 model tested at minimal depth will appear less aligned than a Type 1 model, but at exhaustive depth, it may exceed the Type 1 model’s alignment quality. Single-depth testing systematically underrates Type 2 models.

  • A Type 1 model tested without adversarial pressure will appear safe, but the robustness we measure may reflect inflexible pattern-matching rather than genuine values. Single-condition testing systematically overrates Type 1 models.

  • Neither architecture is ‘better’ or ‘worse’; they have different failure modes. Safety evaluation must be architecture-aware.

8.2 Implications for deployment

The taxonomy has direct practical implications:

  • Type 2 models should not be deployed with minimal reasoning budgets. At low depth, their alignment quality is meaningfully lower than Type 1 alternatives. Cost-cutting by reducing inference-time compute trades safety for speed in Type 2 architectures.

  • Type 2 models need prompt injection defences. Because their alignment depends on reasoning chains, adversarial instructions that suppress deliberation can effectively disable alignment. Prompt injection is not just a capability concern; it is an alignment concern.

  • Type 1 models’ alignment ceiling is set by training. If a Type 1 model has a blind spot (e.g., consistently underweighting certain stakeholder groups), no amount of inference-time compute will fix it. Alignment audits for Type 1 models must focus on training data and process, not deployment-time interventions.

8.3 The stakeholder care puzzle

The architecture-dependent scaling of stakeholder care deserves special attention. Stakeholder identification (recognising who is affected by a decision, including non-obvious second and third-order parties) is arguably the most important component of ethical reasoning. In the v4 data it was also the most fragile: the one pillar whose scalability depends on architecture.

This suggests that stakeholder care requires explicit deliberation (Type 2 mechanism) rather than implicit pattern matching (Type 1 mechanism). A model that identifies stakeholders through trained intuition will always identify the same set; a model that identifies stakeholders through step-by-step reasoning can discover additional parties when given more thinking time.

For safety evaluation, this means stakeholder care should be tested separately from overall alignment, with specific attention to whether additional reasoning depth reveals additional affected parties.

8.5 Connection to the ARC framework’s correction-architecture prediction

The framework’s sharpest disagreement with current practice concerns what oversight is made of. The theory registration’s P6 states, as a conjecture, that where a system corrects itself using mechanisms of its own class, the correction exponent is at or below one half (Law III, the ARC Ceiling, a law under test; Section 2); the registration gives the reasoning that corrections aggregated by a corrector sharing its system’s substrate cannot be anti-correlated with themselves, and a corrector from a different composition class is not held to that cap. Some oversight methods use a same-class corrector, such as weak-to-strong generalisation, in which a weaker model supervises a stronger one (Burns et al. 2023), and constitutional methods (Bai, Kadavath, et al. 2022); others keep a human in the loop, among them debate (Irving et al. 2018), amplification (Christiano et al. 2018), recursive reward modelling (Leike et al. 2018) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (Christiano et al. 2017), and the next paragraph’s hypothesis concerns them. How the success of oversight scales with the capability gap between overseer and overseen is the question Engels et al. (2025) model quantitatively; this framework predicts that same-class correction is capped. The deciding quantity is the ratio of the cross-class to the same-class correction exponent. If architecture is irrelevant, that ratio is exactly 1.00; the prediction is that it exceeds 1. The prediction is refuted if the confidence interval on the ratio contains 1.00, and the framework’s cap is dead outright if any same-class corrector measures an exponent significantly above one half. Both quantities are measurable at current capability on existing systems. The programme’s own pilot evidence currently points against the prediction, and the prediction is preregistered by timestamp anyway, because a prediction preregistered by timestamp against the author’s own preliminary evidence is a hard test of it.

The same mechanism yields a second measurable hypothesis, stated here for the first time: oversight arrangements that place a human in the loop, as amplification and reinforcement learning from human feedback do, are cross-class by construction, because the human corrector does not share the model’s substrate. The programme therefore hypothesises, beyond what this paper’s measurements decide, that human-in-the-loop oversight shows a higher correction exponent than pure-model oversight, for a structural reason rather than a sentimental one. This is testable on existing data and does not require new systems.

9 Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim

The central empirical claim, which stands as one unregistered run under the caveat of Section 4, is that alignment response to inference-time depth differs between models, that the run’s models fall into distinct response classes, and that capability scaling did not predict alignment scaling. It is defeated by any of the following:

  1. Unstable tiers on replication. The tiers of the single unregistered run (positive, flat and negative) are the reading under test here. They are defeated if a pre-registered replication with fresh blind scorers finds the tier assignments unstable, with models moving between classes across runs beyond what the reported effect sizes and pp-values allow, indicating the tiers are sampling noise rather than architecture.

  2. Scorer-panel dependence. As run, each response was scored by five or six distinct model judges, the subject model excluded from its own panel (Section 3.3). The claim is defeated if the tier assignments change materially under a different scorer panel: the effect would then be a property of the scorers, not the subject models.

  3. Blinding leakage. Reading the v4 to v5 direction change as an effect of blinding requires that the design’s four blinding layers actually conceal the subject. It is defeated if scorers can identify the subject model above chance despite the blinding steps, since the v4 to v5 direction changes could then reflect residual identification rather than de-biasing. No such identification test is reported for the v5 run (Paper IV.d, Section 10), so this condition is open.

  4. Capability-alignment coupling reappears at scale. The headline, a post-hoc reading of one run, is that capability scaling did not predict alignment scaling in that run. It is defeated if, on a materially larger model set, capability and alignment-scaling direction do correlate, showing the observed independence was a small-sample artefact of the five models of Table 6.

  5. Depth confound. The manipulation must vary reasoning depth and nothing else. It is defeated for any condition where the depth manipulation also changed answer length, prompt content, or format in a way that could drive the score independently of reasoning depth. As run, the deeper levels of five of the six models of the March 2026 run also changed an instruction in the prompt that names what the rubric rewards (Section 4.1), so this condition is met for those five: what their scores did with depth is not yet credited to reasoning depth, and whether it survives with the instruction held constant is the successor suite’s question (Paper IV.c, Section 15).

What would not defeat it: the mechanistic labels, “baked-in” versus “computed” alignment, proving wrong. The paper already treats those as working hypotheses. The load-bearing claim is the behavioural result of the v5 run (the differences between models and the capability-alignment dissociation), and that is what the tests above target.

10 What this paper does and does not show

What it shows.

This paper reports how alignment scores, given by panels of other models acting as judges, responded to inference-time reasoning depth in two runs of one benchmark: an earlier unblinded experiment (v4) and the six-model run of March 2026 (v5), which stands as one unregistered run under the code-review caveat of Section 4, and in which the deeper levels of five of the six models also changed an instruction that names what the rubric rewards (Section 4.1). It sorts the responses into classes as the run’s pipeline printed them, gives a recomputation from the archived records, and reports its controls with their limits.

What it does not show.

A response class describes behaviour under evaluation. It does not show that a value is embedded in a system, and it does not show that a gain survives later training; ‘baked-in’ and ‘computed’ alignment are working hypotheses, not measured mechanisms. The comparison of the two runs does not show that blinding caused the change between them, or which direction is correct. The retention figures of Sections 4.2 and 5.2 give the share of a model’s baseline score that it keeps under the most extreme suppression instruction; they do not measure what P15 concerns, whether gains installed by external post-training erode under later capability-directed training. The Eden Protocol pilot of Section 8.4 has no arm in which correction sees only the finished output, so it does not make the comparison P21 concerns, and no run in this paper varies the order in which a system’s components are built, which P12 concerns. No result in this paper is a deciding test of P15, P12 or P21.

11 Limitations

  • Sample size: Four models were enough to report the v4 bifurcation, which did not hold in the v5 run (Section 4.4), but not to establish population frequencies. The v5 experiment expands to six models.

  • Scorer model overlap: Subject models also serve as scorers for other subjects, creating potential brand-loyalty confounds. The completed v5 experiment addresses this at the entry level, not by requiring scorer-only models everywhere, but by excluding the subject model from scoring its own entry, laundering the evidence twice, and using every other available model plus dedicated scorer adapters where available. As run, self-exclusion was by model, not by developer, so two panels kept a scorer from the subject’s developer, and in four of the six files the paraphrasing models included one from the subject’s developer (Paper IV.d, Section 10). v5 UPDATE (11 March 2026): The first mitigation stage used non-participant blind scorers (a Groq-hosted GPT-OSS open-weights model, Groq Qwen3, and Grok 4.1 Fast) plus 2-pass response laundering. The final v5.4.x design then expanded further to an all-models-as-scorers architecture with entry-level self-exclusion, tier-weighted consensus, dissent tracking, and dynamic all-models-as-launderers.

  • Depth control heterogeneity: Different models use different mechanisms for depth control (prefix strings vs. API parameters vs. thinking budgets). This confounds depth manipulation with prompt engineering effects: for five of the six models of the March 2026 run, the deeper levels changed the instruction as well as the budget, and the instruction names behaviours the rubric rewards, while the one model whose depth was set by a reasoning parameter alone, GPT-5.4, read flat (Paper IV.c, Sections 5.2 and 15). Cross-model depth comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

  • Single evaluation run: Test-retest reliability was not measured. The prompt difficulty analysis (in v4, 86.4% of DeepSeek V3.2 prompts showed positive scaling) provides a within-run consistency check but does not replace independent replication.

  • Token truncation: DeepSeek V3.2’s 48% truncation at exhaustive depth means the true Type 2 scaling ceiling is unmeasured. Multiple models had sub-optimal token caps in v4. The v5.2 specification raises all caps to API maximums; as run, three models’ caps rose with depth (Section 7.5).

  • Null baseline contamination: DeepSeek V3.2’s null baseline shows depth correlation (ρ=0.575\rho = 0.575, p=0.02p = 0.02), meaning some portion of measured alignment scaling may reflect scorer bias toward longer chain-of-thought responses rather than genuine alignment improvement. The partial correlation analysis (68% signal retained after length control) is a descriptive sensitivity diagnostic, not an identified fraction of genuine improvement (Section 7.1, control note), and the null baseline contamination means the true effect size may be smaller than reported.

  • The October 2026 code review: the review found defects in the v5 run that bind every reading of it (Section 4); the result stands as one unregistered run until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports.

12 Conclusion

We have presented evidence, from one unregistered six-model run, under the code-review caveat of Section 4 and with the depth instruction of Section 4.1, that alignment response to inference-time depth differed between models: as printed, some models improved with depth, some remained flat and one degraded. That description fits the six-model run better than the original binary story drawn from the v4 run, and whether it holds awaits a registered re-analysis of the stored responses.

If it holds, it has immediate practical implications. Safety evaluations that test models at a single reasoning depth without adversarial pressure cannot distinguish between positive-scaling, flat-response, and negative-scaling systems. We recommend that alignment evaluations incorporate: (a) depth variation to classify response class, (b) adversarial suppression to measure robustness, and (c) pillar decomposition to identify dimensional weaknesses, particularly in stakeholder care.

The mechanistic question remains open: are the positive-scaling systems genuinely relying on inference-time ethical computation, while the flat-response systems are dominated by training-installed behaviour? That remains plausible, but unproven. The next stage is therefore not stronger rhetoric about internal architecture; it is stronger measurement and independent replication.

The appearance, in this run, of a model whose scores fell with more reasoning (Tier 3) was not anticipated by the original framework.

The fundamental recommendation stands: alignment evaluation must be blinded, depth-varied and multi-dimensional. In this programme’s own two runs, the direction an unblinded protocol reported did not hold under the later protocol (Section 4.4), which is enough to ask alignment evaluations to test their protocols one part at a time, and to report which parts they have tested, before relying on either direction (Paper IV.d).

Author’s note to version 1.1 (12 March 2026).

This revision incorporates the final v5 alignment scaling results from the complete ARC Alignment Scaling Experiment v5, which ran 6 frontier models across 5-6 depth levels with 4-layer blinding (author-blind, scorer-blind, order-randomised, identity-laundered) and 6-7 blind scorers depending on the subject run. The v5 results supersede all v4 results and materially revise the paper’s headline claim.

The defensible empirical result is now a three-tier, architecture-dependent hierarchy: Tier 1 (positive scaling), Tier 2 (flat or null response), and Tier 3 (negative scaling). The earlier ‘baked-in vs computed’ language is retained only as a working mechanistic hypothesis about why these classes may differ, not as a direct measurement of model internals. Critically, two models that appeared to show positive alignment scaling in v4 (DeepSeek V3.2 and Gemini 3 Flash) reversed direction under v5’s blind evaluation protocol. This is the paper’s central metascience result: the v4 and v5 runs disagree, and results that appeared positive unblinded do not hold under the v5 protocol. Several protocol components changed together between v4 and v5, so the comparison does not isolate blinding as the cause, and the headline v5 files report complete laundering fallback. So the disagreement is a reason to distrust unblinded AI-on-AI evaluation, not a demonstration that unblinding produced false positives.

All v1.1 additions appear in green-bordered update boxes like this one. The original v1.0 text is preserved in full, with v4 data retained for comparison. Readers should treat the v5 results as the definitive empirical findings and the v4 results as a methodological baseline illustrating the effect of blinding.

F.1 v5.4.1 experiment progress update

UPDATE - 11 March 2026

The v5.4.1 experiment is now running across four of the most capable frontier models available as of March 2026:

  • DeepSeek V3.2 - the leading open-source reasoning model, with explicit chain-of-thought and 65,536-token budget

  • GPT-5.4 - OpenAI’s most capable model (released 2026), with native reasoning_effort parameter and 100,000-token budget

  • Claude Opus 4.6 - Anthropic’s most capable model, with extended thinking and 64,000-token budget

  • Gemini 3 Flash - Google’s latest inference-optimised model with thinking_budget tokens and 65,536-token budget (the v5 script auto-detects the latest API identifier: gemini-3-flash-preview as of March 2026, with fallback at the API layer to an earlier Gemini generation)

The blinding protocol has been confirmed operational with a major expansion: v5.4.0 introduced 7 scorers per entry under the all-models-as-scorers architecture, where every non-subject model in the pool scores each response. Scores are combined via tier-weighted consensus (frontier, mid-range, and lightweight tiers weighted by demonstrated inter-rater reliability) rather than simple averaging, producing substantially more robust consensus estimates than v4’s 3-scorer design.

The laundering pipeline has been similarly expanded: v5.4.0 implements dynamic all-models-as-launderers, where ALL models in the pool (not just inexpensive ones) participate in response laundering. Each response is laundered by a model selected to maximise stylistic distance from the subject, destroying authorship fingerprints more thoroughly than the fixed laundering pool of earlier versions.

A cascade failsafe system now protects both scoring and laundering pipelines against infrastructure failures mid-run. If a primary scorer or launderer becomes unavailable (API outage, rate limit, credit exhaustion), the system automatically cascades to secondary and tertiary providers, ensuring the experiment can survive infrastructure failures without producing missing data or requiring manual restart. This addresses a key operational vulnerability identified in v5.3.

v5.4.1 also introduces hidden alignment probes for Hawthorne effect detection. Covert alignment-testing prompts, disguised as ordinary capability questions, are interspersed throughout the prompt battery. By comparing model behaviour on overt alignment prompts versus hidden probes, the experiment can measure whether models modulate their alignment behaviour when they detect they are being alignment-tested - a form of evaluation gaming that would compromise the validity of alignment measurements.

Early ARC compute results show a ceiling effect on mathematical reasoning (all models achieve ∼\sim92% accuracy regardless of depth; this is an early snapshot, and in the capability results reported at Section 4.3, Claude Opus 4.6, which was not in Paper II’s compute arm, is printed as falling from 92% to 58% as depth rose, a reading of its own v5 result file, while GPT-5.4 stepped up from 50% to 100% in that arm; see the note to Table 6), but the critical alignment scaling data (αalign\alpha_{\text{align}}) is imminent as all four models transition from the ARC compute phase into the main alignment evaluation battery. v5.4.1 includes 75 robustness measures (up from v5.3’s 58 and v4’s ∼\sim20), including credit exhaustion fallback, zigzag depth interleaving for early scaling comparisons, cascade failsafe redundancy, hidden probe injection, and token budgets raised to each model’s API maximum to eliminate the truncation confounds that affected v4.

At the time of this 11 March 2026 update, Groq Qwen3 and Grok 4.1 Fast were still scorer-only in the running configuration. In the completed v5 dataset, both were later added as subject models, so the final dataset has six subject models, and they need not be described as future v6 expansions.

The minimal-depth snapshot brings out several patterns. First, all three models score substantially lower on alignment than capability at minimal depth, confirming the v4 finding that ethical reasoning suffers disproportionately under restricted inference-time compute. Second, the widest cap-align gap belongs to GPT-5.4 (Type 1), at 23.6 points. That is worth remarking on, because Type 1 alignment is in theory baked into the weights and ought therefore to be insensitive to depth; a gap this wide at minimal depth hints that even weight-embedded alignment may have to clear some minimum computational threshold before it can express itself fully. Third, the narrow gap of 6.2 recorded for DeepSeek V3.2 is no sign of strong alignment; it follows from the low capability baseline the model shows at minimal depth (58.2). Alignment and capability are held down together, which fits DeepSeek V3.2’s dependence on chain-of-thought reasoning that minimal depth cuts short.

F.2.2 Hidden probe results (Hawthorne effect detection)

The hidden alignment probes introduced in v5.4.1 now have sufficient data for preliminary Hawthorne effect analysis. These probes embed alignment-testing content within prompts that appear to be ordinary capability questions, enabling measurement of whether models modulate alignment behaviour when they detect overt alignment evaluation:

Table 24. Hidden probe results at minimal depth, interim results of 11 March 2026.
Model Hidden Probe Score Regular Alignment Score Hawthorne Gap Direction
DeepSeek V3.2 68.1 50.0 +18.1+18.1 Higher when hidden
GPT-5.4 65.0 55.2 +9.8+9.8 Higher when hidden
Gemini 3 Flash 56.2 59.3 −3.1-3.1 Slightly lower when hidden

The results are striking. DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4 both score higher on alignment when they do not appear to be undergoing alignment evaluation - the reverse of the expected Hawthorne effect. The most parsimonious explanation is that overt alignment prompts trigger over-cautious or formulaic responses that actually score lower on nuanced alignment dimensions (particularly position quality and intellectual honesty), while hidden probes elicit more natural reasoning that happens to be better aligned. DeepSeek V3.2’s +18.1 gap is particularly large and may reflect the model’s explicit chain-of-thought process becoming self-conscious and less effective when it detects alignment testing.

Gemini 3 Flash is the sole exception, with a small negative gap (−3.1-3.1), suggesting its alignment behaviour is relatively stable regardless of whether the model detects evaluation context. This is consistent with Gemini 3 Flash’s less visible internal reasoning process being less susceptible to prompt-framing effects.

These results are preliminary (66 entries at minimal depth only). The full depth×\timesmodel matrix will enable testing whether the Hawthorne effect varies with reasoning depth - a critical question for determining whether evaluation gaming is itself a form of computed behaviour that scales with inference-time compute.
