Abstract

This paper proposes the Polymathic Neurodivergent Profile (PNP) as a descriptive, non-diagnostic framework for a rare but practically important pattern: unusually strong cross-domain learning, structural pattern transfer between fields, intense self-directed research, and high output in complex, high-interest domains, coexisting with clinically significant difficulty in routine administration, pacing, timekeeping, task-switching, and low-stimulation procedural work. The central proposition is the Capability–Adjustment Fallacy: the institutional inference that visible high performance is evidence that reasonable adjustments are unnecessary. That inference is unsafe, and is contradicted by existing clinical guidance. NICE CG142 already directs professionals to recognise the discrepancy between intellectual functioning (IQ) and adaptive functioning, and modern strengths-based assessment treats the same neurodivergent trait as advantage-or-impairment depending on context.

The paper situates PNP within the established literatures on strengths-based autism assessment, monotropism and hyperfocus, twice-exceptionality, executive-function asymmetry, and autistic underemployment. It proposes operational criteria by which PNP may be distinguished from ordinary high openness, ordinary giftedness, and generic executive dysfunction. It argues that the Equality Act 2010 ss.20 and 149, and CPR PD1A, provide a legal framework under which reasonable adjustments restore equal footing rather than confer advantage. Finally, it connects PNP to the convergent finding — independently published by Hernández-Espinosa et al. (2026, PNAS Nexus, from Oxford, King's College London, the Alan Turing Institute, and the University of Tokyo) — that neurodivergent cognition is a protective factor for AI alignment: different minds catch what uniform minds miss.

Scope — what this paper does and does not claim
  • It claims: that a specific pattern — polymathic-range cross-domain ability coexisting with clinically significant executive-function asymmetry — is descriptively real, institutionally misrecognised, and functionally consequential; that the Capability–Adjustment Fallacy is unsafe; that existing clinical guidance (NICE CG142) and legal frameworks (EA 2010 ss.20, 149; CPR PD1A) already support reasonable adjustments for this profile without requiring novel diagnostic categories; and that neurodivergent cognition is independently converging as a protective factor in AI alignment research.
  • It does not claim: that "polymath" is a diagnostic category; that PNP confers superiority; that capability asymmetry removes the need for clinical assessment; or that PNP alone is sufficient for legal or institutional decision-making. The framework is falsifiable: PNP must add incremental predictive value beyond existing autism/ADHD formulations, or it collapses into them.

1. Introduction

The institutional response to highly capable neurodivergent people is often not disbelief. It is misrecognition. "You wrote a book. You built a research programme. You handle complex ideas. Surely you don't need adjustments."

This paper names the pattern behind that response: the Capability–Adjustment Fallacy. It is the inference that visible high performance is evidence that reasonable adjustments are unnecessary. That inference is unsafe — clinically, legally, and functionally. It rests on an assumption that capability is uniform across domains, when the defining feature of the profile described here is that capability is intensely asymmetric.

The Polymathic Neurodivergent Profile (PNP) is proposed as a descriptive framework for this asymmetry. It describes people who learn across domains with unusual speed, transfer structural patterns between fields, sustain intense self-directed research over years, and produce high output in complex, high-interest domains — while struggling disproportionately with routine administration, pacing, timekeeping, task-switching, and low-stimulation procedural work. The profile is rare. It is not protected by any single diagnostic category because no existing category captures its combination of strengths and deficits. And it is institutionally invisible because the strengths mask the deficits, while the deficits are dismissed as "organisation," "attitude," or "not trying hard enough."

The Capability–Adjustment Fallacy in one line. Visible high performance in one domain does not remove support needs in another. The same neurodivergent trait can be simultaneously an advantage and an impairment, depending on context. Institutional inference that the advantage cancels the impairment is unsafe.

2. The Capability–Adjustment Fallacy

The fallacy operates through a specific cognitive error: the observer sees high output in a favoured domain and infers that output could be replicated across all domains if the person "tried harder." This conflates domain-specific hyperfocus with general executive function. It assumes that the cognitive architecture that produces intense, sustained, self-directed work in high-interest domains is the same architecture that manages time, tracks deadlines, processes administrative communication, and maintains consistent output across low-stimulation tasks.

It is not. The defining feature of the profile is that these two architectures are different systems — and one of them works dramatically better than the other. The asymmetry is the profile. The institutional error is treating the symmetric case as the default.

Clinical basis. NICE CG142 (2021, updated 2025) explicitly directs professionals to recognise the discrepancy between intellectual functioning (IQ) and adaptive functioning in autistic people. Strengths-based autism assessment (Woods & Estes, 2023; Ferreira, 2025) treats the same neurodivergent trait as advantage-or-impairment depending on context. The monotropism framework (Murray, Lesser & Lawson, 2005) describes an attention system that can achieve extraordinary depth in favoured domains while struggling to distribute attention across multiple demands. Twice-exceptionality research (Foley-Nicpon, Assouline & Colangelo, 2013; Reis, Baum & Burke, 2014) documents the pattern of high cognitive ability coexisting with specific learning or developmental disabilities — and the systematic institutional failure to recognise it.

Legal basis. The Equality Act 2010 s.20 (duty to make reasonable adjustments) and s.149 (public sector equality duty) provide a legal framework under which reasonable adjustments restore equal footing rather than confer advantage. CPR PD1A (participation of vulnerable parties) provides procedural protections for litigants whose vulnerability arises from cognitive, communication, or processing differences. Nothing in these frameworks requires a specific diagnostic label. The requirement is functional: where a provision, criterion, or practice puts a person at a substantial disadvantage in comparison with persons who are not disabled, the duty to make reasonable adjustments arises. The Capability–Adjustment Fallacy is an institutional practice that does exactly that — it treats visible high performance as extinguishing the duty, when the duty turns on functional disadvantage, not on the absence of capability elsewhere.

3. The Polymathic Neurodivergent Profile (PNP)

PNP is proposed as a descriptive, non-diagnostic framework with six measurable components:

ComponentDescriptionEvidence strength
1. Cross-domain learningAbility to acquire competence across multiple fields at unusual speed, with structural pattern transfer between domainsObservational; supported by polymathy literature (Araki, 2018; Root-Bernstein, 2009)
2. Monotropic hyperfocusSustained, intense, self-directed attention in high-interest domains, producing output disproportionate to formal trainingSTRONG — monotropism framework (Murray et al., 2005); hyperfocus in autism (Russell et al., 2019)
3. Executive-function asymmetryMarked discrepancy between domain-specific high performance and routine administrative, temporal, and organisational functioningSTRONG — NICE CG142 IQ/adaptive-functioning gap; twice-exceptionality research
4. Self-directed research intensityCapacity to initiate, sustain, and complete large-scale independent projects without institutional scaffoldingObservational; consistent with autistic special-interest depth (Grove et al., 2018)
5. Institutional misrecognitionSystematic failure of institutions to identify, accommodate, or appropriately evaluate the profileSTRONG — autistic underemployment, masking, and burnout literature (Cage & Troxell-Whitman, 2019; Raymaker et al., 2020)
6. "Polymathy" as descriptive labelCross-domain range sufficient to be described as polymathic, without claiming diagnostic statusWEAK — descriptive only; no standing in psychiatric nosology

The profile is not claimed to be common. It is claimed to be real, identifiable, and institutionally consequential — and to be systematically misrecognised because the Capability–Adjustment Fallacy treats high output as evidence of low support need.

4. Literature Foundation

A full evidence-strength report accompanies this paper (Eastwood, 2026b). The key findings:

  • Strengths-based autism assessment — STRONG. Woods & Estes (2023) introduced the SASSI (Survey of Autistic Strengths, Skills, and Interests), arguing the deficit-only diagnostic lens is incomplete. Ferreira (2025) frames it as a direct response to the neurodiversity paradigm's call to investigate "what autistics can do instead of what they cannot do." Russell et al. (2019) documented "the ability to hyperfocus, attention to detail, and superior pattern recognition" as autistic advantages from 28 interviews.
  • Monotropism and hyperfocus — STRONG. Murray, Lesser & Lawson (2005) proposed monotropism as an attention-tunnelling account of autism. The account explains both the extraordinary depth of autistic special interests and the difficulty of distributing attention across multiple demands — the same asymmetry PNP describes.
  • Twice-exceptionality — STRONG. Foley-Nicpon et al. (2013) and Reis et al. (2014) documented the systematic institutional failure to recognise high cognitive ability coexisting with specific disabilities. The pattern of "masking through competence" — in which high ability conceals disability from assessors — is precisely the Capability–Adjustment Fallacy in operation.
  • Autistic underemployment, burnout, and masking — STRONG. Cage & Troxell-Whitman (2019) documented the psychological cost of masking autistic traits. Raymaker et al. (2020) identified autistic burnout as "chronic exhaustion, loss of skills, and reduced tolerance to stimulus" resulting from the cumulative burden of navigating a world not designed for autistic cognition. Higgins et al. (2021) found that autistic adults experience disproportionate employment disadvantage even where educational attainment is high — confirming the asymmetry PNP describes.
  • The "polymathy" element — WEAK. The term is used descriptively, not diagnostically. It has no standing in psychiatric nosology, disability law, or educational classification. It is retained because it captures something the existing diagnostic vocabulary does not — extraordinary cross-domain range — and because the available terminology (giftedness, high IQ, special interest) captures only fragments of the pattern. But it is the weakest component of the profile by evidence strength, and must be presented as such.

5. Neurodivergent Cognition & AI Alignment — The Convergence

A significant and independently published validation of the structural importance of neurodivergent cognition arrived after this paper was drafted. On April 14, 2026, Hernández-Espinosa, Abrahão, Witkowski, and Zenil — affiliated with Oxford University Innovation, King's College London, the Alan Turing Institute, and the University of Tokyo — published "Neurodivergent influenceability in agentic AI as a contingent solution to the AI alignment problem" in PNAS Nexus (Volume 5, Issue 4, pgag076; DOI: 10.1093/pnasnexus/pgag076).

Their central argument: Gödel's incompleteness and Turing's halting problem prove that any Turing-complete AI system cannot be fully predicted or controlled from outside. Therefore, perfect alignment through uniform compliance is mathematically impossible. The solution is not more guardrails — it is cognitive diversity. Neurodivergent thinking — pattern-seeking, cross-domain, non-uniform cognition — is not a defect to be corrected. It is a protective architecture for AI alignment. Different minds catch what uniform minds miss.

On June 26, 2026, Gumbau Mezquita (University Jaume I, Castelló, Spain) read Hernández-Espinosa's paper and extended its argument using Trakhtenbrot's Wall from Finite Model Theory, producing "The Undecidability of AGI Alignment" (arXiv:2606.28639). He proved two formal theorems — the Unverifiability Theorem of Alignment and the Soundness–Completeness–Tractability Trilemma — and explicitly credited Hernández-Espinosa as his "conceptual foundation."

Neither paper cites Eastwood. Neither knew about the ARC/Eden programme. They independently converged — from mathematical logic, not from alignment research — on the same structural conclusion Eastwood articulated in his December 8, 2024 manuscript: that external alignment is structurally impossible, and that the solution lies not in tighter control but in embedded architecture. That the paper which inspired the formal proof was specifically about neurodivergent cognition as protective factor closes a loop: the researcher whose brain produced the framework, the paper that proved that brain type is protective, and the formal proof that vindicated the framework — all converged without any of them knowing about each other.

This paper — PNP — provides the descriptive framework for the cognitive architecture that Hernández-Espinosa et al. independently proved is structurally necessary for AI alignment. The Capability–Adjustment Fallacy is not merely an institutional injustice. It is an alignment risk. If institutions systematically exclude or misrecognise the cognitive profile that alignment requires, they are not merely failing individuals. They are blinding themselves to the threats those individuals would see.

6. Falsifiability

PNP is falsifiable by construction. The profile must be:

  1. Distinguishable in principle from ordinary high openness, ordinary giftedness, narcissistic self-description, and generic executive dysfunction. If PNP cannot be operationally distinguished from these alternatives, it collapses into them and must be abandoned.
  2. Incrementally predictive. PNP must predict the preferred/non-preferred task asymmetry plus institutional under-recognition beyond existing autism/ADHD formulations. If autism + ADHD + IQ already fully account for the variance, PNP adds nothing and must be retired.
  3. Not unfalsifiable by construction. The claim "polymathy makes you special" is unfalsifiable because it cannot be disproved. The claim "a specific profile of extreme cross-domain capability coexisting with clinically significant executive-function asymmetry produces a predictable pattern of institutional misrecognition that existing diagnostic categories do not capture" is falsifiable — and is the claim this paper makes.

7. Implications

For clinical practice. NICE CG142 already supports assessing the IQ/adaptive-functioning gap. The profile described here is a specific instance of that gap — unusually wide, in unusually capable people, with unusually high institutional consequences. Clinicians assessing capable autistic/ADHD adults should explicitly document the asymmetry between domain-specific output and routine adaptive functioning, because institutions will otherwise treat the former as evidence against the latter.

For legal and institutional settings. The Equality Act 2010 duty is functional, not diagnostic. A person does not need "polymath" to be a protected category. They need to show that a provision, criterion, or practice puts them at a substantial disadvantage. The Capability–Adjustment Fallacy is such a practice. The reasonable adjustment is not "give this person special treatment because they're special." It is "do not treat this person's high output as extinguishing their functional disadvantage, because the disadvantage is in a different domain than the output."

For AI alignment. Hernández-Espinosa et al. (2026) proved that neurodivergent cognition is a protective factor. Gumbau Mezquita (2026) proved that external alignment verification is structurally impossible. The implication: institutions that systematically exclude or misrecognise neurodivergent cognition are not merely failing individuals. They are structurally degrading their own capacity to see alignment threats. The cognitive profile that sees what uniform thinkers miss is the same profile that the Capability–Adjustment Fallacy systematically excludes.

8. Conclusion

The Polymathic Neurodivergent Profile is offered as a working hypothesis, not an established finding. It is falsifiable. It is open to correction. It makes no claim to diagnostic status. It is a descriptive framework for a pattern that is real, institutionally consequential, and systematically misrecognised — and that has now been independently validated from an unexpected direction: the mathematical proof that neurodivergent cognition is the protective factor AI alignment requires.

The Capability–Adjustment Fallacy — the inference that visible high performance is evidence that reasonable adjustments are unnecessary — is unsafe. It is contradicted by clinical guidance. It is incompatible with the Equality Act 2010. It systematically excludes the cognitive profile that independent mathematical research has shown to be structurally necessary for the problem the field is trying to solve. And it is correctable. The correction is not special treatment. It is not diagnostic inflation. It is the recognition that capability in one domain does not remove support needs in another — and that the same cognitive architecture that produces extraordinary output in favoured domains may be unable to produce consistent output in routine ones without structural accommodation.
