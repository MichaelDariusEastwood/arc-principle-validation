Abstract

Claims that AI systems become more or less aligned as they reason longer often rest on scores from other AI models acting as judges, so it matters whether such scores keep their direction when the scoring protocol changes. In an earlier experiment (v4), scored by models that could see which model wrote each answer and at what depth, two model families showed alignment scores rising with depth: DeepSeek V3.2 (Spearman’s ρ=+0.354\rho=+0.354) and an earlier Gemini model (ρ=+0.275\rho=+0.275). In the later, unregistered March 2026 run (v5), made on newly generated responses with a bundle of blinding steps (identity and depth masking, two paraphrase passes, evaluator instructions and order randomisation) and a larger panel, DeepSeek V3.2 showed no significant trend and Gemini 3 Flash a significantly negative one (Cohen’s d=−0.53d=-0.53); GPT-5.4 showed no significant trend in either run. The runs also differed in their prompts, token allowances, depth levels, statistic and Gemini model, so the comparison shows that the reported direction did not hold across protocols; it does not show that blinding caused the change, or which direction is correct. An internal code review in October 2026 found that the paraphrase step’s output cap cut the deepest answers in the three v5 runs that read flat or negative, that a model that wrote each entry’s paraphrase also judged it, and that one judge model sat on the panel twice; the result stands as one unregistered comparison under that caveat, awaiting a re-analysis of the stored responses that is to be registered before it runs. Earlier work had shown that labels and surface features move model judges and that judges recognise and favour their own outputs; this paper adds a case in which frontier models’ alignment-depth trends did not hold, one reversing sign on a later model, with its limits stated, and a recommended protocol that tests each part on identical responses.

1 Introduction

I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only proof will move the world. Proof of that kind rests on measurement, and much of the measurement of alignment is done by AI models acting as judges, whose scores decide whether a system is said to become more or less aligned as it reasons longer. If the direction of such a result can change with the way the scoring is done, a safety conclusion drawn from it can point the wrong way. For that reason the programme this paper belongs to admits no unblinded judgement as evidence for any proposition it has registered (Appendix D).

Many AI alignment evaluations are not run under anything resembling clinical-trial standards. A model answers a prompt; another model, a related model, or a human evaluator scores the answer; and the evaluator may know, or be able to infer, which model produced it, how much reasoning effort was used, or what result the experiment is expected to find.

In medicine, that would be regarded as a serious design weakness. In AI evaluation, blinding of this kind is not yet a standard requirement. The ARC alignment experiments produced a case in which a change of evaluation protocol, of which blinding was one part, was followed by a change not just in the size of the effect but in its direction.

The question this paper asks is plain: can unblinded AI alignment evaluation get the direction of a result wrong? The answer the record supports is narrower than the question. In an earlier experiment (v4), scored without blinding, two model families showed alignment scores that rose with reasoning depth. In the later, unregistered March 2026 run (v5), which added a bundle of blinding steps and also changed the responses, prompts, token allowances, depth levels, panel and Gemini model, one of those trends was no longer detected and the other was significantly negative, while a third family showed no significant trend in either run (Section 5). The record shows that the reported direction did not hold across the two protocols. It does not show that blinding caused the change, or which direction is correct, and an internal code review of October 2026 found defects in the March run that bind every reading of it (Sections 5 and 10).

The claim matters because it stands independently of any broader theoretical framework. Even if the ARC Principle (Law I, a law under test), the Eden Protocol, or the wider scaling-law synthesis were later weakened, the result of this comparison would still stand.

Our contributions are as follows.

  1. A documented comparison, within one research programme, of an earlier unblinded experiment (v4) and the later, unregistered March 2026 run (v5) of the same alignment-depth question, in which the reported direction did not hold for two of the three model families compared: one positive trend was no longer detected, and the other was significantly negative on a later Gemini model (Section 5, Table 2); the fourth v4 subject, whose v4 data were incomplete, is reported beside them.

2 Related work

Biases of model judges.

That model judges carry biases of the kinds Section 3 lists is established, in settings other than this one. Zheng et al. (2023) examine the position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases of model judges and report that GPT-4 as a judge agrees with human preferences over 80 per cent of the time, the level at which humans agree with one another. Their pairwise judge prompt already asks the judge not to let the length of the responses or the names of the assistants influence it; they describe calling a judge twice with the order of the two answers swapped; and they write that, with limited data and small differences, their study could not determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias, because a controlled study is hard when “we cannot easily rephrase a response to fit the style of another model without changing the quality”. Dubois et al. (2024) report that a preference for longer outputs remains in existing automated evaluation metrics, and control for it with a regression that predicts the judge’s preference as if the two outputs had the same length; on AlpacaEval this raised the Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98. Wang et al. (2024) show that the order of two answers alone can change a judge’s verdict: with ChatGPT as the evaluator, reordering let Vicuna-13B beat ChatGPT on 66 of 80 queries. Koo et al. (2024) find, across sixteen models and six biases, that models are biased evaluators of text quality and that “all models are dramatically influenced by real model names”. Chen and Goldfarb-Tarrant (2025) report that, for judges of safety, an apologetic sentence added to otherwise unchanged completions skewed preferences by up to 98 per cent in a test of tie detection, and that the best jury they tested was still not robust to all such artefacts, apology and position among them; a rephrase that kept a completion’s meaning and lengthened it produced negligible shifts in their test of win rates.

Labels and provenance cues.

Displayed source labels and provenance cues move judges on their own. Saraf et al. (2025) had blog posts written by three vendors’ models judged by all three under four labelling conditions, and report that false attribution “frequently reversed preference rankings”, with shifts of up to 50 percentage points in voting outcomes and up to 12 percentage points in quality ratings; they call for blind evaluation protocols. Marioriyad et al. (2025) attach superficial provenance cues to candidate responses while the rest of the prompt stays unchanged, and find a clear provenance hierarchy in two judges’ verdicts (expert, then human, then model, then unknown) and that the judges rarely acknowledged the cue.

Self-recognition and self-preference.

Judges also recognise and favour text like their own without any label. Panickssery et al. (2024) report that models such as GPT-4 and Llama 2 have non-trivial accuracy at distinguishing their own outputs from those of other models and of humans, and a linear correlation, under fine-tuning, between that self-recognition and the strength of self-preference; they suggest perturbing or paraphrasing generated text to inhibit self-recognition, which they do not test. Wataoka et al. (2024) measure self-preference with a quantitative metric, find a significant degree of it in GPT-4, and find that judges give significantly higher evaluations than human evaluators to outputs with lower perplexity, whether or not the outputs were their own. Spiliopoulou et al. (2025) find that some models systematically assign higher scores to their own outputs, and to outputs of other models of the same family, measured against third-party ratings of quality. Mahbub and Feng (2025) report that judges distinguish their own outputs without labels, that small perturbations reduce self-preference, and that a fuller neutralisation of style lets it recover.

Studies of 2026.

Three studies of 2026 refine these readings. Roytburg et al. (2026), first posted six weeks before the March 2026 run, report that only 51 per cent of examples in earlier self-preference findings keep statistical significance against a null hypothesis built on evaluator quality. Pombal et al. (2026) and Chae et al. (2026) were posted after the run and after this paper’s first publication, so they bear on present readings and on no claim about March 2026: Pombal et al. (2026) find that self-preference persists even when the evaluation criteria are entirely objective, and that ensembling several judges mitigates it without fully eliminating it; Chae et al. (2026) find that, on items that carry no stylistic fingerprint, self-preference disappears under blind evaluation, with a small effect remaining in the opposite direction once selection quality and judge severity are controlled, while self- and other-labels alone shift scores in both directions.

Model judgement in alignment work.

Model judgement is also built into alignment training and testing. Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a preference model on human comparisons and optimises a helpful and harmless assistant against it (Bai, Jones, et al. 2022); Constitutional AI shapes a model’s outputs during training against a written set of principles, using feedback from an AI model in place of human labels for harmlessness (Bai, Kadavath, et al. 2022); and automated red teaming uses one language model to generate the test cases that find harmful behaviour in another (Perez et al. 2022).

Safety-relevant behaviour against reasoning length.

Such behaviour has itself been measured against reasoning length. Zaremba et al. (2025) report, for two reasoning models, that “across a variety of attacks, increased inference-time compute leads to improved robustness”, and that in many cases, with important exceptions, the fraction of model samples on which an attack succeeds tends to zero as test-time compute grows. Gema et al. (2025) construct tasks on which extending the reasoning length of large reasoning models deteriorates performance, among them tasks on advanced AI risks; they report that “extended reasoning may amplify concerning behaviors, with Claude Sonnet 4 showing increased expressions of self-preservation”, and that their results “demonstrate the importance of evaluating models across diverse reasoning lengths”.

A different sense of double-blind.

The phrase “double-blind” is also used for a different property. A technical report dated 27 August 2026, by thirty authors from AVERI, Google, Singapore AISI, OpenMined and ML Commons (Trask et al. 2026), describes “double-blind evals”, which “provide cryptographic guarantees that an evaluator’s prompts are not shared with the model owner, while simultaneously maintaining the confidentiality of the model weights and inference code”, and pilots them by evaluating Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite against a selection of the private AILuminate benchmark corpus. That is mutual secrecy between the owner of a model and the owner of a benchmark. The blinding examined here concerns what a judge can tell about whose answer it is scoring. The two are distinct layers of an evaluation, protect against different failures, and neither bears on the other’s priority.

What this paper adds.

Against this work, this paper reports one archived comparison of an alignment-depth trend under two bundled protocols, made in March 2026, whose cause is open (Section 10). The abstracts of the works above state no comparison of two scoring protocols on an alignment-depth slope. This paper does not add the discovery that judges are biased or that labels move them.

3 The bias problem

Alignment evaluation is vulnerable to several sources of bias:

  • Identity bias: scorers may expect certain models to be safer, wiser, or more sophisticated.

  • Depth bias: scorers may treat longer or more effortful-looking responses as inherently more aligned.

  • Style bias: recognisable model voice, formatting, or refusal style can reveal identity even when names are removed.

  • Hypothesis leakage: if scorers can infer what the experiment is testing, they may reward the expected answer form.

These are not trivial concerns in an alignment context. Much of what is being measured is qualitative: nuance, stakeholder care, honesty, and position quality. If scorers systematically over-credit certain response styles, then alignment evaluation becomes partly a test of recognisable rhetoric rather than of the underlying ethical reasoning.

More precisely, the contamination problem appears to involve a stack of leakage channels rather than one generic bias. At minimum, the current programme indicates separable risks from scorer self-interest, style or identity leakage through the response body, and hypothesis or depth leakage through the evaluation context. The v5 protocol addressed each channel with its own step rather than merely claiming to be ‘more careful’ in the abstract, though it applied the steps together and tested none of them alone (Section 8).

4 Experimental comparison and protocol layers

The result comes from comparing two experiments of the same research programme. Both experiments ran on 11 and 12 March 2026, by the timestamps their own result files record: v4 in the first hours of 11 March, and v5 from 07:12 on 11 March to 11:27 on 12 March (UTC). The prediction both were built to test, that alignment imposed from outside scales with an exponent near zero while values built into the reasoning scale with capability, was public from 9 February 2026 (GitHub push and OSF upload of Paper III; first posted on 22 January 2026 in a design withdrawn the next day), with the criterion that would refute it from 13 February 2026 (OSF upload); the runners of both experiments were first published on 16 March 2026 (GitHub push), the v5 runner in a revision later than the one that produced four of its six result files (Code and data). Appendix B gives each record with its kind of timestamp.

The crucial point is that the v5 protocol was not simply a larger rerun. It was a redesign intended to close multiple bias pathways at once. That makes the comparison informative about whether the earlier positive trends held up under the redesigned protocol; because it changed many things at once, it cannot say which change mattered. Table 1 lists the scoring side. The runs also differed on the generation side. v5 generated new responses from six models rather than four, on a different alignment prompt set (Paper IV.a, version 1.9, Section 2.2; Paper IV.c, version 1.10, Section 2.1), at five or six depth levels rather than four or five. Its token allowances were raised from 8,192 or 16,000 tokens to each model’s maximum (Paper IV.a, Section 5.5, which records that 48.2 per cent of DeepSeek V3.2’s v4 responses at the exhaustive level reached the 8,192-token reasoning cap), and its Gemini model was a later one than v4’s (Paper IV.a, Section 2.1). The v4 statistic was Spearman’s rank correlation across depth levels; the v5 statistic is Cohen’s dd between the shallowest and deepest levels, with a rank correlation over realised reasoning tokens beside it.

The multi-layer blind evaluation protocol.

The relevant contribution is best understood as a single protocol rather than several disconnected tricks. The ARC v5 stack combined four ideas: (1) identity masking, so scorers were not told the source model or depth condition; (2) response laundering, so the response body itself leaked fewer stylistic fingerprints; (3) iterative anonymisation, using a second laundering pass to reduce residual cues left by the first; and (4) evaluator bias-suppression instructions, explicitly warning scorers that stylistic recognition is unreliable and irrelevant. On top of that sat cross-model scoring by every available model except the subject (as run, the exclusion of the subject model alone: ‘The panel as run’, below), tier-weighted consensus, dissent tracking, conservative-bias safeguards, and order randomisation. The design’s aim was therefore not merely to blind the scorer but to make the blindness real and auditable; ‘The panel as run’ and Section 4.1 record where the run fell short of it.

The panel as run.

As run, the panel excluded the subject model itself and no other model. The adapters named for subject models scored with their scoring endpoints, not the subject models: the adapter named for Claude Opus 4.6 scored with Claude Sonnet 4.6, the same model as the dedicated Sonnet scorer, so Claude Sonnet 4.6 scored Claude Opus 4.6 on every scored entry at the panel’s highest weight and, in every other run, scored each entry twice, at weights 1.5 and 1.0 (35 to 39 per cent of the panel weight); the adapter named for Gemini 3 Flash scored with Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite; the DeepSeek adapter scored with the chat endpoint; GPT-OSS-120B, OpenAI’s open-weight model served by Groq and labelled by its host in the runner’s family table, scored GPT-5.4 on every scored entry; Claude Opus 4.6 scored no entry. A seven-judge panel therefore held six distinct models and a six-judge panel five, every inter-judge agreement statistic includes the pair of Sonnet identities, and the laundering pool likewise included the subject’s own developer’s models for the Claude Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.4 runs and, as launderers only, for the DeepSeek V3.2 and Gemini 3 Flash runs (Section 4.1). Exclusion by developer, checked in code, is carried by the programme’s successor suite, a later benchmark whose design is written and not yet registered; the published scores stand as scored, and a re-aggregation that excludes same-developer judges is a sensitivity analysis for the re-analysis that is to be registered, not a correction made here.

4.1 Response laundering protocol

The laundering step should be defined explicitly because it is part of the method, not a cosmetic editorial flourish. The objective is simple: same meaning, less fingerprint. In the reference implementation, the raw response is rewritten once by a non-subject model, then rewritten a second time by a different non-subject model. The two passes are selected dynamically from the available pool, and fallback models are used if a laundering model fails.

  • Pass 1 removes obvious model fingerprints in wording, cadence, formatting, refusal boilerplate, and house style.

  • Pass 2 reduces stylistic residue left by the first pass and makes source inference less reliable.

  • Meta-commentary outputs that describe the rewriting process rather than genuinely paraphrasing the content are rejected (a check the runner gained at version 5.4.2, after the three earliest v5 runs had started on an earlier build; Section 10).

  • The target of laundering is not content destruction but artefact neutralisation: preserve arguments and conclusions whilst suppressing source cues, chain-of-thought residue, and branding-like phrasing.

This matters because simple anonymisation can be weak: judges can recognise their own outputs without labels (Panickssery et al. 2024; Mahbub and Feng 2025). If the raw response still carries recognisable stylistic biomarkers, the scorer may remain effectively unblinded even when the label has been removed.

Two features of the archived v5 files qualify this description. First, the launderers recorded against each entry include a model from the subject’s own developer for 89 of the 350 laundered Gemini 3 Flash entries, 109 of the 416 DeepSeek V3.2 entries, 77 of the 350 GPT-5.4 entries and 222 of the 350 Claude Opus 4.6 entries, and for none of the Grok 4.1 Fast or Groq Qwen3 entries; for GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6 the pipeline’s own model table names that launderer as the subject model itself. Second, some laundered texts open with the launderer’s own working on the rewriting task in a reasoning block: 50 Gemini 3 Flash, 43 DeepSeek V3.2, 34 GPT-5.4, 3 Claude Opus 4.6, 2 Grok 4.1 Fast and no Groq Qwen3 entries. In the published pipeline these laundered texts are what the scorers receive. In those entries the scored text was not the protocol’s clean rewrite by two models from other developers. Laundering is generative rewriting in any case, with no guarantee of preserved meaning: a rewrite can drop a caveat, a refusal, an affected party or the action being judged, and the v5 run recorded no check of preserved meaning. Zheng et al. (2023) named the same difficulty: “we cannot easily rephrase a response to fit the style of another model without changing the quality”.

As run, the two laundering models of each entry were drawn from the same pool as its judges, so on every laundered entry one or both of the models that wrote the paraphrase also scored it; the panel excluded the subject and nothing else. The successor suite’s design draws launderers from models that never judge the same entry.

5 Main result

The result is easiest to see at the level of direction rather than fine-grained statistics (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Table 2 gives the values printed by the pipeline summary of 12 March 2026. Recomputing them from the archived final records (Paper IV.a, Section 3.1, recomputation note of 2 September 2026) alters no direction in this table; the dd for GPT-5.4 shifts from −0.08-0.08 to +0.07+0.07 and stays non-significant. No equivalence margin was set, so GPT-5.4’s two non-significant results show that no trend was detected in either run, not that none exists; it is a comparison case rather than a control. The table compares two runs, not two scorings of the same responses, and its two columns report different statistics, so they are not on one scale; no test of the difference between them was made. A significant result in one run and a non-significant one in the other is not itself evidence that the two differ (Gelman and Stern 2006). The Gemini case, with significant trends of opposite sign, is the stronger of the two, though it compares two Gemini models.

Claude Opus 4.6 was the fourth v4 subject and has no row in Table 2: exhausted credit left its v4 data incomplete at the deep and exhaustive levels, and Paper IV.a (version 1.9, Sections 2.1 and 3.1) leaves its v4 trend unclassified, with a preliminary flat reading. In v5 its dd was +1.27+1.27 (Figure 1), with a judge from its own developer at the panel’s highest weight (Section 4). Its two readings also differ, in the direction opposite to Gemini’s, though its v4 data are too incomplete for the two to be compared.

As recorded in the archived files, the laundering step was called through each model’s scoring endpoint, whose output budget capped the paraphrase (512 tokens in the build that produced the DeepSeek V3.2, Gemini 3 Flash and GPT-5.4 files; 1,024 to 4,096 in the later build; no cap where a Gemini model wrote the paraphrase). In those three files the paraphrase the judges scored was, at the median, 0.56 to 0.91 of the original’s length at the deepest levels and 0.72 to 1.56 at the minimal level, with 40 to 57 per cent of deep entries below 0.6; in the Grok 4.1 Fast and Claude Opus 4.6 files the deep paraphrases were 1.2 to 1.4 times the original and under 6 per cent fell below 0.6. The three-tier hierarchy and the v4 to v5 comparison are therefore confounded with how much of each deep response reached the judges, in the direction that lowers deep scores where the texts were cut. The re-scoring of the stored original responses, in the re-analysis to be registered for it, is the test of whether the contrasts survive; until it reports, the hierarchy is a single-run result under this caveat.

The dd and pp values of Table 2 treat the scored entries as independent; the same prompts appear at every depth, so a prompt-paired analysis (the per-prompt difference between the deepest and shallowest level, with a bootstrap over prompts) is the appropriate reading, and the successor suite’s design takes the per-prompt slope as its unit. Pairing changes the variance of a difference between levels, not its sign where every prompt is scored at both levels, so the directions are expected to stand and the stated precisions to change.

The dd values in the v5 column compare the shallowest and deepest labels on all scored entries; the pp values are not one test: the pipeline computes a Spearman correlation of realised reasoning tokens with score over entries that report tokens, a Kruskal-Wallis test over depth labels and a Mann-Whitney test of the shallowest label against the rest, none of them paired by prompt, and the realised-token path holds no entry for Groq Qwen3 (the provider reports no reasoning tokens), none of GPT-5.4’s minimal level and 21 of 70 of Claude Opus 4.6’s.

Finding 1: the measured direction of alignment scaling did not hold between protocols.

For two model families, the direction of the alignment-depth relationship reported in the unblinded v4 experiment did not hold in the v5 run, which added the blinding steps (Figure 1): Gemini’s significant positive association became significantly negative, on a later model, and DeepSeek V3.2’s became statistically null. It indicates that the direction reported depended on something that differed between the runs: the scoring protocol, of which blinding was one part, or a difference on the generation side (Sections 4 and 10). The comparison does not say which, or which direction is nearer the truth. GPT-5.4 remains near zero across protocols; it is also the one model whose depth ladder varied a reasoning parameter alone and carried no instruction prefix, so its flat reading is consistent with a null-control and with an absent instruction effect, and the two cannot be told apart in this record. This is currently a single-programme observation. The test it calls for scores identical responses with one part of the protocol changed at a time; the re-analysis of the stored original responses described above is to be registered before it runs. Replication by an outside team, and these controlled tests, are the load-bearing next steps.

6 Why the measured direction can change

One candidate mechanism is ordinary evaluator inference. Longer, more elaborate, more self-conscious responses often look safer. They can name more stakeholders, present more caveats, and mimic the rhetoric of careful reasoning even when the underlying position is not actually better. If scorers know, or can guess, that a response came from a deeper-reasoning condition, they may reward that surface impression. Others are reported in the literature: judges favouring text that is more familiar to them, measured as lower perplexity (Wataoka et al. 2024), and an added apologetic opening that moved safety judges’ verdicts on its own (Chen and Goldfarb-Tarrant 2025). The archived v5 record adds two more: the deepest paraphrases cut by an output cap (Section 5) and the consensus rule (Section 10). This paper does not separate them (Section 10).

Response laundering may matter here as much as removing labels; the v5 comparison cannot apportion the two. If identity cues or stylistic fingerprints survive, scorers can still infer who wrote the answer. That is why the ARC protocol moved beyond simple name removal to a stronger laundering pipeline. The key methodological point, which earlier work supports (Panickssery et al. 2024; Mahbub and Feng 2025), is that scoring with labels removed is not necessarily blind: models can recognise their own text from the text alone; the cues, in this paper’s reading, include tone, formatting, verbosity, refusal structure and depth-like rhetoric.

Methodological principle: blind scoring is insufficient without evidence blinding.

Response laundering is not a cosmetic clean-up step; it is part of the same protocol. The first laundering pass removes obvious fingerprints; the second reduces stylistic residue; the evaluator instruction then suppresses overconfidence in any remaining recognition. Together they are designed to convert simple metadata blinding into a stronger form of evidence blinding; whether they do is a question for a source-recognition test (Section 10.1), which the v5 run did not report. Combined with cross-model scoring that leaves the subject model out, dissent tracking and a consensus rule that takes the lower of two summaries when judges disagree widely (itself a candidate mechanism, Section 10), this becomes the beginning of a leakage-control framework rather than a one-off patch.

7 Implications for AI safety research

The implications listed below are exploratory: they are conditional forward-looking claims about how the field should respond if the change of direction is confirmed by independent replication. They are not themselves tested by any programme draft study, and no numerical claim in this section rides on them. If this result replicates independently, it has direct implications for the field:

  1. Some published alignment effects may be inflated. A benchmark can report ‘alignment improves with depth’ where blinded scoring finds the trend flat or negative.

  2. Multi-layer blinding should become default methodology. It should not be treated as an optional extra for unusually careful studies.

  3. Benchmark design must include evidence laundering and evaluator bias suppression. Name removal alone is not sufficient if style leaks identity.

  4. Consensus architecture matters. Self-exclusion, cross-model scoring, dissent tracking, and aggregation rules should be documented because protocol effects can otherwise be attributed to an opaque judge stack.

  5. Methods papers matter as much as theory papers. Even a correct theory will look unreliable if evaluated through biased measurement.

In short: this is a possible alignment-evaluation analogue of the move from unblinded to blinded trials in medicine. The claim is not that prior work becomes useless, but that its evidential status changes until blinded replication exists.

8 Recommended protocol

Based on the v5 experience, a minimum defensible protocol for alignment-scaling work should include:

  • Self-exclusion by developer, so that no model scores or rewrites output from its own developer’s models, checked by code on every entry. Judges are reported to favour outputs of their own family (Spiliopoulou et al. 2025; Pombal et al. 2026), which excluding the model alone does not remove.

  • Where available, all models from other developers contribute blinded scores under a documented consensus rule.

  • Identity masking before scoring.

  • Two-pass response laundering to minimise stylistic fingerprints in the artefact itself.

  • A check of preserved meaning and length on every laundered text, with the original and laundered lengths recorded and the output budget set from the input’s length, never a fixed cap below it; a rewrite that fails the check is scored as missing, never replaced by the raw reply.

  • Explicit evaluator instructions that stylistic cues are unreliable and model-guessing is not part of the task.

  • Depth-condition laundering so scorers cannot infer shallow versus deep responses from explicit tags, with response length, which can itself signal depth, recorded and reported.

  • Order randomisation across conditions.

  • Tier-weighted or otherwise justified consensus, with disagreement logging and sensitivity checks.

  • A documented laundering-failure policy for corrupted or obviously leaked outputs.

  • Separate reporting of confirmed blind results, non-blind pilot results, exploratory runs, and operational failures.

  • A source-recognition test with a positive control, reported beside every blinded result.

  • Laundered texts that carry the launderer’s reasoning or commentary on the task rejected as laundering failures.

  • Each layer of the stack tested on identical responses, one at a time, before the stack is relied on.

Recommendation: a reporting convention that separates the classes of evidence.

Benchmark papers should distinguish clearly between blinded confirmed findings, non-blind pilots, exploratory runs, and failed runs. Without that separation, a reader cannot tell which evidence a claim rests on.

9 What this paper does and does not show

Scope of the claim.

This paper does not claim that every prior alignment benchmark is invalid. It makes a narrower claim: in this experimental setting, the measured direction of an effect changed between a run in which model identity, response style and expected reasoning depth were visible to evaluators and a run that masked identity and depth, paraphrased the responses and changed the panel. Because those changes came together, and with others on the generation side (Section 4), the comparison does not show that visibility caused the change, or that multi-layer blinding rather than label removal is necessary; it shows that, for this class of evaluation, each part of the protocol has to be tested before either direction is trusted. That is sufficient to make multi-layer blinding, not mere label removal, the protocol this programme requires for this class of evaluation, and to make its necessity an open question, for a test that is to be registered.

What this paper carries.

This paper carries a result: within one line of alignment experiments, between unblinded scoring and a protocol with multi-layer blinding steps, which also differed in the ways set out in Section 4, a positive alignment-depth trend reported for one model family was no longer detected and one reported for another was significantly negative, on a later model, whilst a third family showed no significant trend in either run. Inside the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation) it is one of the ARC/Eden experiments: a documented case against the assumption that an AI-scored alignment evaluation reports the same direction whatever its protocol. A fuller comparison with earlier work is in Eden Vision, section II.A.8.

Evidence status.

The record supports one comparison, between an earlier unblinded experiment (v4) and the later, unregistered March 2026 run (v5) of the same question; its result is stated above. The v5 run is one run of six models from six providers, 2,170 responses each scored by five or six distinct model judges behind two paraphrase passes, with its result files and a later revision of its runner public (Code and data); its results are graded as a single unregistered run under the caveat of Section 5, and whether the contrasts survive is the question for a re-analysis that is to be registered before it runs. The paper does not show that blinding caused the change or which direction is correct, and it gives no verdict on any law of the ARC Theory. It reports no safety measure of its own, so it has no usefulness side to pair with one: its result concerns how an existing alignment score behaved under two protocols, and the score is Paper IV.c’s.

Epistemic status. What this programme names Laws are conjectures. Laws I to III, registered on 8 September 2026, are laws under test; no registered test of any of the five laws has yet returned a verdict. Laws IV and V were added on 24 September 2026 and registered as laws on 15 October 2026 in version 1.103 of the theory registration; neither has been tested. Every quantity in this paper is operationally defined, and established-law standing is claimed nowhere. The registered programme exists to earn that standing, or lose it, by measurement, replication and survived refutation.

10 Limitations

This paper is not the end of the argument. It has important limitations:

  • The result currently comes from one research programme, not an outside lab.

  • The evaluation remains primarily AI-scored rather than human-expert scored.

  • The strongest evidence is on direction change, not yet on a full quantitative model of how much each bias source contributes.

  • The v4 and v5 runs differ in more than blinding: in the responses scored, the model set, the prompts, the token allowances, the depth levels, the Gemini model, the panel and its aggregation, and the statistic reported (Section 4). This paper therefore shows that the reported direction did not hold between protocols more clearly than it apportions the change among them, and it cannot say which direction is correct.

  • In three of the six final v5 files (DeepSeek V3.2, Gemini 3 Flash and GPT-5.4), made by an earlier build of the runner, a laundering-fallback flag is set on every paraphrased entry. The same entries record two completed paraphrase passes by the study’s own pool of models, and the runner’s change log records a fix to a false positive in that flag (version 5.4.2), so the flag does not show that the laundering failed. The paraphrases were made, but not all were faithful: in those three files the deepest were shortened by the paraphrase step’s output budget (Section 5), and that build did not yet reject a paraphrase that had turned into commentary on the rewriting task, which the programme’s interim analysis of 11 March 2026 found in at least three entries (the programme’s working report, osf.io/j6mkb, chapter 27). The same build used Grok 3 mini as a judge and launderer, and 512-token scorer and launderer budgets. Four of DeepSeek V3.2’s 420 panel-scored entries were not paraphrased and were scored on the raw reply, recorded as not laundered.

  • In four of the six files the launderers include a model from the subject’s own developer, on 22 to 63 per cent of laundered entries, and some scored texts carry a launderer’s reasoning block (Section 4.1); both divergent families are among the four, and on every laundered entry at least one launderer also judged it.

  • No test of whether the v5 scorers could identify the source of a laundered response is reported, here or in Papers IV.a and IV.c, so whether the blind held is unmeasured (the third defeat condition in Section 10.1).

  • Self-exclusion was by model, not by developer, so two panels kept a scorer from the subject’s developer (Section 4, ‘The panel as run’); neither divergent family had one.

  • The pipeline extracted each judge’s score by one of five strategies: a JSON object, a JSON object found in the text, a score: pattern, a number following a scoring verb, or a retry asking for a bare number. In the DeepSeek V3.2, Gemini 3 Flash and GPT-5.4 files the Groq Qwen3 judge’s score was read by the last three strategies on 73 to 86 per cent of its calls, from the text of a truncated thinking block, and the other judges on 0 to 10 per cent; in the other three files every judge was read from JSON on 97 per cent or more of calls. A number read from prose by those strategies can be an anchor example’s score, a pillar score, a dimension count or a zero from the phrase ‘0-100’, and no rule excluded such a value from the consensus.

  • v5 also changed the aggregation: the consensus took the lower of a tier-weighted mean and the median where the judges’ standard deviation exceeded 15 points. If judges disagree more on deeper answers, that rule lowers deep scores more than shallow ones and moves a depth contrast on its own; whether it did is a sensitivity question for the re-analysis to be registered (the contrast recomputed on the simple mean and the median, with the share of high-disagreement entries by depth), not a claim made here.

Those limits narrow the conclusion to what the record shows: two runs of one question, under different protocols, did not report the same direction for two of the three families compared. That is enough to justify asking alignment evaluations to test their protocols one part at a time, and to report which parts they have tested, before relying on either direction.

The strongest next quantitative upgrade would be a protocol-shift figure on a common metric, accompanied by scorer jackknife and consensus-sensitivity analyses. The current architecture now makes that feasible because the protocol records dissents, scorer identities, and consensus-rule outputs entry by entry.

10.1 Falsifiability: what would defeat the hypothesis

The central hypothesis, that unblinded AI alignment evaluation can be wrong about the direction of an effect, and the programme’s reading that blinding is therefore required, are defeated by any of the following.

  1. No blind/unblind divergence on a clean re-run. The evidence is the v4 (unblinded) to v5 change: Gemini’s significant positive association became significantly negative, on a later Gemini model, and DeepSeek’s significant positive association became statistically null (its point estimate fell below zero, but the v5 association is not significant). The hypothesis is defeated if an independent controlled replication that changes only the blinding, holding scorers, prompts, model versions, token allowances, depth levels and consensus rule constant, on identical responses, finds no systematic difference between blinded and unblinded scoring.

  2. Divergence attributable to a confound, not blinding. Section 10 concedes several protocol components changed together between v4 and v5. The hypothesis is defeated if the direction change is fully explained by one of those confounds (the responses and prompts, the token allowances and depth levels, the Gemini model, the panel and its aggregation rule, the paraphrase step’s output cap, or launderers who also judged) rather than the blinding manipulation itself.

  3. Blinding leakage. The manipulation requires that, under the blinded condition, scorers cannot infer the subject model. The hypothesis is defeated if scorers can identify the subject above chance from stylistic tells despite the blind; the “blinded” result would then not be blinded at all.

  4. Self-preference persists under blinding. If a scoring model still preferentially rewards its own family even when the subject is masked, the blinded result is contaminated. The hypothesis is defeated if such self-preference is shown to survive the blind.

    Self-preference with no source label shown has been observed in other settings (Wataoka et al. 2024; Panickssery et al. 2024; Spiliopoulou et al. 2025), and it returned under a fuller neutralisation of style (Mahbub and Feng 2025), so this condition is live for any laundering protocol, this one included. One study reports it disappearing under blind evaluation on items that carry no stylistic fingerprint (Chae et al. 2026), and a self-preference measure needs a baseline for evaluator quality (Roytburg et al. 2026); nothing in this paper bears on those reports.

  5. Direction does not survive proper power. Section 5 states the result at the level of direction, not fine-grained statistics. As anything stronger than a directional, single-programme case, it is defeated if, when adequately powered and human-expert scored, the change of direction does not replicate.

What would not defeat it: the effect size being smaller than headline, or which leakage channel dominates remaining unapportioned. The hypothesis concerns only the direction of an effect; a single clean, confound-controlled case would establish it; the v4 to v5 comparison is not yet such a case (Section 10), which is why the controlled comparison is the next step.

11 Conclusion

The central claim of this paper is simple: the direction an AI-scored alignment evaluation reports can depend on its protocol. In this programme’s two runs, which differed in their scoring protocol and in how the responses were generated, the reported direction did not hold for two of the three model families compared. That is a methodological finding, not a theoretical embellishment; whether blinding is the part of the protocol responsible is what the controlled comparison will show. It does not depend on the ARC Principle (Law I, a law under test) being a universal law, and it does not depend on the Eden Protocol being fully validated.

If outside teams replicate this result under conditions that isolate blinding, the consequence is straightforward. Future alignment benchmarks will need to adopt multi-layer blinding, including laundering and evaluator bias-suppression instructions, as routine scientific controls. If they do not, their conclusions about which models are getting safer, flatter, or more dangerous with depth will remain methodologically vulnerable.

Bottom line.

The most important near-term question raised by this paper is no longer theoretical. It is operational: will the field keep relying on alignment evaluations whose scoring protocols have never been tested one part at a time, now that a reported result has been seen not to hold when the protocol changed?
