                                                         Abstract
           I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it
       reasons; only proof will move the world. Does intelligence compound when each step builds on
       the last, or merely accumulate? This paper formalises and preliminarily tests Law I of the ARC
       Theory, the ARC Principle (Artificial Recursive Creation), which I first stated in an earlier form,
       as the ARC Hypothesis, in my manuscripts of 8 December 2024 and published, with the exponent
       two, in Infinite Architects (2026). As registered, it says that usable capability is a power of recursive
       depth, 𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 , with the exponent 𝛼 measured rather than assumed. The paper’s hypothesis is that
       sequential recursion, in which each step builds on the last, scales super-linearly and faster than parallel
       recursion over independent samples. On published reasoning-model data, parallel sampling gives
       𝛼 ≈ 0.1 to 0.3, and two points of sequential reasoning give 𝛼 ≈ 1.34; the two series differ in system,
       benchmark and resource axis as well as in the form of recursion, and the sequential points are two
       versions of one model, so their contrast is held as a hypothesis. A later measurement on five frontier
       models (Paper II) finds one interpretable sequential fit, on Gemini 3 Flash: a regression exponent of
       0.49 (standard error 0.20) and an endpoint exponent of 0.59 with a bootstrap 95 per cent interval of
       −1.3 to 2.9. That is one model’s fit and inconclusive, so super-linear scaling is unresolved rather than
       established. The paper also states the ARC Bound, the conjectured value two of Law III’s ceiling:
       𝛼 = 2 as a stability ceiling, not an impossibility limit, which a durably stable system above two would
       refute. Falsification criteria and their three-outcome decision form are given. Law I remains a law
       under test.

1    Introduction
I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons;
only proof will move the world. If control of a capable AI system were lost and could not be won back,
what the system itself carries would decide what it does. The programme’s position is that people keep
oversight, containment and shutdown for as long as they physically can, and never give them up by choice;
correction built into a system is the contingency for control lost against our will, not a reason to let
go of it. Whether any correction can keep pace depends first on how capability grows when a system
works on its own output, and that is this paper’s question: does intelligence compound when each step
builds on the last, or merely accumulate? In plain words, its answer so far is this. Published data from
reasoning models suggest that reasoning built step by step gains more from extra effort than voting over
independent attempts, but the two series compared differ in more than the form of reasoning, and the one
later measurement able to read an exponent cleanly is inconclusive. The question stays open, and the
paper states it in a form that can be tested and refuted.
    This paper belongs to the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation), the founding
theory of Recursive Dynamics, the science of systems that improve themselves, which the author founded
(the founding paper). The theory is carried by five laws, each a law under test: Law I, the ARC Principle,
that usable capability is a power of recursive depth, with the exponent measured rather than assumed;
Law II, the ARC Co-Scaling Law; Law III, the ARC Ceiling; and Laws IV and V, the ARC Persistence
Law and the ARC Embedding Law, added on 24 September 2026 and registered as laws on 15 October
2026, their mathematics in development. The laws and twenty-two propositions are registered on OSF
(the theory registration), and the statement paper states the theory whole. This paper states Law I formally
and gives a preliminary result for it, and it states the ARC Bound, the conjectured value of Law III’s
ceiling; Appendix E places it among the laws.
    The scaling laws governing artificial intelligence have been extensively studied. Kaplan et al. (2020)
established power-law relationships between model performance and parameters, while Hoffmann et al.
(2022) refined these with compute-optimal training prescriptions. These laws govern what to scale but do
not address why scaling produces intelligent behaviour.
    The emergence of reasoning models in 2024 and 2025 introduced a new variable: test-time compute.
OpenAI’s o1 (September 2024; OpenAI 2024) and DeepSeek’s R1 (January 2025; DeepSeek-AI 2025a)
allocate computational resources at inference time to reason before responding, producing substantial
capability improvements on reasoning benchmarks.
    This paper’s hypothesis is that test-time compute serves as a proxy for recursive depth, and that
recursive depth may be a fundamental driver of capability amplification in artificial intelligence systems.
    Our contributions are:
    1. A formal statement of Law I, the ARC Principle, as the ARC Equation 𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 , equation (1),
       with its terms, and definitions of parallel and sequential recursion (Definitions 1 to 3), a contrast
       drawn earlier, within single models, in the study of test-time compute (Section 2).
    2. An estimator of 𝛼 from error rates at two depths, equation (3), applied to published reasoning-model
       data: 𝛼 ≈ 0.1 to 0.3 for parallel sampling and 𝛼 ≈ 1.34 from two points of sequential reasoning,
       with the reasons their contrast is held as a hypothesis (Section 4).
    3. The quadratic stability ceiling hypothesis: 𝛼 = 2, the ARC Bound, as the conjectured value of
       Law III’s ceiling 𝛼crit = 1/(1 − 𝛾), with the domain on which a ceiling of two is equivalent to
       𝛾 ≤ 1/2 (Proposition 1) and what would refute it (Section 3.3).
    4. Falsification criteria for the principle, the three-outcome decision form they inherit, and a programme
       hypothesis about human-in-the-loop oversight (Section 5).

2    Related work, antecedents and the rival instrument
This section states each debt to prior work in the source’s own terms, beginning with the work nearest
to this paper’s own empirical question, the scaling of test-time compute, then the scaling premise the
programme borrows, and ending with the nearest work on how oversight scales and its antecedents.

Test-time compute, studied directly. The scaling of test-time compute in language models has been
studied directly, and the contrast this paper draws between sequential and parallel recursion was drawn
earlier within single models. Snell et al. (2024), first posted on 6 August 2024, compare sequential
revisions with parallel samples under one generation budget and report that “while increased sequential
revisions tends to outperform more parallel compute, at higher generation budgets there is an ideal ratio
that strikes a balance between the two extremes”, the ratio depending on the difficulty of the question.
Brown et al. (2024), first posted on 31 July 2024, report that under repeated sampling “the relationship
between coverage and the number of samples is often log-linear and can be modelled with an exponentiated
power law”, while majority voting and reward models “plateau beyond several hundred samples and fail to
fully scale with the sample budget”. Muennighoff et al. (2025), first posted on 31 January 2025, lengthen
one model’s thinking by budget forcing, which “allows extrapolating beyond its performance without
test-time intervention: from 50% to 57% on AIME24”. Zeng et al. (2025), first posted on 17 February
2025, find that the longer chains of thought of o1-like models “do not consistently enhance accuracy”
and, comparing “sequential and parallel scaling strategies on QwQ, R1 and LIMO”, that “parallel scaling
achieves better coverage and scalability”. The published evidence within single models is therefore
mixed, and the comparison of Zeng et al. (2025), which holds the system fixed, runs against this paper’s
hypothesis. What this paper adds is the statement of the contrast as a difference between exponents of one
power law in recursive depth, equation (1), the two-point estimates of Section 4, and the stability ceiling
of Section 3.3.

The pipes premise, with its contest. The premise that transport networks set the pace of biological
growth is not this programme’s claim to defend; it is the premise of a leading account in quantitative
biology, and a contested one. West, Brown and Enquist derived the allometric scaling laws of biology
from the geometry of nutrient-distribution networks (West et al. 1997, 4 April 1997; one of the most
cited papers in its field, with an OpenAlex citation count of 5,046 as of 12 August 2026, a single-source
and index-dependent count). The exact exponent remains contested: White and Seymour report basal
metabolic rate proportional to body mass to the two-thirds power rather than three-quarters (White and
Seymour 2003; 725 citations, OpenAlex), and Kozłowski and Konarzewski have twice asked whether the
model is “mathematically correct and biologically relevant” (Kozłowski and Konarzewski 2004, 2005).
This programme’s premise needs only the mechanism, that the delivery network sets the ceiling, and not
any particular exponent; the dispute over the exponent’s value leaves it untouched, but the critics question
the mechanism too, so the programme takes it as a premise, and its biological reading stands or falls with
it.

The exception West’s own group found. The escape from the pipe limit is also not this programme’s
assertion; it is measured in the urban scaling literature by the same senior author. Bettencourt, Lobo,
Helbing, Kühnert, and West (2007) (2,704 citations, OpenAlex, 12 August 2026, a single-source count)
report, in the paper’s own words, read from its abstract (their 𝛽 is a city-scaling exponent, not this paper’s
coupling 𝛽 𝐿 ):
      “Quantities reflecting wealth creation and innovation have 𝛽 ≈ 1.2 > 1 (increasing returns),
      whereas those accounting for infrastructure display 𝛽 ≈ 0.8 < 1 (economies of scale).”
Infrastructure, the literal pipes, scales sublinearly. Information-mediated activity scales superlinearly.
They state the contrast with biology directly: “we discuss how cities are similar to, and differ from,
biological organisms, for which 𝛽 < 1”. They derive “growth equations, which quantify the dramatic
difference between growth fueled by innovation versus that driven by economies of scale” (spelling as
in the original). And their closing inference names the consequence: “This difference suggests that, as
population grows, major innovation cycles must be generated at a continually accelerating rate to sustain
growth and avoid stagnation or collapse.”
     On this paper’s reading, that is the shape of the open problem this programme addresses: in the
findings of West’s group, information-mediated growth escapes the throughput limit, and the only brake
in their framework is external and must be applied again and again, faster and faster. No internal limit is
derived. The replacement limit, a ceiling belonging to the growing system itself, is the gap.

The pipes argument at fixed throughput. Everything that grows is fed through a channel, and for
everything before software, fixing the channel fixes the growth: starve a tumour’s blood supply and
its growth is held back; fix a chain reaction’s fuel and geometry and it stops; the Eddington limit is a
luminosity ceiling where radiation pressure balances gravity, not a hard ceiling on the rate of accretion:
ultraluminous X-ray sources sit well above it, one accreting neutron star at about a hundred times it
(Bachetti et al. 2014). Cosmic inflation is excluded by scope (expansion of space, not growth of a structure
on a substrate); evolution is the sharpest case and fits (complexity rising for billions of years at roughly
fixed solar throughput, glacially, with no internal corrector, so on this framework evolution would carry a
measurable exponent far below the ceiling, a hypothesis about biology stated without a criterion or a
planned test, and outside the theory registration’s scope). Software is the first thing that keeps growing
when the pipes are held fixed, within the finite number of states and the maximum rate a fixed machine
allows (Margolus and Levitin 1998; Lloyd 2000), and the operational definition is load-bearing: an artefact
loop on a fixed substrate, measured on a substrate-fixed clock; a model merely thinking longer does not
qualify. The physicist’s objection is Landauer’s (1961), that computation is physically implemented; the
fixed-substrate clock is the answer, because the regime under study holds the substrate fixed and asks
what still grows.

The rival instrument. The nearest work to this programme’s question, and the right paper to weigh it
against, is Engels et al. (2025), arXiv:2504.18530, first posted 25 April 2025 at 17:54:27 UTC (arXiv’s
record; a NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight Poster, checked on 15 August 2026 against the NeurIPS virtual page for
poster 115536, as recorded in the statement paper’s reference dossier). It asks how oversight itself scales
and answers quantitatively: oversight success is modelled as a game between capability-mismatched
players whose oversight-specific Elo is a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence with two
plateaus, and optimal numbers of oversight levels are derived numerically and in some cases analytically
for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted models that then
become the trusted models at the next step.
    The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen,
measured in Elo. This programme’s variable is the composition class of the corrector, measured through

the corrector’s own scaling exponent. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight
improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the
overseen system’s (𝑚 𝐺 > 𝑚 𝐻 ), and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does. Their
framework has no term for what the overseer is made of. The programme’s separate prediction, outside
this paper’s framework, is that iterated same-class oversight, of which Nested Scalable Oversight is an
instance, stays capped however many rungs are added, while a cross-class corrector is not held to that cap.
The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class correctors, at or below one half, as a conjecture
on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III (the ARC Ceiling, a law under test), an exponent the
registration keeps distinct from Law II’s 𝛽𝐶 . That is where the two programmes could disagree about a
measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it.

Antecedents and near-misses, each with its differential. On the question. Hutter asked directly
whether intelligence can explode (Hutter 2012, arXiv, 28 February 2012), undertaking to separate speed
from intelligence explosion and to consider possible bounds on intelligence, augmenting the analysis of
Chalmers (2010). The question itself goes back to Good (1966). What that literature does not contain, so
far as has been surveyed, is a derived ceiling with an architecture dependence; growth exponents of the
form used here are older, as the item on the form below concedes, and power laws in inference compute
have been fitted directly (the paragraph on test-time compute above). A prior source that supplies a
derived ceiling with an architecture dependence defeats the corresponding priority here, and readers who
locate one are invited to report it.
    On the form. The relation 𝛼 = 1/(1 − 𝛽 𝐿 ) that Figure 1 and Section 5.2 take from the Foundational
paper is the exponent of the elementary solution of a separable growth equation. The same structure, a
stock that grows at a rate proportional to a power below one of itself, is the knowledge production function
of Jones (1995), whose long-run growth rate carries the factor 1/(1 − 𝜙).
    On impossibility. Three 2025 arXiv papers argue for limits on alignment or its control: Yao (2025b),
“The Alignment Trap: Complexity Barriers” (arXiv:2506.10304, version 1 of 12 June 2025 at 02:30:30
UTC, independently observed by the Internet Archive on 13 June 2025; cited by arXiv:2512.03048); Yao
(2025a), “On the Mathematical Impossibility of Safe Universal Approximators” (arXiv:2507.03031, 3 July
2025, the only paper in the arXiv abstract corpus containing the phrase “irreducible uncontrollability”,
abstract-search total of one, measured 12 August 2026); and Ball et al. (2025), “On the Impossibility of
Separating Intelligence from Judgment” (arXiv:2507.07341, 9 July 2025). All three are worst-case and
qualitative: measure zero, coNP-completeness, cryptographic hardness. None reports an average-case
scaling exponent or a rate. The third concludes that “safety cannot be achieved by designing filters external
to the LLM internals (architecture and weights)” and argues that “an aligned AI system’s intelligence
cannot be separated from its judgment”; that argument from filtering intractability is consistent with this
programme’s architecture dependence, though it does not test it. Two of the three are by one author. The
measured object of all three is verification of a fixed system, and it is closed within its own terms; this
programme’s measured object is a different one, persistence of alignment as capability compounds under
recursive amplification, and closure of the first does not settle the second.
    On the mechanism. That anti-correlated estimates average better than independent ones is textbook
variance reduction (antithetic variates). The mechanism is not the claim. The claim is that architecture
determines whether anti-correlation is available at all, and that this caps a safety-relevant exponent.
    The nearest structural analogue. The quantum error-correction threshold theorem (Aharonov and
Ben-Or 2008) also converts a qualitative worry into a critical value. It concerns physical error rates in a
fixed architecture, not a corrector’s scaling exponent, so it is a near-miss rather than an occupant; the
analogy is one of method. This analogue was identified by the programme’s own search rather than by a
referee, and is disclosed accordingly.

    On recursive creation. Smolin’s cosmological natural selection (Smolin 1992) is the antecedent for
selection-shaped universes, and the programme’s own December-era notes cite it contemporaneously
(“Echoing Smolin’s cosmological natural selection, AI could create recursive universes with their own
laws”, from the author’s notes).
    On feedback and stability (classical). The general proposition that inadequate corrective gain relative
to system gain causes instability has a long control-theory lineage (small-gain theorems; Zames 1966).
The differential: those are gain conditions on interconnected systems, not a power-law exponent criterion
on a corrector’s scaling with the capability of a recursively improving system. The general proposition is
never claimed; the exponent formulation, dynamics and estimator are the contribution surface.
    The nearest quantitative neighbour. Liu and Meng (2026), “Self-Correction as Feedback Control:
Error Dynamics, Stability Thresholds, and Prompt Interventions in LLMs” (arXiv:2604.22273) recasts
self-correction as a closed-loop control problem via a two-state Markov model and derives a directly
measurable stability threshold: iterate only when the error-correction rate over the error-introduction
rate exceeds Acc/(1 − Acc). The differential: theirs is a per-step rate threshold at a fixed capability
level, deciding whether another iteration helps now; this programme’s criterion is a scaling relation
across capability, deciding whether corrective strength keeps pace as the system improves. Theirs
has no scaling exponent on the corrector and no capability-growth exponent, the two quantities the
programme’s co-scaling criterion compares; the two models meet at zero growth, as Paper X shows.
Two adjacent empirical results from the same search: Li (2025), “Decomposing LLM Self-Correction:
The Accuracy-Correction Paradox and Error Depth Hypothesis” (arXiv:2601.00828, first posted on 24
December 2025; an accuracy-correction paradox, weaker models showing higher intrinsic correction rates)
and Tsui (2025), “Self-Correction Bench: Uncovering and Addressing the Self-Correction Blind Spot in
Large Language Models” (arXiv:2507.02778, first posted on 3 July 2025; a systematic self-correction
blind spot across open models).


3     The ARC Principle and its framework
3.1     The ARC Equation
The ARC Principle (Artificial Recursive Creation), stated in an earlier form, as the ARC Hypothesis,
in the author’s manuscripts of 8 December 2024 (Eastwood 2024) and articulated in Infinite Architects
(Eastwood 2026a), where its exponent is two, states, in the form this paper gives it:

                                                𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅𝛼                                              (1)

Capability scales with intelligence multiplied by recursive depth raised to a power, where:
      • 𝑈 = Effective capability (measurable via benchmark performance; the letter’s earlier senses in this
        programme are in Appendix D.2)
      • 𝐼 = Base intelligence (single-pass processing capacity without recursive reasoning)
      • 𝑅 = Recursive depth (number of self-referential processing iterations)
      • 𝛼 = Scaling exponent (empirically determined; hypothesised stability ceiling = 2, a scaling limit
        above which self-correction is not stably maintained)
   The principle’s core claim: recursion does not merely add to capability; it multiplies it according to a
power law. The letter 𝛼 has an earlier sense in this programme, recorded in Appendix D.2.

3.2    Recursion and its two forms
Definition 1 (Recursion). Recursion is self-reference: a process whose output becomes its input. It is
distinct from mere iteration (repeating the same operation) because each cycle operates on the transformed
results of previous cycles.

Definition 2 (Parallel recursion, weak). Multiple independent solutions generated simultaneously. No
information transfer between branches. Example: generating 𝑁 samples and selecting by majority vote.
Expected scaling: diminishing returns as redundancy increases.

Definition 3 (Sequential recursion, strong). Each processing step builds explicitly on previous steps.
Errors can be detected and corrected iteratively. Example: chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al.
2022) that folds self-reflection into each step. Expected scaling: compounding returns as depth enables
self-correction.

    The ARC Principle predicts that sequential recursion should produce higher 𝛼 values than parallel
recursion.

3.3    The quadratic stability ceiling hypothesis
We hypothesise that 𝛼 = 2 represents the stable limit, the most a recursively self-correcting system can
grow and stay correctable: a scaling limit, not a speed limit. The exactly quadratic speedup belongs to
the quantum search algorithm of Grover (1996); Bennett et al. (1997) then established the lower bound
that matches it, which is what makes that speedup optimal for unstructured search. The correspondence

5.2     What would refute the principle
The ARC Principle in this paper’s original, stronger form, that capability scales super-linearly with
recursive depth, 𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 , with the sequential/parallel distinction determining the regime, is refutable.

     1. No super-linear depth scaling. If capability scaled only linearly or sub-linearly with recursive
        depth 𝑅 across reasoning models (i.e. 𝛼 ≤ 1 where the principle predicts 𝛼 > 1 for sequential
        recursion), the central super-linearity claim fails.
     2. Exponent-composition mismatch. If the measured exponent systematically failed to match the
        predicted 𝛼 = 1/(1 − 𝛽 𝐿 ) from the self-referential coupling 𝛽 𝐿 (a quantity distinct from the
        correction exponent 𝛽𝐶 that Law II carries), the mechanistic identity, not merely the fitted curve, is
        falsified.
     3. Regime non-distinction. If sequential and parallel recursion produced the same scaling regime
        (no super-linear vs sub-linear split), the paper’s central distinction collapses.
     4. Better-fitting alternative. If a functional form outside the ARC family fit the test-time compute
        data materially better across models, ARC would not be the operative law.

5.4   An objection pre-empted
One objection pre-empted, because a physicist reaches it in one step. Fault-tolerant quantum error
correction suppresses logical error exponentially in code distance below threshold, which looks like a
counterexample to any square-root cap (the same-class limit 𝛾 ≤ 1/2 of Section 3.3). Two scope facts
answer it: the exponential suppression runs on the redundancy axis at fixed capability, not on the coupling
of corrective strength to the capability of the corrected system, which is the exponent this programme
measures; and the threshold theorem itself requires sufficiently uncorrelated physical noise, which is the
independence premise again, wearing hardware. Concentration bounds likewise give exponentially falling
error probability while estimator width falls as the square root: different quantities, and conflating them
manufactures a refutation.

8     Implications
8.1     For AI development
If the ARC Principle holds, recursive depth constitutes a third scaling axis alongside parameters and data.
Investment in recursive architectures may yield better returns than scaling model size alone.

8.2     For AI safety
If recursion amplifies not only capability but also embedded values, then well-aligned initial values should
strengthen through recursive self-improvement. Misaligned values would also compound, making early
alignment critical. The paper measures nothing about values and nothing about usefulness (legitimate
task completion, needless refusal, resource use, correction cost), so neither half of that conditional says
whether a system whose values compound stays useful. Neither half is a reason to relax oversight,
containment or shutdown, which stay in human hands for as long as they can be held; correction built in is
the contingency for control lost against our will.

9     Conclusion
We have formalised the ARC Principle and presented preliminary evidence:
    1. Parallel recursion yields 𝛼 ≈ 0.1 to 0.3 (sub-linear, diminishing returns)
    2. Sequential recursion yields 𝛼 ≈ 1.34 (super-linear on these two points alone, from two versions of
       one model; Paper II’s cross-architecture study displaces it and leaves the regime unresolved: its one
       interpretable fit, on Gemini 3 Flash, is one model’s fit and inconclusive, Section 4.4)
    3. The form of recursion may determine whether capability compounds, which the paper holds as a
       hypothesis (Section 4.5)
   In plain terms, the principle claims: “Thinking about thinking makes you smarter. Not linearly
smarter, but disproportionately smarter, if the thinking is sequential rather than parallel.”

The proof of Proposition 1, in the words of the note of 19 September 2026 that first printed the domain

Proof of Proposition 1. For 𝛾 < 1 the quantity 1−𝛾 is positive, so 1/(1−𝛾) ≤ 2 multiplies to 1 ≤ 2(1−𝛾),
that is 𝛾 ≤ 1/2, and the implication reverses by the same step: the equivalence holds in both directions.
At 𝛾 = 1 the quantity 1 − 𝛾 is zero, so 1/(1 − 𝛾) is undefined and the reciprocal inequality has no truth
value, while 𝛾 ≤ 1/2 reads 1 ≤ 1/2 and is defined and false. Exactly one side is undefined, and the
displayed equivalence is therefore not defined at that point. For 𝛾 > 1 the quantity 1 − 𝛾 is negative, so
1/(1 − 𝛾) < 0 < 2 holds for every such 𝛾 while 𝛾 ≤ 1/2 holds for none, and the equivalence fails. The
domain 𝛾 < 1 is exactly the range on which 𝛼crit = 1/(1 − 𝛾) is positive and finite and can serve as a
ceiling at all.                                                                                          □
