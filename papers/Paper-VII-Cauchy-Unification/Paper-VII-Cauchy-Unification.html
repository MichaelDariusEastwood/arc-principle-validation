Abstract

Why do the same scaling forms recur in systems that share no mechanism, from a mouse’s heartbeat to neural networks? A rule that predicts the form of a scaling law from how a system composes, before its data are seen, would let a scaling claim be tested rather than fitted, including the power-law form that Law I of the ARC Theory, a law under test, gives recursive capability. Under a stated regularity condition, the non-zero solutions of Cauchy’s four functional equations form exactly four families: linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic. Read as composition rules in a declared variable, they give a power law where depth and output both multiply and an exponential where depth adds and output multiplies. A saturating form follows from none of them: it needs added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own, since a ceiling alone selects no form. In a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule, the composition operator of each of 25 empirical domains was classified from known physics before the author fitted candidate models and selected among them by AICc. The predicted family was the one selected in 19 of 25 domains (marginal-conditioned permutation p=7×10−5p=7\times10^{-5}), on a six-model candidate set with no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate, and in 14 of the 18 multiplicative and additive domains (p=3.0×10−4p=3.0\times10^{-4}). Secondary tiers are reported separately and never blended: a baseline rerun gives 15 of 20; 13 published metabolic exponents sit nearest the dimensional comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1), seven of them still without a per-row source; 6 analytic identities check the fitter. The predictions were not timestamped in advance of their own fits and the operators were classified by the author, so the result is not proven; a replication with independent classification and a four-family test are preregistered by timestamp and await submission.

1 Introduction

The challenge is to preserve value-guided correction as AI becomes more capable and rewrites itself. Through the Eden Protocol, I propose recursive loops for ethical evaluation and self-correction, built into the system’s computational architecture before training. These loops would operate at each defined reasoning and action-selection step and participate in the processes that rewrite the system, including changes to the loops themselves. The ARC Theory asks whether correction can keep pace with capability and whether human-protective commitments persist through those changes, even if external control is lost. Human control should be retained for as long as possible.

This paper addresses one part of that programme: whether the way a process composes constrains the form of its scaling law. Predicting a family from a composition rule before fitting gives researchers a claim they can test, including the power-law form that Law I of the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation) gives recursive capability. Law I, the ARC Principle, is a law under test, stated formally in Paper I. The contribution here concerns mathematical form under stated assumptions and a structured comparison of predicted families with fitted data. It does not test the proposed correction architecture or establish that ethical commitments persist through self-modification.

This paper states and tests a domain-independent answer: under the conditions stated next, the form of a scaling law is determined by the composition operator of the underlying recursive process, and Cauchy’s functional equations constrain which forms are possible. Stated exactly (Section 3), under a regularity condition and in a declared variable, composition in which depth and output both multiply admits only the power-law family, and composition in which depth adds while output multiplies admits only the exponential. The equations fix the family, not its exponent. A saturating form follows from none of them: the bounded case rests on added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own (Proposition 1), and a ceiling alone selects no form. Two domains of the comparison show what the rule asks. In radioactive decay (domain 9 of Table 2), elapsed time adds while the surviving fraction multiplies, so the composition is additive and the predicted family is the exponential; the domain’s values are exact reference values, so it shows the rule at work rather than testing it (Section 11.1). The loss of neural language models against their number of parameters (domain 19; Kaplan et al. 2020) was classified as multiplicative, so the predicted family was the power law, and AICc model selection returned it.

In plain words, the result is this. In a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule, the family predicted from each domain’s composition operator was the one that AICc model selection returned in 19 of the 25 empirical domains, on a six-model candidate set that held no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate (Section 4.3). The operators were classified by the author, and the predictions for this cohort were not timestamped in advance of their own fits (Section 4.1); the comparison is therefore neither a proof nor a blind trial, and Sections 5 to 10 report it with its misses and with its evidence tiers kept apart. The novel contribution is not Cauchy’s mathematics itself, nor the idea that invariance or one principle fixes the possible forms of laws, which earlier work states (Section 2), but the use of Cauchy’s four equations together as one predictor of a domain’s scaling family from its composition rule, applied before the fit and across domains, and the test of that predictor.

2 Related work

The mathematics this paper uses is classical, and several of its ideas have specific precedents. The closest existing work falls into five groups: functional equations and the forms of laws; dimensional analysis and similarity; scale invariance; composition rules and generative processes; and the derivations of the dimensional comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1). Each work is stated below in its own terms, and the closing paragraphs state what this paper claims as its own.

Functional equations and the forms of laws.

Cauchy’s functional equations, and the forms of their solutions, go back to Cauchy (1821). Aczél (1966) is the standard reference on all four, with applications to geometry, mechanics, business mathematics, probability theory and information theory; it does not treat scaling laws across domains. That invariance can fix the possible forms of a law before any data are seen is the content of Luce (1959), the closest antecedent of this paper’s approach. For a continuous law relating a dependent variable to an independent one, each measured on a ratio, an interval or a logarithmic interval scale, Luce requires that an admissible transformation of the independent variable lead only to an admissible transformation of the dependent variable, and that it leave the form of the law unchanged; he lists the possible laws for each combination of scale types, among them the power function when both variables are ratio scales. Bingham and Ostaszewski (2015) state that the Cauchy functional equation is central to regular variation, and study the Goldie equation and inequality of Karamata regular variation, its counterpart in Beurling regular variation and the Gołąb-Schinzel equation; theirs is a mathematical study, with no application to scaling in physical, biological or social systems. Regular variation describes positive functions whose scale ratios f(λx)/f(x)f(\lambda x)/f(x) converge to a power of λ\lambda as xx grows, allowing a slowly varying factor at finite scale (Bingham et al. 1987). Hyers (1941) proves that the additive equation is stable under a defect bound that holds over the whole domain, the result on which Remark 3 draws.

Dimensional analysis and similarity.

Buckingham (1914) shows, from the principle of dimensional homogeneity, that every complete physical equation among nn quantities measured with kk fundamental units is reducible to a relation among the n−kn-k independent dimensionless products of powers of those quantities; where all the other arguments of that relation are held constant, the remaining product is constant whatever the unknown relation, so any one of the quantities in it varies with the others as a product of known powers. Barenblatt (1996) sets out dimensional analysis, physical similarity theory and a general theory of scaling phenomena, and treats scaling as intermediate asymptotics: self-similar behaviour that sets in once the influence of initial and boundary conditions has disappeared, while the system is still far from equilibrium. The book constructs self-similar solutions by dimensional analysis and treats self-similarities of the second kind separately. Both start from the dimensions of the quantities. This paper does not: it fixes the family by the rule by which a domain composes and leaves the exponent free (Section 12.2), and the dd of its comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1) is a dimension of transport geometry, not a dimension of units (Assumption 1).

Scale invariance, universality and renormalisation.

That scale invariance forces a power law, by solving p(bx)=g(b)p(x)p(bx)=g(b)\,p(x), is textbook (Newman 2005, sect. III.E). Newman shows that a probability distribution whose shape is unchanged when the unit of xx changes, so that p(bx)=g(b)p(x)p(bx)=g(b)\,p(x) for every bb, must be a power law: setting x=1x=1 turns the condition into p(bx)=p(b)p(x)/p(1)p(bx)=p(b)\,p(x)/p(1), the multiplicative equation of Theorem 1(ii) normalised at one, which he solves for a differentiable pp, and he links such scale-free behaviour to continuous phase transitions. Scaling, universality and renormalisation are linked in the review of Stanley (1999), which presents them as three of the many important themes that have served to provide the framework of much of the current understanding of critical phenomena. What this paper adds is the forward test: classifying a domain’s composition before its curve is seen, and scoring the family that follows.

Composition rules and generative processes.

That a composition rule, the structure of a generative process or the information a process keeps selects among exponential, lognormal and power-law forms also has precedents. Bíró (2008) proves that the infinite repetition of an arbitrary composition rule on an infinitesimal amount leads to a rule with a formal logarithm, so that the stationary distribution of energy in the thermodynamical limit is the Boltzmann-Gibbs exponential composed with that formal logarithm; interactions that depend only on the relative four-momentum squared then give kinetic energy distributions of the cut power-law (Tsallis-Pareto) form in the high-energy limit. Mitzenmacher (2004) surveys generative models for power-law and lognormal distributions: multiplicative processes, which follow Gibrat’s law of proportionate effect, generate lognormal distributions, and a small change, such as a minimum that acts as a lower reflective barrier, yields a power law instead. Frank (2009) presents an informational framework for the common patterns of nature, based on the method of maximum entropy, in which those patterns follow from simple constraints on information: any aggregation of processes that preserves information only about the mean attracts to the exponential pattern, and any that preserves information only about the geometric mean attracts to the power-law pattern; his examples include the age of disease onset, rates of amino acid substitution and the composition of ecological communities. The idea that one principle selects the common form of patterns across unrelated fields is therefore stated in Frank’s work, and the idea that invariance under changes of scale restricts the possible forms of a law is stated in Luce’s. The works of Newman, Bíró, Mitzenmacher and Frank concern probability distributions. Luce’s concerns laws between measured variables, with the form fixed by the scale types of the variables; this paper fixes the family of a law by the rule by which its domain composes, classified from the domain’s known physics before the fit.

Derivations of the dimensional comparator.

The expression d/(d+1)d/(d+1), or a special case of it, is reached in earlier work through different frameworks. West et al. (1997) derive allometric scaling relations, including the three-quarter power law for metabolic rates, from a model of how essential materials are transported through space-filling fractal networks of branching tubes, assuming that the energy dissipated is minimised and that the terminal tubes do not vary with body size; beyond mammalian circulation, their model predicts structural and functional properties of vertebrate cardiovascular and respiratory systems, plant vascular systems, insect tracheal tubes and other distribution networks. The empirical exponent is debated (the note on the three-quarter exponent in Section 8.1). Banavar et al. (1999) derive a general relationship between size and flow rates in networks with local connectivity, which accounts in a general way for the quarter-power allometric scaling of living organisms, with metabolic rate scaling as mass to the power D/(D+1)D/(D+1) in the most efficient networks filling DD dimensions; their theory also predicts scaling relations applicable to all efficient transportation networks, which they verify with observational data on river drainage basins. In two models of resource distribution networks, Banavar et al. Demetrius (2003, 2006) and Demetrius and Tuszynski (2010) reach allometric scaling through quantum statistics: the 2010 model, of coupled energy-transducing oscillator networks embedded in a dd-dimensional space, joins the chemiosmotic theory of energy transduction to the Debye model of the thermal properties of solids, and gives the exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1) in its quantum regime (1/21/2, 2/32/3 and 3/43/4 for d=1d=1, 22 and 33) and isometry in its classical regime, on physical assumptions different from those of vascular branching. The same framework gives socioeconomic outputs that grow superlinearly with population, with exponent 7/67/6 in that case. Zhao (2022) derives an n/(n+1)n/(n+1) power scaling law for an nn-dimensional system from producer-consumer dynamics, and places biomass production, eukaryote growth, mammalian brain size and the distribution of public facilities in cities in a new universality class whose dimensionality determines its growth scaling. He and Zhang (2004) state, of brain cells, that the fractal geometry of the cell “leads to a 4/5 allometric scaling law for human brain”; they give a physical explanation for the fourth dimension of life for three-dimensional organisms and the fifth dimension for the human brain. Maino et al. (2014), within dynamic energy budget theory, “derive an equation for the scaling of metabolic rate for adult heterotrophs without relying on optimisation arguments or implying cellular nutrient supply limitation”; their abstract states no exponent. Geoffrey West’s book Scale (West 2017) discusses scaling in organisms, cities and companies, and does not mention Cauchy’s functional equations.

What is and is not claimed here.

These derivations reach the expression d/(d+1)d/(d+1), in its general form or as a special case, through different frameworks: fractal branching networks, the geometry of efficient transportation networks, quantum metabolism, urban scaling (as D/(D+H)D/(D+H) with H=1H=1), producer-consumer dynamics, optimal vascular geometry and dynamic energy budget theory; of these, the He and Chen item could not be confirmed at a registry on 3 October 2026, and the abstract of Maino et al. (2014) states no exponent. They are not counted here as separate confirmations of the comparator. This paper’s hypothesis is that where such a derivation reaches d/(d+1)d/(d+1), it rests on three conditions: multiplicative composition, which Theorem 1 constrains to the power-law family; dd-dimensional space-filling transport geometry; and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow (Assumption 1). On this hypothesis, Cauchy’s equation accounts for the family and the shared transport conditions for the value. A common numerical fraction does not by itself identify a common mechanism, as the framework paper now states (On the Origin of Scaling Laws, version 3.14), so the hypothesis is tested derivation by derivation, by whether each one’s premises include the three conditions. The mathematical theorems are 200 years old. The biological data are 90 years old. What this paper claims as its own is neither the formula d/(d+1)d/(d+1), which is well established, nor Cauchy’s mathematics, but the hypothesis just stated and the predictor of the next paragraph, which Sections 4 to 10 put to a structured test.

Because the work conceded above already uses invariance, or one principle, to fix the possible forms of laws and patterns across fields, the novelty this paper claims is narrower. The search recorded in this paper’s public prior-art record (first committed on 27 June 2026 and last revised on 21 October 2026) found no publication that uses Cauchy’s four equations together as one predictor of a domain’s scaling family from its composition rule, applied before the fit and across domains, and tests that predictor as this paper does; nor did it find an earlier source that names Cauchy’s multiplicative equation in accounting for the common form of the derivations above. Readers are invited to submit prior work; a genuine antecedent that does this defeats the priority claim of this paper.

3 The theory

Cauchy’s four functional equations, additive, multiplicative, exponential and logarithmic, each have one family of non-zero solutions under a stated regularity condition (Theorem 1). We argue that this 200-year-old mathematical result has a physically testable consequence: under that regularity condition, it forces a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where depth adds while output multiplies. Where output is bounded it forces no form. A saturating response satisfies none of the four equations (Proposition 1): a saturation curve compatible with a bound rests on added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own, and the bound alone selects no form (Section 3.3). Once the variable in which depth composes is declared (Remark 2), the family is determined by the composition operator of the underlying recursive amplification process.

3.1 Cauchy’s four equations

Augustin-Louis Cauchy established in the early nineteenth century that the continuous solutions of four functional equations form four one-parameter families (Cauchy 1821). The solutions are families, not unique functions, and the same families follow under conditions weaker than continuity (Aczél 1966, ch. 2). Theorem 1 states the four equations with their domains and the regularity each needs.

Theorem 1 (Cauchy’s four equations under regularity). Let ff satisfy one of the following equations for all arguments in its domain, and suppose ff is continuous, or Lebesgue measurable, or bounded on some interval of positive length.

  1. f:ℝ→ℝf:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}, f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y): then f(x)=cxf(x)=cx.

  2. f:(0,∞)→(0,∞)f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty), f(xy)=f(x)f(y)f(xy)=f(x)f(y): then f(x)=xcf(x)=x^{c}.

  3. f:ℝ→(0,∞)f:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty), f(x+y)=f(x)f(y)f(x+y)=f(x)f(y): then f(x)=axf(x)=a^{x}, with a=ec>0a=e^{c}>0.

  4. f:(0,∞)→ℝf:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}, f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)f(xy)=f(x)+f(y): then f(x)=clog⁡xf(x)=c\log x.

Here cc is a real constant, the logarithm is natural, and each stated function satisfies its equation. If the targets in (ii) and (iii) are taken to be all real numbers, the only further solution is f≡0f\equiv0. Restricting the arguments of (i) and (iii) to (0,∞)(0,\infty) leaves the same families.

Proof in Appendix A.1.

These are not four independent results. They are four faces of one constraint: the requirement that a function be compatible with a binary operation. Under regularity (continuity, or Lebesgue measurability, or boundedness on an interval of positive length), a function that carries one of the two structures, addition on the real numbers or multiplication on the positive real numbers, into one of the two falls into exactly four families: linear (f(x)=cxf(x)=cx), power law (f(x)=xcf(x)=x^{c}), exponential (f(x)=axf(x)=a^{x}) and logarithmic (f(x)=clog⁡xf(x)=c\log x). This exhausts the homomorphisms. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure: saturating behaviour, curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers all lie outside the grid, and the grid says nothing about them. A critical power law can have the power-law form; Theorem 1 neither excludes it nor derives its critical exponent or physical mechanism. The additive equation yields the linear form f(x)=cxf(x)=cx; the multiplicative equation yields power laws; the exponential equation governs exponential growth and decay; and the logarithmic equation governs the map clog⁡xc\log x, which is multiplicative in input and additive in output; its increments diminish, but it is unbounded, so it is not a saturating family.

Remark 1. Without a regularity condition the additive equation has other solutions, which are not Lebesgue measurable and are unbounded on every interval (Aczél 1966); through the substitutions of Appendix A each of the other three equations has corresponding solutions. A rule observed only at some arguments, for example only under doubling, does not establish the equation (Eastwood 2026j).

Remark 2. The family label depends on the declared variable. With s=log⁡Rs=\log R, the multiplicative equation in RR becomes the exponential equation in ss, and Rc=ecsR^{c}=e^{cs}; a power law in RR is an exponential in log⁡R\log R. 3.2 The prediction

Consider any system where a quantity UU grows with recursive depth RR, measured in a declared variable (Remark 2). The system’s composition operator determines how consecutive applications combine, and Definition 1 states the two exact rules.

3.3 Bounded composition

Bounded composition (output constrained to [0,K][0,K]): a bound selects no form. A bound and convergence are different premises: a bounded response need not converge at all, and one that rises monotonically with depth converges to a limit no higher than KK, at a rate and along a path that the bound does not fix (Eastwood 2026d, sect. 5). The saturation curves of the candidate set of Section 4, the logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten and saturation-exponential forms, are such responses; they form a candidate set, not an exhaustive class. None of them satisfies any of the four equations in RR: each rests on the ceiling KK, an assumption added to the composition rule, and satisfies one of the equations only in a variable that the ceiling defines (Proposition 1). Which of them, if any, a bounded domain follows is fixed by dynamics of its own, not by the bound.

Proposition 1 (Saturating responses lie outside the four equations). Let ff be a non-constant solution of one of the four equations of Theorem 1, with the regularity it assumes. The framework paper reaches the same conclusion: “Saturation therefore requires its own dynamics and is not a fifth Cauchy cell” (Eastwood 2026d, sect. 5).

The prediction.

If the composition operator of a system is known, in a declared variable, and its response obeys the corresponding rule of Definition 1 for every pair of depths, the family of its scaling law follows before any data are seen: a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where it is additive (Theorem 1). Where output is bounded, the prediction is a saturation curve, which rests on an assumed ceiling and on dynamics that the composition rule does not supply (Proposition 1). Whether a domain’s response obeys such a rule is the premise that the classification of its composition operator supplies, and the comparison of Sections 6 to 10 puts that premise at risk against rival families on each domain’s data. The family is predicted with no curve fitting; its parameters, the exponent, the base and the ceiling, are not, and they are fitted. Where composition holds only approximately, the prediction carries the condition of Remark 3.

4 Prediction protocol

We present a structured prediction comparison across 50 domains in five evidence tiers (Section 5), among them six analytic identities that check the fitter. Each empirical domain followed the same sequence: known physics fixed the composition operator before any fitting began, the operator class then set which scaling family to expect, published or standard reference data were loaded, and that expectation was finally put to the test by model fitting with AICc-based model selection, run by the author after the classification rule was public (16 March 2026, 23:33 UTC).

For each domain:

  1. Classify the composition operator from known physics (before fitting). Determine whether the system’s recursive amplification process combines multiplicatively, additively, or with a bound.

  2. Predict the scaling family from the operator class: by Cauchy’s equations, a power law for a multiplicative operator and an exponential for an additive one (Theorem 1); for a bounded operator, a saturation curve, which rests on an assumed ceiling and on dynamics of its own rather than on Cauchy’s equations (Proposition 1).

  3. Load published or standard reference data.

  4. Fit candidate models (power law, exponential, logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten, saturation-exponential) to the data: the four saturation curves by nonlinear least squares on the response, and the power law and the exponential by linear least squares on the logarithm of the response. For the muscle force-velocity domain (domain 15), whose predicted model was a hyperbola, a seventh candidate, the hyperbolic decay a/(b+R)a/(b+R), counted in the bounded family, was also fitted.

  5. Select the best model by AICc (corrected Akaike information criterion) and compare the prediction against the selected model’s family.

4.2 Statistical method

The v2 test uses proper AICc (corrected Akaike information criterion) computed from residual sum of squares, sample size, and parameter count, not the R2R^{2}-based approximation used in the legacy v1 test. The v1 approximation, AIC≈nln⁡(1−R2)+2k,\begin{equation*} \text{AIC} \approx n \ln(1 - R^2) + 2k, \end{equation*}(1) was adequate as a heuristic but should not have been described as strict AIC in the standard statistical sense. The v2 rewrite corrects this.

Every candidate’s AICc is computed from the residual sum of squares of the response itself. The power law and the exponential are scored at the parameters of their fit on the logarithmic scale, which do not minimise that sum, so their AICc is never lower than at their own least-squares optimum on the response: the procedure can only disfavour them against the saturation curves. That is conservative for the matches among the 18 multiplicative and additive domains, may have contributed to the four multiplicative misses, and can favour the bounded family in the seven bounded domains; whether it changed any selection has not been checked.

The primary significance test is a marginal-conditioned permutation test: holding both the predicted and the observed family marginals fixed and permuting the pairing 400,000 times, the probability of 19 or more matches is p=7×10−5p = 7 \times 10^{-5}. The expected match count under this null is 9.5 of 25, not the 8.33 a uniform null gives, because power laws are simply common among the observed fits. A one-sided binomial test against a uniform three-family null gives p=1.56×10−5p = 1.56 \times 10^{-5} and is retained as a sensitivity check; it is the weaker null and is not used as the headline. Sigma equivalences are not quoted because they shift with the choice of null. The script that computes this test, which parses the published Table 2 directly so that it cannot drift from this paper, is held in a private repository and is not yet public; the test can be recomputed from that table’s Predicted and AICc best columns, which fix both marginals.

4.3 A model-set limitation this paper must state against itself

Section 3.1 names four Cauchy families. The candidate model set of this section contains six models (seven for domain 15, step 4): a power law and an exponential, which span two of those families, and four saturation curves, which lie outside the four equations (Proposition 1). Two of the four continuous Cauchy solutions have no candidate of their own: the logarithmic form clog⁡xc\log x and the pure linear form cxcx. The power-law candidate contains the linear form as its case of exponent one, and cxcx satisfies the multiplicative rule of Definition 1, so a linear response is fitted as a power law and counts as a match wherever its domain was classified as multiplicative. One consequence of the missing logarithmic candidate runs each way. A genuinely logarithmic domain could not have been classified correctly, because the fitter had no logarithmic candidate to select, so some of the six misses may have been guaranteed misses; and some of the nineteen hits may be logarithmic relations that a power law fits acceptably over a finite range. Every hit count in this paper therefore carries this qualifier, and none may be quoted without it. 8 Secondary tier: published exponents

8.1 Direct published exponents (13 of 13)

Thirteen taxa with well-established metabolic scaling exponents were compared with the dimensional comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1), the expression the derivations of Section 2 reach; it applies under the conditions of Assumption 1.

Assumption 1 (The conditions of the dimensional comparator). (a) Multiplicative composition (Theorem 1(ii) fixes the power-law family); (b) dd-dimensional space-filling transport geometry; (c) a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow (energy minimisation in West et al. 1997; supply-demand balance in Banavar et al. 2010; steady-state energy balance in Demetrius 2003, 2006; Demetrius and Tuszynski 2010). Under (a) to (c), and when total demand scales as LdL^{d} and the demand-weighted mean route length as LL, with LL a dimensionless linear size, the throughput exponent against flow-length cost is, asymptotically, d/(d+1)d/(d+1) (Eastwood 2026d). Reading it as metabolic rate against body mass needs a further bridge from transport to mass: in the models of Banavar et al. (2010), the mass exponent is 2/32/3 when flow velocity is independent of body size and reaches 3/43/4 when velocity scales as M1/12M^{1/12}.

Neither Cauchy alone, nor space-filling alone, is sufficient. Where throughput is the metabolic rate and body mass MM scales in proportion to flow-length cost, neither of which the geometry supplies (Eastwood 2026d), organisms with three-dimensional vascular transport and conserved flow should scale as M3/4M^{3/4}, those with two-dimensional transport as M2/3M^{2/3}, and those with one-dimensional filamentous transport as M1/2M^{1/2}.

Note on the 3/43/4 exponent.

The empirical value of the mammalian metabolic scaling exponent is debated, with estimates ranging from approximately 0.67 to 0.75 depending on taxon, mass range, temperature correction, and statistical method (White and Seymour 2003; Glazier 2005, 2022). The largest modern dataset (White and Seymour 2003, compiling 619 species) gives a maximum-likelihood estimate of 0.687 (95% CI 0.674 to 0.701), which excludes 0.750. The d/(d+1)d/(d+1) prediction of 0.750 for d=3d=3 matches the upper end of the empirical range. The variation itself is consistent with the framework: organisms with effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 would produce exponents between 2/32/3 and 3/43/4. The framework accommodates this variation rather than requiring a single universal exponent; since such dimensions can produce any exponent in that range, the variation is not evidence for it.

Caveat on d=2d=2 biological entries.

The jellyfish, cnidarian, and ctenophore entries (rows 33 to 35) are classified as nearest-comparator matches to d=2d=2, meaning their observed exponents fall closer to 2/32/3 than to either 1/21/2 or 3/43/4. In physics, the companion framework paper treats the percolation value as an analytic identity and holds the fragmentation values as unsourced (Eastwood 2026d). Neither field supplies a dimensional confirmation of d=2d=2, and biology does not either. These rows should be understood as empirical proximity to the d=2d=2 comparator, not as dimensional confirmation.

9 Significance framing

The primary result (p=7×10−5p=7\times10^{-5}, permutation) deserves honest contextualisation.

In absolute terms:

under the uniform-chance binomial null, roughly 1 in 64,000: if 64,000 researchers each picked scaling families at random for 25 domains, on average one would match 19 or more by chance. The stricter permutation null, which concedes that power laws are simply common among real fits, still leaves p=7×10−5p=7\times10^{-5}.

Relative to convention:

the permutation test lands nearly three orders of magnitude beyond p<0.05p<0.05, and the binomial test more than three. Sigma equivalences are not quoted because they shift with the choice of null.

What it supports:

the result is strong enough to publish, as a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule rather than a per-domain advance timestamp, as evidence that Cauchy-type composition constraints predict scaling families at well-above-chance rates.

What it does not support:

it is not strong enough to claim the theory is proven. The particle-physics discovery threshold is 5σ5\sigma (p≈3×10−7p\approx3\times10^{-7}). The test lacks formal preregistration. The operator classifications were made by the author, not an independent panel. The candidate set contained no logarithmic and no pure linear model, so the hit rate is conditional on that model set (Section 4.3).

What is needed:

independent replication with preregistered operator classification, ideally by researchers with no stake in the outcome.

10 Group analysis

10.1 Multiplicative composition: power laws

Of 14 empirical domains classified as multiplicative, 10 produce power-law scaling as their AICc-best model. These span metabolic allometry (heart rate vs body mass), urban economics (Bettencourt et al. 2007), technology learning curves (Wright’s law), computational linguistics (Heaps’ law), skill acquisition (Crossman 1959), Brownian motion (Catipovic et al. 2013), neural network scaling (Kaplan et al. 2020), VLSI design (Rent’s rule), population ecology (Taylor’s power law), and fluvial geomorphology (Hack’s law).

Four multiplicative domains miss: Kleiber’s law (bounded model edges out power law on a 13-point dataset), species-area (high scatter on small island data), stellar mass-luminosity (scatter at high mass), and Zipf’s law (finite-corpus truncation).

10.2 Additive composition: exponentials

All four domains with additive composition operators produce exponential scaling as their AICc-best model: Moore’s law (transistor counts), radioactive decay (P-32), the Gutenberg-Richter earthquake frequency law, and Horton’s law (stream numbers). This is 4 of 4; two of the four use reference or compiled values (radioactive decay and Moore’s law; Section 11.1).

10.3 Bounded composition: saturation curves

Of seven domains with bounded composition operators, five produce bounded (saturation) models as AICc-best: bacterial growth (logistic), haemoglobin binding (Hill), Ebola epidemic (Hill), Amdahl’s law (Michaelis-Menten, with values computed from its own formula; Section 11.1), and Facebook growth (logistic). Two miss: muscle force-velocity (exponential decay fits the truncated range better) and time crystal order (only 4 data points).

12 What this paper does and does not show

12.1 What it shows

As mathematics: under a stated regularity condition, the non-zero solutions of Cauchy’s four functional equations form exactly four families, linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic (Theorem 1). Read as composition rules in a declared variable, they give a power law where depth and output both multiply and an exponential where depth adds and output multiplies (Definition 1 and Remark 2), and a non-constant response that tends to a finite non-zero ceiling satisfies none of them in that variable (Proposition 1).

As evidence: a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule. The composition operator of each of 25 empirical domains was classified from known physics before the fitting procedure ran, and the predicted family was the family that AICc model selection returned in 19 of the 25 (marginal-conditioned permutation p=7×10−5p=7\times10^{-5}), on the six-model candidate set of Section 4.3, and in 14 of the 18 multiplicative and additive domains (permutation p=3.0×10−4p=3.0\times10^{-4}; Section 6). The secondary tiers are reported separately and never blended (Sections 5 and 8).

12.2 What it does not show

  • It is not proven. The comparison’s predictions were not timestamped in advance of their own fits, its operators were classified by the author, without blinding to the forms established for most of its domains, and its candidate set had no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate (Sections 4.1 and 4.3). A replication with independent classification and a four-family test are preregistered by timestamp (12 August 2026, Bitcoin block 962066) and await human submission.

  • No registered verdict on P18 or P9. This paper informs Law I through two propositions of the theory registration, P18, that the composition operator predicts the family, and P9, the cross-domain form (Eastwood 2026m). Its comparisons are dated results under the rule of March 2026, and the registration scores none of them against any prediction.

  • No universal classification of nature. Theorem 1 classifies the solutions of four functional equations. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure (Section 3.1), and finitely many noisy observations cannot establish that a domain’s composition rule holds exactly, or within an error bound over the whole domain (Remark 3).

  • No exponent selected by Theorem 1. The theorem fixes a family and leaves its constant cc free. The comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1) of Section 8.1 rests on the transport conditions of Assumption 1, not on the theorem.

  • The bounded class rests on added assumptions. No saturating form follows from the four equations (Proposition 1): the bounded class rests on a ceiling on output and on dynamics of its own, and a ceiling alone selects no form (Section 3.3).

13 Limitations

  • No registry submission for this test, and no advance per-domain timestamp for this cohort. The predictions, data arrays, and fitting logic all reside in a single author-written script. The timing of the classification rule, the per-domain assignments and the fits is set out in Section 4.1.

  • Operator classification by author. The composition operator classification is done by the author, not by an independent assessor. A replication with independent operator classification would be more rigorous.

  • The classification was not blind to the established forms. Most of the 25 domains are named laws whose functional forms are established in their fields (Kleiber’s, Zipf’s, Heaps’, Moore’s, Wright’s, Rent’s, Taylor’s, Horton’s and Hack’s laws among them), and their operators were classified by the author with those forms already published. A classification made before the fit is therefore not blind to the expected form, and the comparison cannot separate what a domain’s composition operator predicts from what is already known of its form. The replication with independent classification (Section 4.1) separates them only if its classifiers do not know each domain’s established form, or if its domains have none.

  • The bounded class rests on added assumptions, and a bound selects no form. No saturating form follows from the four equations: each bounded candidate rests on a ceiling on output, an added assumption, and satisfies one of the equations only in a variable that the ceiling defines (Proposition 1); which curve a bounded domain follows is fixed by dynamics of its own (Section 3.3). The bounded category groups the logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten and saturation-exponential forms (with the hyperbolic decay for domain 15, Section 4) by empirical convention rather than mathematical proof.

  • Legacy AIC was an approximation. The v1 test used an R2R^{2}-based AIC approximation, equation (1), not a full likelihood-based nonlinear AIC. The v2 test corrects this with proper AICc computed from residual sum of squares.

  • Two candidates are fitted on another scale. The power law and the exponential are fitted on the logarithm of the response and scored by AICc on the response itself, while the saturation curves are fitted and scored on the response (Section 4.2). A re-run that fits every candidate on the scale AICc scores is needed to show whether any selection depends on it.

  • Three datasets use reference values. Radioactive decay (a reference half-life whose publisher could not be confirmed), Amdahl’s law (its theoretical formula) and Moore’s law (compiled manufacturer specifications) use theoretical or reference values rather than independently collected empirical measurements; the first two are analytic identities counted among the 19 matches, and the data source of the time-crystal domain could not be found (Section 11.1).

  • Family prediction only. The framework predicts the functional family but not the specific exponent. Predicting exponents requires additional structure (dimensionality, constraint type) developed in the companion Foundational paper (Eastwood 2026b).

  • Six empirical misses. The misses are not dismissible. They identify real limits of the framework: small datasets, truncated ranges, high-scatter regimes, and domains where competing physical effects (radiation pressure, corpus boundaries) perturb the ideal prediction.

  • Operator classification is descriptive, not explanatory. The framework does not explain why a particular domain has a multiplicative rather than additive composition operator. It takes the operator as given and predicts the consequence.

  • Baseline-20 rerun is not cleanly comparable to v1. The fitter changed from R2R^{2}-based AIC approximation to proper AICc. The model set expanded. A direct comparison of 14/20 (v1) to 15/20 (v2 baseline rerun) is not meaningful because the selection criterion changed.

  • Publication bias risk. The 5 new expansion domains were selected after the baseline-20 test. While they were chosen to span new physical regimes (stellar astrophysics, allometric heart rate, VLSI, ecology, geomorphology), the selection was not randomised.

  • Finite model set, and specifically two Cauchy families out, one non-Cauchy family in. The fitter compares six candidate models (power law, exponential, logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten, saturation-exponential). Two of the four Cauchy solutions named in Section 3.1 have no candidate (logarithmic clog⁡xc\log x; pure linear cxcx), while the bounded family, which rests on added assumptions (Section 3.3), is represented four times. The true best model for any domain may not be in this set, and Section 4.3 states the consequences for the hit counts.

  • Sample size. Twenty-five empirical domains is a reasonable first sample but not definitive. Expansion to 100+ domains with preregistered selection criteria would strengthen or weaken the result.

13.1 Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim

The limitations above describe where the framework is weak; this subsection states the specific outcomes that would falsify it. The central claim, that a domain’s composition operator, classified from known physics before fitting, predicts its scaling-law family, is defeated by any of the following:

  1. Hit rate at chance. The primary result is a family-prediction hit rate of 19/25 empirical domains (Sections 6 and 7), on the six-model candidate set of Section 4.3. With three to four candidate families, assignment at random gives a chance baseline of roughly one in three to one in four. The claim is defeated if a replication whose domain list and per-domain predictions are timestamped in advance, and whose operator classes are assigned by an external human classifier, returns a hit rate not significantly above that chance baseline. The advance-timestamped run on the record is the 12-domain extension of 17 March 2026, its predictions committed to the public repository at 00:19:19 UTC in commit 8edaec0, attestation class public repository commit date, its fits run twenty-three minutes later: 10 of 12, and 9 of 11 on the strictly fresh rows. Its predictions were thus fixed and timestamped in advance of its fits; it is described neither as registered nor as preregistered, and it is not an accepted registry submission; its twelve classifications are the author’s own, so this criterion has not yet been put at risk in the form stated here.

  2. Post-hoc operator classification. The test’s strength depends on the operator being assignable from independently known physics without seeing the data. The claim is defeated if, in a blind trial, independent assessors cannot reproduce the author’s operator classifications from the physics alone, or if any classification can be shown to have been chosen to fit the observed exponent.

  3. Fit degeneracy. The prediction is meaningful only if the candidate families are empirically distinguishable on the data at hand. It is defeated for any domain where the fitter reaches the observed curve equally well under the wrong family; that is, the family assignment carries no discriminating information for that domain’s range and noise.

  4. Unrationalised misses. Six domains miss (Section 7). The misses are consistent with the claim only if their causes are fixed as a preregistered exclusion rule before the next test, not supplied after seeing which domains missed. The claim is defeated if the miss rate rises under a preregistered replication with no post-hoc exclusions.

  5. Four-family re-run. The four-family confirmatory study, preregistered by timestamp (Section 4.3), re-runs this comparison with logarithmic and pure linear candidates included. The claim is weakened if the hit rate falls materially when the missing families are available, and defeated if it falls to the chance baseline; hits that migrate to the logarithmic family will be reported as reclassifications, not as new confirmations.

  6. Exploratory ceiling. Until the operator classes and the per-domain predictions for a randomised domain sample are fixed before any fitting by an external human classifier and timestamped in advance with the attestation class stated, the claim cannot rise above exploratory, and any presentation of it as established would itself falsify the paper’s stated evidential status. A registry deposit raises the attestation class; it is not what lifts the ceiling. Part of that condition is already met on the record: the 12-domain extension of 17 March 2026 (item 1) had its per-domain predictions committed before its fits; its classifier was the author, its candidate set was the pre-correction one, and the author’s demotion of 00:56 that night stands so far as it rests on the packet’s own written condition, deposit to a registry before data extraction, which was not met, and is reversed as of 7 September 2026 only so far as it rested on the absence of a registry form.

What would not defeat it: a single additional miss, or a better-fitting model appearing in one domain. The claim is about the aggregate operator-to-family mapping, not any single fit; but that aggregate must clear the chance baseline under preregistered, independently classified replication.

14 Implications

If the framework holds under independent preregistered replication, it would suggest:

  1. The forms of scaling laws are not coincidences but mathematical consequences of composition structure: a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where it is additive, in the declared variable and under the regularity of Theorem 1, while a saturating form needs in addition a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own (Proposition 1).

  2. The same composition rule that fixes the power-law family of a mouse’s heart rate would fix the family, though not the exponent, of how neural networks improve with scale.

  3. Critical phenomena would be one case of the wider rule: scale invariance gives their power-law form through the multiplicative equation, while their exponents are left to the renormalisation group. Section 2 sets out what earlier work already states on scale invariance, universality and renormalisation (Newman 2005; Stanley 1999), and what this paper adds.

  4. Any new system’s scaling family can be predicted from first principles by classifying its composition operator, before any data fitting. The prediction follows only where the composition rule holds exactly, or within an error bound over the whole domain, in a declared variable (Remark 3); finitely many noisy observations cannot establish such a bound, so each such prediction remains a hypothesis for the system’s data to test.

  5. Domain-specific explanations of the forms of scaling laws (fractal networks, learning curves) may share a single constraint on form, that of the functional equations Cauchy (1821) solved, while the values of their exponents, and behaviour outside the four families, need physics of their own (Section 3.1).

These implications are conditional. The current evidence is not definitive.

15 Conclusion

Plant an acorn and given centuries, you get an oak. But plant that oak’s acorn, and its acorn, recursively across millennia, and you get a forest that shapes the climate of continents. What we plant in these systems will compound across scales we cannot imagine. The seed determines the forest.

Read as composition rules, the functional equations Cauchy solved 200 years ago (Cauchy 1821) say that the seed also determines the form of the forest. Multiplicative seeds produce power laws. Additive seeds produce exponentials. Both hold under the regularity of Theorem 1, in the variable in which depth composes. Bounded seeds can produce saturation, but not from Cauchy’s equations: the ceiling is an added assumption (Proposition 1), and the form of the saturation needs dynamics of its own. Twenty-five empirical domains tested against this prediction yield 19 matches at p=7×10−5p=7\times10^{-5} under the marginal-conditioned permutation test, on the six-model candidate set of Section 4.3; the four-family re-run is preregistered by timestamp and awaits human submission. Thirteen published metabolic exponents sit nearest the comparator the dimensional theory predicts.

The mathematics was always there, and earlier work had read invariance as fixing the possible forms of laws (Section 2); what this paper adds is a forward test, across domains, of the form each domain’s composition rule predicts. Whether that reading holds under independent scrutiny is now a question for the scientific community to answer.

Frame key.

This paper carries a result: under a stated regularity condition and in a declared variable, Cauchy’s functional equations force a power law under multiplicative composition and an exponential under additive composition (Theorem 1), turning a two-hundred-year-old identity into a physically testable prediction about the shapes empirical scaling curves may take. For bounded composition no form follows from the equations: a saturation curve rests on a ceiling on output, which none of the four equations supplies, and on dynamics of its own (Proposition 1). Inside the ARC Theory it gives the classification behind the form of Law I, the ARC Principle, and is one of the ARC/Eden experiments, the mathematical spine that ties composition constraints to observed functional forms.
