Abstract

Why do the same scaling forms recur in systems that share no mechanism, from animal heat production to language-model loss? A rule that predicts the form of a scaling law from how a system composes, before its data are seen, would let a scaling claim be tested rather than fitted, including the power-law form that Law I of the ARC Theory, a law under test, gives recursive capability. Under a stated regularity condition, the non-zero solutions of Cauchy’s four functional equations form exactly four families: linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic. Read as composition rules in a declared variable, they give a power law where depth and output both multiply and an exponential where depth adds and output multiplies. A saturating form follows from none of them: it needs added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own, since a ceiling alone selects no form. Predicting a family from a composition rule before fitting gives researchers a claim they can test, including the power-law form that Law I of the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation) gives recursive capability. Law I, the ARC Principle, is a law under test, stated formally in Paper I. The contribution here concerns mathematical form under stated assumptions and a structured comparison of predicted families with fitted data. It does not test the proposed correction architecture or establish that ethical commitments persist through self-modification.

This paper states and tests a domain-independent answer: under the conditions stated next, the form of a scaling law is determined by the composition operator of the underlying recursive process, and Cauchy’s functional equations constrain which forms are possible. Stated exactly (Section 3), under a regularity condition and in a declared variable, composition in which depth and output both multiply admits only the power-law family, and composition in which depth adds while output multiplies admits only the exponential. The equations fix the family, not its exponent. A saturating form follows from none of them: the bounded case rests on added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own (Proposition 1), and a ceiling alone selects no form. Two domains of the comparison show what the rule asks. In radioactive decay (domain 9 of Table 2), elapsed time adds while the surviving fraction multiplies, so the composition is additive and the predicted family is the exponential; its tabulated reference activities illustrate the rule. Six entries differ from its stated formula at the printed precision (Section 11.1). In the Dudhnai watershed, the archived comparison selected the predicted exponential family for stream counts by order (domain 18). All six counts are traceable to Table 2 of Singh et al. (2021).

In plain words, the result is this. In a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule, the family predicted from each domain’s composition operator was the one that AICc model selection returned in 17 of the 23 rows retained in version 3.19, on an archived candidate set that held no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate (Section 4.3). One retained match follows the published Brownian reference line to the last displayed decimal. A locator for measured points is still needed (Section 11.1). The operators were classified by the author, and the predictions for this cohort were not timestamped in advance of their own fits (Section 4.1); the comparison is therefore neither a proof nor a blind trial, and Sections 5 to 10 report it with its misses and with its evidence tiers kept apart. The novel contribution is not Cauchy’s mathematics itself, nor the idea that invariance or one principle fixes the possible forms of laws, which earlier work states (Section 2), but the use of Cauchy’s four equations together as one predictor of a domain’s scaling family from its composition rule, applied before the fit and across domains, and the test of that predictor.

2 Related work

The mathematics this paper uses is classical, and several of its ideas have specific precedents. The closest existing work falls into five groups: functional equations and the forms of laws; dimensional analysis and similarity; scale invariance; composition rules and generative processes; and the derivations of the dimensional comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1). Each work is stated below in its own terms, and the closing paragraphs state what this paper claims as its own.

Functional equations and the forms of laws.

Cauchy’s functional equations, and the forms of their solutions, go back to Cauchy (1821). Aczél (1966) is the standard reference on all four, with applications to geometry, mechanics, business mathematics, probability theory and information theory; it does not treat scaling laws across domains. That invariance can fix the possible forms of a law before any data are seen is the content of Luce (1959), the closest antecedent of this paper’s approach. For a continuous law relating a dependent variable to an independent one, each measured on a ratio, an interval or a logarithmic interval scale, Luce requires that an admissible transformation of the independent variable lead only to an admissible transformation of the dependent variable, and that it leave the form of the law unchanged; he lists the possible laws for each combination of scale types, among them the power function when both variables are ratio scales. In his later account, Luce (1996, sect. 5.1, equations 26 and 27) distinguishes changing measurement units from changing the physical stimuli. His power relation for matched stimuli rests on an observable compatibility condition. Here the corresponding empirical question is whether two operations on the system compose as specified. Expressing the same observation in a different unit cannot answer it.

Bingham and Ostaszewski (2015) study the roles of the Cauchy and Goldie equations in Karamata and Beurling regular variation and the related Gołąb-Schinzel equation. Regular variation describes positive functions whose scale ratios f(λx)/f(x)f(\lambda x)/f(x) converge to a power of λ\lambda as xx grows, allowing a slowly varying factor at finite scale (Bingham et al. 1987). Hyers (1941) proves that the additive equation is stable under a defect bound that holds over the whole domain, the result on which Remark 4 draws.

Dimensional analysis and similarity.

Buckingham (1914, 348 to 351) reduces a dimensionally homogeneous physical relation to independent dimensionless products. With nn positive variables and a dimensional exponent matrix of rank kk, there are n−kn-k independent products. Two speeds, for example, both have dimension length divided by time. Their columns coincide, so k=1k=1 and their ratio is one dimensionless product. The reduced relation is implicit. If the ratio zz satisfies (z−1)(z−2)=0(z-1)(z-2)=0, both z=1z=1 and z=2z=2 are admissible. A unique value at fixed values of the other products requires a single-valued physical response or a specified branch.

Barenblatt (1996, Introduction, equations 0.6 and 0.7) reduces sphere drag to a dimensionless function of Mach number. Its values must still be determined experimentally or by calculation. His account of intermediate asymptotics distinguishes self-similar solutions constructed by dimensional analysis from self-similarities of the second kind. Both start from the dimensions of the quantities. This paper does not: it fixes the family by the rule by which a domain composes and leaves the exponent free (Section 12.2), and the dd of its comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1) is a dimension of transport geometry, not a dimension of units (Assumption 1).

Scale invariance, universality and renormalisation.

For a differentiable positive scaling profile, the identity p(bx)=g(b)p(x)p(bx)=g(b)p(x) yields a power law (Newman 2005, sect. III.E, equations 29 to 34). Setting x=1x=1 gives the multiplicative equation for p/p(1)p/p(1) after the reference scale is set to one. For a probability density, the range of the power law also matters. A non-zero density proportional to x−ax^{-a} cannot integrate to one on the whole positive half-line. On an upper tail x≥xmin>0x\geq x_{\min}>0 its integral is finite for a>1a>1 (Newman 2005, sect. III.A, equations 8 and 9). A response measured against depth has no probability normalisation. Scaling, universality and renormalisation are linked in the review of Stanley (1999), which presents them as three of the many important themes that have served to provide the framework of much of the current understanding of critical phenomena. What this paper adds is the forward test: classifying a domain’s composition before its curve is seen, and scoring the family that follows.

Composition rules and generative processes.

That a composition rule, the structure of a generative process or the information a process keeps selects among exponential, lognormal and power-law forms also has precedents. For the kinetic-energy composition of Bíró (2008, equations 5 to 12), repeated infinitesimal increments can produce an associative limiting rule. The argument assumes a finite limit, h(x,0)=xh(x,0)=x and a right derivative in the increment that yields an invertible formal logarithm. The stationary energy distribution is exponential in that transformed variable. In the relativistic model examined there, the high-energy limit for interactions depending on squared relative four-momentum gives a cut power-law distribution. Mitzenmacher (2004, sect. 5) contrasts a model of proportionate random growth, whose logarithm is a sum of random increments, with a model that places a reflecting lower boundary on income. These models give a lognormal approximation and a power-law tail under their respective assumptions. Frank (2009) uses maximum entropy to connect probability distributions to the information preserved about a system. His exponential example fixes an arithmetic mean on a nonnegative domain. His power-law example constrains a mean logarithm and specifies a lower bound. Applications include disease onset, amino acid substitutions and ecological communities. The idea that one principle selects the common form of patterns across unrelated fields is therefore stated in Frank’s work, and the idea that invariance under changes of scale restricts the possible forms of a law is stated in Luce’s. The works of Newman, Bíró, Mitzenmacher and Frank concern probability distributions. Luce’s concerns laws between measured variables, with the form fixed by the scale types of the variables; this paper fixes the family of a law by the rule by which its domain composes, classified from the domain’s known physics before the fit.

Derivations of the dimensional comparator.

The expression d/(d+1)d/(d+1), or a special case of it, is reached in earlier work through different frameworks. West et al. (1997) derive allometric scaling relations, including the three-quarter power law for metabolic rates, from a model of how essential materials are transported through space-filling fractal networks of branching tubes, assuming that the energy dissipated is minimised and that the terminal tubes do not vary with body size; beyond mammalian circulation, their model predicts structural and functional properties of vertebrate cardiovascular and respiratory systems, plant vascular systems, insect tracheal tubes and other distribution networks. The empirical exponent is debated (the note on the three-quarter exponent in Section 8.1). Banavar et al. (1999) derive a general relationship between size and flow rates in networks with local connectivity, which accounts in a general way for the quarter-power allometric scaling of living organisms, with metabolic rate scaling as mass to the power D/(D+1)D/(D+1) in the most efficient networks filling DD dimensions; their theory also predicts scaling relations applicable to all efficient transportation networks, which they verify with observational data on river drainage basins. In two models of resource distribution networks, Banavar et al. (2010) show that the exponent of metabolic rate against body mass is 2/32/3 if the velocity of flow stays constant and reaches its maximum, 3/43/4, if velocity scales with its maximum exponent, 1/121/12, and that quarter-power scaling can arise with no underlying fractality; the geometry of the network alone therefore does not select one exponent. The abstract of Demetrius (2003) links metabolic scaling to energy transduction and the availability of resources. In the abstract of his later paper, Demetrius (2006) combines chemiosmotic energy transduction with quantum statistics. The oscillator model of Demetrius and Tuszynski (2010, sect. 3, equation 3.14) gives d/(d+1)d/(d+1) in its quantum regime and isometry in its classical regime. This route uses the energetics of coupled oscillators. Its dimensional dependence differs in origin from vascular branching.

In the city model of Bettencourt (2013, Table 1), land area scales with population to the power D/(D+H)D/(D+H), where DD is the spatial dimension and HH is the fractal dimension of travel paths. At D=2D=2 and H=1H=1 the predicted exponents are 2/32/3 for land area, 5/65/6 for network volume and 7/67/6 for socioeconomic rates. The table gives observed land-area exponents from 0.56 to 1.04 across measurements. The numerical comparison with metabolic scaling concerns the land-area formula at H=1H=1. Zhao (2022) derives an n/(n+1)n/(n+1) power scaling law for an nn-dimensional system from producer-consumer dynamics, and places biomass production, eukaryote growth, mammalian brain size and the distribution of public facilities in cities in a new universality class whose dimensionality determines its growth scaling. Earlier versions of this paper also listed a derivation of metabolic scaling exponents by He and Chen (2003) from optimal vascular network geometry with flow conservation constraints; the one He and Chen record of 2003 at Crossref (He and Chen 2003) is Effects of size and pH on metabolic rate, in the International Journal of Nonlinear Sciences and Numerical Simulation; it carries no abstract, and on 3 October 2026 it could not be confirmed that this record is that work, so nothing here rests on it. He and Zhang (2004) state, of brain cells, that the fractal geometry of the cell “leads to a 4/5 allometric scaling law for human brain”; they give a physical explanation for the fourth dimension of life for three-dimensional organisms and the fifth dimension for the human brain. Maino et al. (2014) model how organisms store and mobilise nutrients. Their Table 1 shows that a dynamic energy budget model and a transport model can give the same metabolic scaling equation with different interpretations of its coefficients. In the large-mass limit it recovers the three-quarter power. Geoffrey West’s book Scale (West 2017) discusses scaling in organisms, cities and companies, and does not mention Cauchy’s functional equations.

What is and is not claimed here.

The efficient transport, oscillator and producer-consumer models above yield comparable exponents under different physical assumptions. The urban land-area expression reduces to D/(D+1)D/(D+1) for H=1H=1. The dynamic energy budget example gives a particular limiting exponent. Extending it to arbitrary dd would require a separate derivation. The 2003 He and Chen attribution remains unverified. This paper’s hypothesis is that where such a derivation reaches d/(d+1)d/(d+1), it rests on three conditions: multiplicative composition, which Theorem 1 constrains to the power-law family; dd-dimensional space-filling transport geometry; and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow (Assumption 1). On this hypothesis, Cauchy’s equation accounts for the family and the shared transport conditions for the value. A common numerical fraction does not by itself identify a common mechanism, as the framework paper now states (On the Origin of Scaling Laws, version 3.16), so this hypothesis has to be checked against the premises of each derivation. The oscillator model obtains the exponent from its energy spectrum and cycle times. A claim that it requires the transport assumptions still needs a derivation. The mathematical theorems are 200 years old. The biological data are 90 years old. What this paper claims as its own is neither the formula d/(d+1)d/(d+1), which is well established, nor Cauchy’s mathematics, but the hypothesis just stated and the predictor of the next paragraph, which Sections 4 to 10 put to a structured test.

Because the work conceded above already uses invariance, or one principle, to fix the possible forms of laws and patterns across fields, the novelty this paper claims is narrower. The search recorded in this paper’s public prior-art record (first committed on 27 June 2026 and last revised on 21 October 2026) found no publication that uses Cauchy’s four equations together as one predictor of a domain’s scaling family from its composition rule, applied before the fit and across domains, and tests that predictor as this paper does; nor did it find an earlier source that names Cauchy’s multiplicative equation in accounting for the common form of the derivations above. Readers are invited to submit prior work; a genuine antecedent that does this defeats the priority claim of this paper.

3 The theory

Cauchy’s four functional equations, additive, multiplicative, exponential and logarithmic, each have one family of non-zero solutions under a stated regularity condition (Theorem 1). We argue that this 200-year-old mathematical result has a physically testable consequence: under that regularity condition, it forces a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where depth adds while output multiplies. Where output is bounded it forces no form. A saturating response satisfies none of the four equations (Proposition 1): a saturation curve compatible with a bound rests on added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own, and the bound alone selects no form (Section 3.3). The family follows once the variable, its domain and the composition rule obeyed by the response are specified (Remarks 2 and 3).

3.1 Cauchy’s four equations

Augustin-Louis Cauchy established in the early nineteenth century that the continuous solutions of four functional equations form four one-parameter families (Cauchy 1821). The solutions are families, not unique functions, and the same families follow under conditions weaker than continuity (Aczél 1966, ch. 2). Theorem 1 states the four equations with their domains and the regularity each needs.

Theorem 1 (Cauchy’s four equations under regularity). Let ff satisfy one of the following equations for all arguments in its domain, and suppose ff is continuous, or Lebesgue measurable, or bounded on some interval of positive length.

  1. f:ℝ→ℝf:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}, f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y): then f(x)=cxf(x)=cx.

  2. f:(0,∞)→(0,∞)f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty), f(xy)=f(x)f(y)f(xy)=f(x)f(y): then f(x)=xcf(x)=x^{c}.

  3. f:ℝ→(0,∞)f:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty), f(x+y)=f(x)f(y)f(x+y)=f(x)f(y): then f(x)=axf(x)=a^{x}, with a=ec>0a=e^{c}>0.

  4. f:(0,∞)→ℝf:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}, f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)f(xy)=f(x)+f(y): then f(x)=clog⁡xf(x)=c\log x.

Here cc is a real constant, the logarithm is natural, and each stated function satisfies its equation. If the targets in (ii) and (iii) are taken to be all real numbers, the only further solution is f≡0f\equiv0. Restricting the arguments of (i) and (iii) to (0,∞)(0,\infty) leaves the same families.

Proof in Appendix A.1.

These are not four independent results. They are four faces of one constraint: the requirement that a function be compatible with a binary operation. Under regularity (continuity, or Lebesgue measurability, or boundedness on an interval of positive length), a function that carries one of the two structures, addition on the real numbers or multiplication on the positive real numbers, into one of the two falls into exactly four families: linear (f(x)=cxf(x)=cx), power law (f(x)=xcf(x)=x^{c}), exponential (f(x)=axf(x)=a^{x}) and logarithmic (f(x)=clog⁡xf(x)=c\log x). This exhausts the homomorphisms. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure: saturating behaviour, curvature on log axes and multi-regime crossovers all lie outside the grid, and the grid says nothing about them. A critical power law can have the power-law form; Theorem 1 neither excludes it nor derives its critical exponent or physical mechanism. The additive equation yields the linear form f(x)=cxf(x)=cx; the multiplicative equation yields power laws; the exponential equation governs exponential growth and decay; and the logarithmic equation governs the map clog⁡xc\log x, which is multiplicative in input and additive in output; its increments diminish, but it is unbounded, so it is not a saturating family.

Remark 1. Without a regularity condition the additive equation has other solutions, which are not Lebesgue measurable and are unbounded on every interval (Aczél 1966); through the substitutions of Appendix A each of the other three equations has corresponding solutions. A rule observed only at some arguments, for example only under doubling, does not establish the equation (Eastwood 2026e).

Remark 2. The family label depends on the declared variable. With s=log⁡Rs=\log R, the multiplicative equation in RR becomes the exponential equation in ss, and Rc=ecsR^{c}=e^{cs}; a power law in RR is an exponential in log⁡R\log R. 3.2 The prediction

Consider any system where a quantity UU grows with recursive depth RR, measured in a declared variable (Remark 2). The system’s composition operator determines how consecutive applications combine, and Definition 1 states the two exact rules.

Definition 1. A response U(R)>0U(R)>0 to recursive depth RR, in the declared variable RR, has multiplicative composition if U(R1R2)=U(R1)U(R2)/U(1)U(R_1R_2)=U(R_1)U(R_2)/U(1) for all R1,R2>0R_1,R_2>0, and additive composition if U(R1+R2)=U(R1)U(R2)/U(0)U(R_1+R_2)=U(R_1)U(R_2)/U(0) for all R1,R2≥0R_1,R_2\geq0.   • Multiplicative composition (depth multiplies, output multiplies): the normalised response U/U(1)U/U(1) satisfies Cauchy’s multiplicative equation, so under the regularity of Theorem 1 it forces U∝RcU\propto R^{c}, a power law.

  • Additive composition (depth adds, output multiplies): the normalised response U/U(0)U/U(0) satisfies Cauchy’s exponential equation, so under the same regularity it forces U∝aRU\propto a^{R}, an exponential.

3.3 Bounded composition

Bounded composition (output constrained to [0,K][0,K]): a bound selects no form. A bound and convergence are different premises: a bounded response need not converge at all, and one that rises monotonically with depth converges to a limit no higher than KK, at a rate and along a path that the bound does not fix (Eastwood 2026d, sect. 5). The saturation curves of the candidate set of Section 4, the logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten and saturation-exponential forms, are such responses; they form a candidate set, not an exhaustive class. None of them satisfies any of the four equations in RR: each rests on the ceiling KK, an assumption added to the composition rule, and satisfies one of the equations only in a variable that the ceiling defines (Proposition 1). Which of them, if any, a bounded domain follows is fixed by dynamics of its own, not by the bound.

Then, as x→∞x\to\infty, f(x)f(x) tends to +∞+\infty, to −∞-\infty or to 00. The framework paper reaches the same conclusion: “Saturation therefore requires its own dynamics and is not a fifth Cauchy cell” (Eastwood 2026d, sect. 5).

The prediction.

If the composition operator of a system is known, in a declared variable, and its response obeys the corresponding rule of Definition 1 for every pair of depths, the family of its scaling law follows before any data are seen: a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where it is additive (Theorem 1). Where output is bounded, the prediction is a saturation curve, which rests on an assumed ceiling and on dynamics that the composition rule does not supply (Proposition 1). Whether a domain’s response obeys such a rule is the premise that the classification of its composition operator supplies, and the comparison of Sections 6 to 10 puts that premise at risk against rival families on each domain’s data. The family is predicted with no curve fitting; its parameters, the exponent, the base and the ceiling, are not, and they are fitted. Where composition holds only approximately, the prediction carries the condition of Remark 4.

4 Prediction protocol

We present a structured prediction comparison across 50 domains in five evidence tiers (Section 5), among them six analytic identities that check the fitter. Each comparison followed the same sequence: known physics fixed the composition operator before any fitting began, the operator class then set which scaling family to expect, published or standard reference data were loaded, and that expectation was finally put to the test by model fitting with AICc-based model selection, run by the author after the classification rule was public (16 March 2026, 23:33 UTC).

For each domain:

  1. Classify the composition operator from known physics (before fitting). Determine whether the system’s recursive amplification process combines multiplicatively, additively, or with a bound.

  2. Predict the scaling family from the operator class: by Cauchy’s equations, a power law for a multiplicative operator and an exponential for an additive one (Theorem 1); for a bounded operator, a saturation curve, which rests on an assumed ceiling and on dynamics of its own rather than on Cauchy’s equations (Proposition 1).

  3. Load published or standard reference data.

  4. Fit candidate models (power law, exponential, logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten, saturation-exponential) to the data: the four saturation curves by nonlinear least squares on the response, and the power law and the exponential by linear least squares on the logarithm of the response. For the muscle force-velocity domain (domain 15), a seventh candidate, the unshifted reciprocal A/(B+P)A/(B+P) in load PP, was fitted and counted in the bounded family. Section 11.1 compares that choice with the physiological equation. The saturation screen accepts a positive fitted asymptote when the largest observed response is at least half its value. This test can pass for a constant prediction. Section 7 gives the word-rank example.

  5. Select the lowest archived score and compare the selected model’s family with the prediction. Section 4.2 gives the score and its parameter count.

4.2 Statistical method

The v2 archive ranks curves by a score it calls AICc (corrected Akaike information criterion). It uses residual sum of squares on the response scale and counts the fitted curve coefficients. The older v1 approximation, AIC≈nln⁡(1−R2)+2k,\begin{equation*} \text{AIC} \approx n \ln(1 - R^2) + 2k, \end{equation*}(1) was used as a heuristic. For nn observations and qq curve coefficients, v2 uses the penalty 2q+2q(q+1)/(n−q−1)2q+2q(q+1)/(n-q-1) and requires n>q+1n>q+1. In the Gaussian regression calculation of Hurvich and Tsai (1989, sect. 2, equations (3) and (4)), estimating the residual variance gives K=q+1K=q+1 parameters. The denominator of the small-sample correction is then n−K−1=n−q−2n-K-1=n-q-2. At n=4n=4 and q=2q=2, the archived denominator is one and the Gaussian denominator is zero. The Gaussian calculation is exact under its linear-model assumptions. Its nonlinear extension is an approximation. The recorded rankings describe the implemented convention. A prospective comparison must specify the observation model, the fitted parameters and which candidates the sample size can distinguish.

The archive scores residuals on the response scale. It estimates power-law and exponential coefficients on the log scale, using positive responses for those fits. Under a fixed curve family, minimising squared error on the response scale can only equal or lower that family’s residual sum. The two families can be affected by different amounts. Their relative rank, their rank against a saturation fit, and the historical match count under a common likelihood would have to be checked in a separately specified analysis.

The primary significance test is a marginal-conditioned permutation test: holding fixed both the predicted and the observed family marginals of the 23 rows retained in version 3.19, the probability of 17 or more matches is p=5.07×10−4p = 5.07 \times 10^{-4}, computed exactly by summing over every table with those margins. The expected match count under this null is 9.3 of 23, not the 7.67 a uniform null gives, because power laws are simply common among the observed fits. A one-sided binomial test against a uniform three-family null gives p=8.17×10−5p = 8.17 \times 10^{-5} and is retained as a sensitivity check; it is the weaker null and is not used as the headline. Versions before 3.19 counted all 25 domains and estimated this test from 400,000 random pairings: p=7×10−5p = 7 \times 10^{-5} for 19 of 25 and p=3.0×10−4p = 3.0 \times 10^{-4} for 14 of 18, where the exact values are 6.05×10−56.05 \times 10^{-5} and 3.27×10−43.27 \times 10^{-4}. 8.1 Where the 13 archived exponents fall

Thirteen entries in the published-exponent table were compared with the dimensional comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1), the expression the derivations of Section 2 reach; it applies under the conditions of Assumption 1.

Assumption 1 (The conditions of the dimensional comparator). This transport model assumes multiplicative composition, dd-dimensional space filling and a constraint on resource flow. Theorem 1(ii) fixes the family. When total demand scales as LdL^{d} and the demand-weighted mean route length as LL, with LL a dimensionless linear size, the throughput exponent against flow-length cost is, asymptotically, d/(d+1)d/(d+1) (Eastwood 2026d). Reading it as metabolic rate against body mass needs a further bridge from transport to mass: in the models of Banavar et al. (2010), the mass exponent is 2/32/3 when flow velocity is independent of body size and reaches 3/43/4 when velocity scales as M1/12M^{1/12}.

Neither Cauchy alone, nor space-filling alone, is sufficient. Where throughput is the metabolic rate and body mass MM scales in proportion to flow-length cost, neither of which the geometry supplies (Eastwood 2026d), organisms with three-dimensional vascular transport and conserved flow should scale as M3/4M^{3/4}, those with two-dimensional transport as M2/3M^{2/3}, and those with one-dimensional filamentous transport as M1/2M^{1/2}.

Note on the 3/43/4 exponent.

Two thirds and three quarters are theoretical reference values in the debate over mammalian metabolic scaling (White and Seymour 2003; Glazier 2022). Its interval excluded both 2/32/3 and 3/43/4. The authors caution that treating related species as independent narrows that interval. In their unadjusted comparison across 17 orders both values lay within the interval. Their temperature-corrected comparisons supported scaling near 2/32/3. However, the d=2d=2 biological prediction remains untested in the strong sense because no known organism possesses a genuinely two-dimensional hierarchical space-filling transport network with conserved flow. These organisms lack the vascular architecture that the d/(d+1)d/(d+1) derivation assumes. The value d=2d=2 appears in cosmology only as an identity, which carries no evidence: in the matter-era solution of Friedmann’s equations for a flat universe the scale factor grows as t2/3t^{2/3} (Friedmann 1922; Einstein and Sitter 1932), and that exponent takes the form d/(d+1)d/(d+1) with d=2d=2 under a change of variables (Eastwood 2026d). In physics, the companion framework paper treats the percolation value as an analytic identity and holds the fragmentation values as unsourced (Eastwood 2026d). Neither field supplies a dimensional confirmation of d=2d=2, and biology does not either. These rows should be understood as empirical proximity to the d=2d=2 comparator, not as dimensional confirmation.

12 What this paper does and does not show

12.1 What it shows

As mathematics: under a stated regularity condition, the non-zero solutions of Cauchy’s four functional equations form exactly four families, linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic (Theorem 1). Read as composition rules in a declared variable, they give a power law where depth and output both multiply and an exponential where depth adds and output multiplies (Definition 1 and Remark 2), and a non-constant response that tends to a finite non-zero ceiling satisfies none of them in that variable (Proposition 1).

The structured comparison tests a publicly stated rule. The predicted family matched the AICc selection in 17 of the 23 archived rows (exact marginal-conditioned permutation p=5.07×10−4p=5.07\times10^{-4}). In the multiplicative and additive subset the corresponding count was 13 of 17 (p=1.47×10−3p=1.47\times10^{-3}). Both calculations include the Brownian reference line and use the candidate set in Section 4.3. The radioactive-decay and Amdahl’s-law rows check the fitter on formula-generated data (Section 6). The secondary tiers are reported separately and never blended (Sections 5 and 8).

12.2 What it does not show

  • It is not proven. The comparison’s predictions were not timestamped in advance of their own fits, its operators were classified by the author, without blinding to the forms established for most of its domains, and its candidate set had no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate (Sections 4.1 and 4.3). A replication with independent classification and a four-family test are preregistered by timestamp (12 August 2026, Bitcoin block 962066); both are written and dated as drafts, not yet submitted for registration.

  • No registered verdict on P18 or P9. This paper informs Law I through two propositions of the theory registration, P18, that the composition operator predicts the family, and P9, the cross-domain form (Eastwood 2026k). Its comparisons are dated results under the rule of March 2026, and the registration scores none of them against any prediction.

  • No universal classification of nature. Theorem 1 classifies the solutions of four functional equations. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most systems are not homomorphisms of either structure (Section 3.1), and finitely many noisy observations cannot establish that a domain’s composition rule holds exactly, or within an error bound over the whole domain (Remark 4).

  • No exponent selected by Theorem 1. The theorem fixes a family and leaves its constant cc free. The comparator d/(d+1)d/(d+1) of Section 8.1 rests on the transport conditions of Assumption 1, not on the theorem.

  • The bounded class rests on added assumptions. No saturating form follows from the four equations (Proposition 1): the bounded class rests on a ceiling on output and on dynamics of its own, and a ceiling alone selects no form (Section 3.3).

13 Limitations

  • No registry submission for this test, and no advance per-domain timestamp for this cohort. The predictions, data arrays, and fitting logic all reside in a single author-written script. The timing of the classification rule, the per-domain assignments and the fits is set out in Section 4.1.

  • Operator classification by author. The composition operator classification is done by the author, not by an independent assessor. A replication with independent operator classification would be more rigorous.

  • The classification was not blind to the established forms. Most of the 25 domains are named laws whose functional forms are established in their fields (Kleiber’s, Zipf’s, Heaps’, Moore’s, Wright’s, Rent’s, Taylor’s, Horton’s and Hack’s laws among them), and their operators were classified by the author with those forms already published. A classification made before the fit is therefore not blind to the expected form, and the comparison cannot separate what a domain’s composition operator predicts from what is already known of its form. The replication with independent classification (Section 4.1) separates them only if its classifiers do not know each domain’s established form, or if its domains have none.

  • The bounded class rests on added assumptions, and a bound selects no form. No saturating form follows from the four equations: each bounded candidate rests on a ceiling on output, an added assumption, and satisfies one of the equations only in a variable that the ceiling defines (Proposition 1); which curve a bounded domain follows is fixed by dynamics of its own (Section 3.3). The bounded category groups the logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten and saturation-exponential forms (with the hyperbolic decay for domain 15, Section 4) by empirical convention rather than mathematical proof.

  • Legacy AIC was an approximation. The v1 test used an R2R^{2}-based AIC approximation, equation (1), not a full likelihood-based nonlinear AIC. The v2 score uses residual sum of squares and counts the curve coefficients. Section 4.2 gives the consequence of also counting an estimated residual variance.

  • Two candidates are fitted on another scale. The power law and the exponential are fitted on the logarithm of the response and scored by AICc on the response itself, while the saturation curves are fitted and scored on the response (Section 4.2). A re-run that fits every candidate on the scale AICc scores is needed to show whether any selection depends on it.

  • Sources of the comparison. Version 3.19 excluded two formula checks and retained the Brownian match. Its 16 tabulated values reproduce the code’s stated line. The transistor counts use compiled specifications. Three island areas differ from their cited file. The four time-crystal settings have source locators and the normalised responses lack a traceable construction (Section 11.1).

  • Family prediction only. The framework predicts the functional family but not the specific exponent. Predicting exponents requires additional structure (dimensionality, constraint type) developed in the companion Foundational paper (Eastwood 2026j).

  • Six empirical misses. The misses are not dismissible. They identify real limits of the framework: small datasets, truncated ranges, high-scatter regimes, and domains where competing physical effects (radiation pressure, corpus boundaries) perturb the ideal prediction.

  • Operator classification is descriptive, not explanatory. The framework does not explain why a particular domain has a multiplicative rather than additive composition operator. It takes the operator as given and predicts the consequence.

  • Baseline-20 rerun is not cleanly comparable to v1. The fitter changed from the R2R^{2} approximation to the v2 score in Section 4.2. The model set expanded and the heat values for domain 1 changed (Section 11.1). The difference between 14/20 in v1 and 15/20 in the v2 baseline cannot be attributed to any one of those changes.

  • Publication bias risk. The 5 new expansion domains were selected after the baseline-20 test. While they were chosen to span new physical regimes (stellar astrophysics, allometric heart rate, VLSI, ecology, geomorphology), the selection was not randomised.

  • Finite model set, and specifically two Cauchy families out, one non-Cauchy family in. The fitter compares six standard curves (power law, exponential, logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten and saturating exponential). Domain 15 also admits hyperbolic decay. Two of the four Cauchy solutions named in Section 3.1 have no candidate (logarithmic clog⁡xc\log x; pure linear cxcx), while the bounded family, which rests on added assumptions (Section 3.3), has four standard candidates and a fifth in domain 15. The true best model for any domain may not be in this set, and Section 4.3 states the consequences for the hit counts.

  • Sample size. Version 3.19 reported 23 rows after its two exclusions. A replication on at least 100 domains chosen under preregistered selection criteria would test how well this result generalises.

13.1 Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim

The limitations above describe where the framework is weak; this subsection states the specific outcomes that would falsify it. The central claim, that a domain’s composition operator, classified from known physics before fitting, predicts its scaling-law family, is defeated by any of the following:

  1. Hit rate at chance. The recorded family-prediction hit rate is 17 of the 23 archived rows (Sections 6 and 7), on the archived candidate set of Section 4.3. With three to four candidate families, assignment at random gives a chance baseline of roughly one in three to one in four. The claim is defeated if a replication whose domain list and per-domain predictions are timestamped in advance, and whose operator classes are assigned by an external human classifier, returns a hit rate not significantly above that chance baseline. This equal-family benchmark in the original falsifier assumes equally likely assignments. The primary analysis in this edition conditions on the observed family counts (Section 4.2). The advance-timestamped run on the record is the 12-domain extension of 17 March 2026, its predictions committed to the public repository at 00:19:19 UTC in commit 8edaec0, attestation class public repository commit date, its fits run twenty-three minutes later: 10 of 12, and 9 of 11 on the strictly fresh rows. Its predictions were thus fixed and timestamped in advance of its fits; it is described neither as registered nor as preregistered, and it is not an accepted registry submission; its twelve classifications are the author’s own, so this criterion has not yet been put at risk in the form stated here.

  2. Post-hoc operator classification. The test’s strength depends on the operator being assignable from independently known physics without seeing the data. The claim is defeated if, in a blind trial, independent assessors cannot reproduce the author’s operator classifications from the physics alone, or if any classification can be shown to have been chosen to fit the observed exponent.

  3. Fit degeneracy. The prediction is meaningful only if the candidate families are empirically distinguishable on the data at hand. It is defeated for any domain where the fitter reaches the observed curve equally well under the wrong family; that is, the family assignment carries no discriminating information for that domain’s range and noise.

  4. Unrationalised misses. Six domains miss (Section 7). The misses are consistent with the claim only if their causes are fixed as a preregistered exclusion rule before the next test, not supplied after seeing which domains missed. The claim is defeated if the miss rate rises under a preregistered replication with no post-hoc exclusions.

  5. Four-family re-run. The four-family confirmatory study, preregistered by timestamp (Section 4.3), re-runs this comparison with logarithmic and pure linear candidates included. The claim is weakened if the hit rate falls materially when the missing families are available, and defeated if it falls to the chance baseline; hits that migrate to the logarithmic family will be reported as reclassifications, not as new confirmations.

  6. Exploratory ceiling. Until the operator classes and the per-domain predictions for a randomised domain sample are fixed before any fitting by an external human classifier and timestamped in advance with the attestation class stated, the claim cannot rise above exploratory, and any presentation of it as established would itself falsify the paper’s stated evidential status. A registry deposit raises the attestation class; it is not what lifts the ceiling. Part of that condition is already met on the record: the 12-domain extension of 17 March 2026 (item 1) had its per-domain predictions committed before its fits; its classifier was the author, its candidate set was the pre-correction one, and the author’s demotion of 00:56 that night stands so far as it rests on the packet’s own written condition, deposit to a registry before data extraction, which was not met, and is reversed as of 7 September 2026 only so far as it rested on the absence of a registry form.

14 Implications

If the framework holds under independent preregistered replication, it would suggest:

  1. The forms of scaling laws are not coincidences but mathematical consequences of composition structure: a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where it is additive, in the declared variable and under the regularity of Theorem 1, while a saturating form needs in addition a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own (Proposition 1).

  2. The same composition rule that fixes the power-law family of a mouse’s heart rate would fix the family, though not the exponent, of how neural networks improve with scale.

  3. Critical phenomena would be one case of the wider rule: scale invariance gives their power-law form through the multiplicative equation, while their exponents are left to the renormalisation group. Section 2 sets out what earlier work already states on scale invariance, universality and renormalisation (Newman 2005; Stanley 1999), and what this paper adds.

  4. Any new system’s scaling family can be predicted from first principles by classifying its composition operator, before any data fitting. The prediction follows only where the composition rule holds exactly, or within an error bound over the whole domain, in a declared variable (Remark 4); finitely many noisy observations cannot establish such a bound, so each such prediction remains a hypothesis for the system’s data to test.

  5. Domain-specific explanations of the forms of scaling laws (fractal networks, learning curves) may share a single constraint on form, that of the functional equations Cauchy (1821) solved, while the values of their exponents, and behaviour outside the four families, need physics of their own (Section 3.1).

These implications are conditional. The current evidence is not definitive.

15 Conclusion

Plant an acorn and given centuries, you get an oak. But plant that oak’s acorn, and its acorn, recursively across millennia, and you get a forest that shapes the climate of continents. What we plant in these systems will compound across scales we cannot imagine. The seed determines the forest.

Read as composition rules, the functional equations Cauchy solved 200 years ago (Cauchy 1821) say that the seed also determines the form of the forest. Multiplicative seeds produce power laws. Additive seeds produce exponentials. Both hold under the regularity of Theorem 1, in the variable in which depth composes. Bounded seeds can produce saturation, but not from Cauchy’s equations: the ceiling is an added assumption (Proposition 1), and the form of the saturation needs dynamics of its own. On the archived candidate set of Section 4.3, the predicted family matched the selected family in 17 of the 23 archived rows (exact marginal-conditioned permutation p=5.07×10−4p=5.07\times10^{-4}). Those 23 rows retain the Brownian match, whose inputs reproduce the stated reference line. The two earlier exclusions are reported in Section 6. The four-family re-run is preregistered by timestamp and not yet submitted for registration. The 13 exponents in the secondary table sit nearest the assigned comparators. Seven trace to the reported quantities at the stated precision. One further value matches two subgroups of birds. Five still lack a matching estimate for the named population.

The mathematics was always there, and earlier work had read invariance as fixing the possible forms of laws (Section 2); this paper states a rule for predicting the scaling family from the composition assigned to a domain and compares its predictions with published data. In the 12-domain extension, the assignments were recorded before the fits (Section 8.4). Whether that reading holds under independent scrutiny is now a question for the scientific community to answer.

F The paper’s place in the programme

Placement.

Version 3.15 printed this statement of the paper’s place in the programme beneath its author line: “The classification behind Law I’s form: under the stated axioms, Cauchy’s functional equations force a power law for multiplicative composition and an exponential for additive composition, with saturation compatible with bounded composition and exhaustiveness open.” Within Recursive Dynamics, the field the author founded, and its founding theory, the ARC Theory, the paper informs Law I, the ARC Principle, a law under test: it tests the classification behind the law’s form, not the law’s exponent. It decides no proposition of Laws II to V, whose status Section 12.3 gives; it carries no proposition of the Eden Protocol, and it makes no claim about the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned Recursive Intelligence.

The propositions it informs.

Version 1.104 of the theory registration places five propositions in ARC-1, the register that tests the first law: P1, P2, P8, P9 and P18. This paper informs two of them. P18, the composition operator predicts the family, reads: “Classified in advance by how a domain composes its inputs and outputs, the scaling family that best fits that domain’s data is the family the classification predicts, and not one chosen after the curve is seen.” P9, the cross-domain form, reads: “Where an effective dimension can be assigned to a domain, the growth exponent follows the dimensional relation with the dimension supplied by that domain’s equation of state.” The primary comparison of Section 6 bears on P18, and the published exponents of Section 8.1 on P9; neither is a registered verdict (Section 12.2). The programme’s Operational Definitions, version 2.0.0, state Cauchy’s multiplicative equation as EQ-16, which informs P18, and record that what has to be checked in each application is whether its regularity conditions hold over the domain claimed.

Frame key.

This paper carries a result: under a stated regularity condition and in a declared variable, Cauchy’s functional equations force a power law under multiplicative composition and an exponential under additive composition (Theorem 1), turning a two-hundred-year-old identity into a physically testable prediction about the shapes empirical scaling curves may take. For bounded composition no form follows from the equations: a saturation curve rests on a ceiling on output, which none of the four equations supplies, and on dynamics of its own (Proposition 1). Inside the ARC Theory it gives the classification behind the form of Law I, the ARC Principle, and is one of the ARC/Eden experiments, the mathematical spine that ties composition constraints to observed functional forms. Section 2 of The Eden Vision (Section II.A.8 in its version 2.9 and earlier) enumerates the nearest prior documents to the programme’s doctrine of values embedded at the foundation, with the differential against each; this paper’s own antecedents are set out in Section 2.
