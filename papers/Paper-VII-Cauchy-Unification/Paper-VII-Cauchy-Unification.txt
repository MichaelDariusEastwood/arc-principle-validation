                                                           Abstract
           Why do the same scaling forms recur in systems that share no mechanism, from a mouse’s
       heartbeat to neural networks? A rule that predicts the form of a scaling law from how a system
       composes, before its data are seen, would let a scaling claim be tested rather than fitted, including the
       power-law form that Law I of the ARC Theory, a law under test, gives recursive capability. Under
       a stated regularity condition, the non-zero solutions of Cauchy’s four functional equations form
       exactly four families: linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic. Read as composition rules in a
       declared variable, they give a power law where depth and output both multiply and an exponential
       where depth adds and output multiplies. A saturating form follows from none of them: it needs
       added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own, since a ceiling alone selects no
       form. In a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule, the composition operator of
       each of 25 empirical domains was classified from known physics before the author fitted candidate
       models and selected among them by AICc. The predicted family was the one selected in 19 of 25
       domains (marginal-conditioned permutation 𝑝 = 7 × 10−5 ), on a six-model candidate set with no
       logarithmic and no pure linear candidate, and in 14 of the 18 multiplicative and additive domains
       (𝑝 = 3.0 × 10−4 ). Secondary tiers are reported separately and never blended: a baseline rerun gives
       15 of 20; 13 published metabolic exponents sit nearest the dimensional comparator 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1), seven
       of them still without a per-row source; 6 analytic identities check the fitter. The predictions were not
       timestamped in advance of their own fits and the operators were classified by the author, so the result
       is not proven; a replication with independent classification and a four-family test are preregistered by
       timestamp and await submission.

1    Introduction
Why do scaling laws exist at all? Why does a mouse’s heart beat faster than an elephant’s by the same
mathematical relationship that governs how cities grow, how solar panels get cheaper, and how neural
networks improve with size? The standard answer is domain-specific. Biologists invoke fractal vascular
networks. Physicists invoke renormalisation group flow. Economists invoke learning-by-doing. Each
explanation works within its domain but offers no reason why the same mathematical forms recur across
domains that share no physical mechanism. For this programme the question matters beyond any one
domain. I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it
reasons; only proof will move the world. A rule that predicts the form of a scaling law from how a
system composes, before its data are seen, lets a claim about growth be tested rather than fitted, including
the power-law form that Law I of the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation) gives
recursive capability; Law I, the ARC Principle, is a law under test, stated formally in Paper I.
    This paper states and tests a domain-independent answer: under the conditions stated next, the form
of a scaling law is determined by the composition operator of the underlying recursive process, and
Cauchy’s functional equations constrain which forms are possible. Stated exactly (Section 3), under a
regularity condition and in a declared variable, composition in which depth and output both multiply
admits only the power-law family, and composition in which depth adds while output multiplies admits
only the exponential. The equations fix the family, not its exponent. A saturating form follows from
none of them: the bounded case rests on added assumptions, a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own
(Proposition 1), and a ceiling alone selects no form. Two domains of the comparison show what the
rule asks. In radioactive decay (domain 9 of Table 2), elapsed time adds while the surviving fraction
multiplies, so the composition is additive and the predicted family is the exponential; the domain’s values
are exact reference values, so it shows the rule at work rather than testing it (Section 11.1). The loss of
neural language models against their number of parameters (domain 19; Kaplan et al. 2020) was classified
as multiplicative, so the predicted family was the power law, and AICc model selection returned it.
    In plain words, the result is this. In a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule,
the family predicted from each domain’s composition operator was the one that AICc model selection
returned in 19 of the 25 empirical domains, on a six-model candidate set that held no logarithmic and no
pure linear candidate (Section 4.3). The operators were classified by the author, and the predictions for
this cohort were not timestamped in advance of their own fits (Section 4.1); the comparison is therefore
neither a proof nor a blind trial, and Sections 5 to 10 report it with its misses and with its evidence tiers
kept apart. The novel contribution is not Cauchy’s mathematics itself, nor the idea that invariance or one
principle fixes the possible forms of laws, which earlier work states (Section 2), but the use of Cauchy’s
four equations together as one predictor of a domain’s scaling family from its composition rule, applied
before the fit and across domains, and the test of that predictor.
    Our contributions are as follows.
    1. A precise statement of the four classical functional equations as composition constraints: under a
       stated regularity condition each has one family of non-zero solutions, linear, power law, exponential
       or logarithmic (Theorem 1), and the family is named only once the variable in which depth composes
       is declared (Remark 2).
    2. A proof that a saturating response satisfies none of the four equations, so a bounded composition
       class rests on added assumptions, a ceiling on output and a rule in a variable that the ceiling defines,
       which the paper names for each of its bounded candidates (Proposition 1).
    3. A classification rule, applied before fitting, that predicts a scaling family from a domain’s composition
       operator (Section 4), and a structured prediction comparison under that publicly stated rule across
       50 domains in five evidence tiers, each reported separately (Sections 5 to 10).

2    Related work
The mathematics this paper uses is classical, and several of its ideas have specific precedents. The closest
existing work falls into five groups: functional equations and the forms of laws; dimensional analysis
and similarity; scale invariance; composition rules and generative processes; and the derivations of the
dimensional comparator 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1). Each work is stated below in its own terms, and the closing paragraphs
state what this paper claims as its own.

Functional equations and the forms of laws. Cauchy’s functional equations, and the forms of their
solutions, go back to Cauchy (1821). Aczél (1966) is the standard reference on all four, with applications
to geometry, mechanics, business mathematics, probability theory and information theory; it does not
treat scaling laws across domains. That invariance can fix the possible forms of a law before any data are
seen is the content of Luce (1959), the closest antecedent of this paper’s approach. For a continuous law
relating a dependent variable to an independent one, each measured on a ratio, an interval or a logarithmic
interval scale, Luce requires that an admissible transformation of the independent variable lead only to an
admissible transformation of the dependent variable, and that it leave the form of the law unchanged; he
lists the possible laws for each combination of scale types, among them the power function when both
variables are ratio scales. Bingham and Ostaszewski (2015) state that the Cauchy functional equation is
central to regular variation, and study the Goldie equation and inequality of Karamata regular variation,
its counterpart in Beurling regular variation and the Gołąb-Schinzel equation; theirs is a mathematical
study, with no application to scaling in physical, biological or social systems. Regular variation describes
positive functions whose scale ratios 𝑓 (𝜆𝑥)/ 𝑓 (𝑥) converge to a power of 𝜆 as 𝑥 grows, allowing a slowly
varying factor at finite scale (Bingham et al. 1987). Hyers (1941) proves that the additive equation is
stable under a defect bound that holds over the whole domain, the result on which Remark 3 draws.

Dimensional analysis and similarity. Buckingham (1914) shows, from the principle of dimensional
homogeneity, that every complete physical equation among 𝑛 quantities measured with 𝑘 fundamental
units is reducible to a relation among the 𝑛 − 𝑘 independent dimensionless products of powers of those
quantities; where all the other arguments of that relation are held constant, the remaining product is
constant whatever the unknown relation, so any one of the quantities in it varies with the others as a
product of known powers. Barenblatt (1996) sets out dimensional analysis, physical similarity theory
and a general theory of scaling phenomena, and treats scaling as intermediate asymptotics: self-similar

behaviour that sets in once the influence of initial and boundary conditions has disappeared, while the
system is still far from equilibrium. The book constructs self-similar solutions by dimensional analysis
and treats self-similarities of the second kind separately. Both start from the dimensions of the quantities.
This paper does not: it fixes the family by the rule by which a domain composes and leaves the exponent
free (Section 12.2), and the 𝑑 of its comparator 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) is a dimension of transport geometry, not a
dimension of units (Assumption 1).

Scale invariance, universality and renormalisation. That scale invariance forces a power law, by
solving 𝑝(𝑏𝑥) = 𝑔(𝑏) 𝑝(𝑥), is textbook (Newman 2005, section III.E). Newman shows that a probability
distribution whose shape is unchanged when the unit of 𝑥 changes, so that 𝑝(𝑏𝑥) = 𝑔(𝑏) 𝑝(𝑥) for every 𝑏,
must be a power law: setting 𝑥 = 1 turns the condition into 𝑝(𝑏𝑥) = 𝑝(𝑏) 𝑝(𝑥)/𝑝(1), the multiplicative
equation of Theorem 1(ii) normalised at one, which he solves for a differentiable 𝑝, and he links such
scale-free behaviour to continuous phase transitions. Scaling, universality and renormalisation are linked
in the review of Stanley (1999), which presents them as three of the many important themes that have
served to provide the framework of much of the current understanding of critical phenomena. What this
paper adds is the forward test: classifying a domain’s composition before its curve is seen, and scoring
the family that follows.

Composition rules and generative processes. That a composition rule, the structure of a generative
process or the information a process keeps selects among exponential, lognormal and power-law forms
also has precedents. Bíró (2008) proves that the infinite repetition of an arbitrary composition rule on
an infinitesimal amount leads to a rule with a formal logarithm, so that the stationary distribution of
energy in the thermodynamical limit is the Boltzmann-Gibbs exponential composed with that formal
logarithm; interactions that depend only on the relative four-momentum squared then give kinetic energy
distributions of the cut power-law (Tsallis-Pareto) form in the high-energy limit. Mitzenmacher (2004)
surveys generative models for power-law and lognormal distributions: multiplicative processes, which
follow Gibrat’s law of proportionate effect, generate lognormal distributions, and a small change, such as
a minimum that acts as a lower reflective barrier, yields a power law instead. Frank (2009) presents an
informational framework for the common patterns of nature, based on the method of maximum entropy,
in which those patterns follow from simple constraints on information: any aggregation of processes that
preserves information only about the mean attracts to the exponential pattern, and any that preserves
information only about the geometric mean attracts to the power-law pattern; his examples include the age
of disease onset, rates of amino acid substitution and the composition of ecological communities. The
idea that one principle selects the common form of patterns across unrelated fields is therefore stated in
Frank’s work, and the idea that invariance under changes of scale restricts the possible forms of a law is
stated in Luce’s. The works of Newman, Bíró, Mitzenmacher and Frank concern probability distributions.
Luce’s concerns laws between measured variables, with the form fixed by the scale types of the variables;
this paper fixes the family of a law by the rule by which its domain composes, classified from the domain’s
known physics before the fit.

Derivations of the dimensional comparator. The expression 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1), or a special case of it, is
reached in earlier work through different frameworks. West et al. (1997) derive allometric scaling relations,
including the three-quarter power law for metabolic rates, from a model of how essential materials are
transported through space-filling fractal networks of branching tubes, assuming that the energy dissipated
is minimised and that the terminal tubes do not vary with body size; beyond mammalian circulation, their
model predicts structural and functional properties of vertebrate cardiovascular and respiratory systems,
plant vascular systems, insect tracheal tubes and other distribution networks. The empirical exponent is

companies, and does not mention Cauchy’s functional equations.

What is and is not claimed here. These derivations reach the expression 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1), in its general
form or as a special case, through different frameworks: fractal branching networks, the geometry of
efficient transportation networks, quantum metabolism, urban scaling (as 𝐷/(𝐷 + 𝐻) with 𝐻 = 1),
producer-consumer dynamics, optimal vascular geometry and dynamic energy budget theory; of these,
the He and Chen item could not be confirmed at a registry on 3 October 2026, and the abstract of
Maino et al. (2014) states no exponent. They are not counted here as separate confirmations of the
comparator. This paper’s hypothesis is that where such a derivation reaches 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1), it rests on
three conditions: multiplicative composition, which Theorem 1 constrains to the power-law family;
𝑑-dimensional space-filling transport geometry; and a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource
flow (Assumption 1). On this hypothesis, Cauchy’s equation accounts for the family and the shared
transport conditions for the value. A common numerical fraction does not by itself identify a common
mechanism, as the framework paper now states (On the Origin of Scaling Laws, version 3.14), so the
hypothesis is tested derivation by derivation, by whether each one’s premises include the three conditions.

3     The theory
Cauchy’s four functional equations, additive, multiplicative, exponential and logarithmic, each have
one family of non-zero solutions under a stated regularity condition (Theorem 1). We argue that this
200-year-old mathematical result has a physically testable consequence: under that regularity condition,
it forces a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where depth adds while
output multiplies. Where output is bounded it forces no form. A saturating response satisfies none of the
four equations (Proposition 1): a saturation curve compatible with a bound rests on added assumptions, a
ceiling on output and dynamics of its own, and the bound alone selects no form (Section 3.3). Once the
variable in which depth composes is declared (Remark 2), the family is determined by the composition
operator of the underlying recursive amplification process.

3.1   Cauchy’s four equations
Augustin-Louis Cauchy established in the early nineteenth century that the continuous solutions of four
functional equations form four one-parameter families (Cauchy 1821). The solutions are families, not
unique functions, and the same families follow under conditions weaker than continuity (Aczél 1966,
chapter 2). Theorem 1 states the four equations with their domains and the regularity each needs.

Theorem 1 (Cauchy’s four equations under regularity). Let 𝑓 satisfy one of the following equations for
all arguments in its domain, and suppose 𝑓 is continuous, or Lebesgue measurable, or bounded on some
interval of positive length.
   (i) 𝑓 : R → R, 𝑓 (𝑥 + 𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) + 𝑓 (𝑦): then 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑐𝑥.
  (ii) 𝑓 : (0, ∞) → (0, ∞), 𝑓 (𝑥𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) 𝑓 (𝑦): then 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑥 𝑐 .
 (iii) 𝑓 : R → (0, ∞), 𝑓 (𝑥 + 𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) 𝑓 (𝑦): then 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑎 𝑥 , with 𝑎 = 𝑒 𝑐 > 0.
 (iv) 𝑓 : (0, ∞) → R, 𝑓 (𝑥𝑦) = 𝑓 (𝑥) + 𝑓 (𝑦): then 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑐 log 𝑥.
Here 𝑐 is a real constant, the logarithm is natural, and each stated function satisfies its equation. If the
targets in (ii) and (iii) are taken to be all real numbers, the only further solution is 𝑓 ≡ 0. Restricting the
arguments of (i) and (iii) to (0, ∞) leaves the same families.

Proof in Appendix A.1.
    These are not four independent results. They are four faces of one constraint: the requirement that a
function be compatible with a binary operation. Under regularity (continuity, or Lebesgue measurability,
or boundedness on an interval of positive length), a function that carries one of the two structures, addition


                                                      8
                                              Cauchy Unification


on the real numbers or multiplication on the positive real numbers, into one of the two falls into exactly
four families: linear ( 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑐𝑥), power law ( 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑥 𝑐 ), exponential ( 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑎 𝑥 ) and logarithmic
( 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑐 log 𝑥). This exhausts the homomorphisms. It does not exhaust scaling laws, because most
systems are not homomorphisms of either structure: saturating behaviour, curvature on log axes and
multi-regime crossovers all lie outside the grid, and the grid says nothing about them. A critical power law
can have the power-law form; Theorem 1 neither excludes it nor derives its critical exponent or physical
mechanism. The additive equation yields the linear form 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑐𝑥; the multiplicative equation yields
power laws; the exponential equation governs exponential growth and decay; and the logarithmic equation
governs the map 𝑐 log 𝑥, which is multiplicative in input and additive in output; its increments diminish,
but it is unbounded, so it is not a saturating family.
Remark 1. Without a regularity condition the additive equation has other solutions, which are not Lebesgue
measurable and are unbounded on every interval (Aczél 1966); through the substitutions of Appendix A
each of the other three equations has corresponding solutions. A rule observed only at some arguments,
for example only under doubling, does not establish the equation (Eastwood 2026f).
Remark 2. The family label depends on the declared variable. With 𝑠 = log 𝑅, the multiplicative equation
in 𝑅 becomes the exponential equation in 𝑠, and 𝑅 𝑐 = 𝑒 𝑐𝑠 ; a power law in 𝑅 is an exponential in log 𝑅.
Normalisation matters as well: 𝑌 (𝑥) = 𝐴𝑥 𝑐 satisfies 𝑌 (𝑥𝑦) = 𝑌 (𝑥)𝑌 (𝑦)/𝐴, so the equation applies to
𝑌 /𝑌 (1) (Eastwood 2026g). The classification therefore fixes the variable in which depth composes, and
its baseline, before it names a family.

3.2     The prediction
Consider any system where a quantity 𝑈 grows with recursive depth 𝑅, measured in a declared variable
(Remark 2). The system’s composition operator determines how consecutive applications combine, and
Definition 1 states the two exact rules.
Definition 1. A response 𝑈 (𝑅) > 0 to recursive depth 𝑅, in the declared variable 𝑅, has multiplicative
composition if 𝑈 (𝑅1 𝑅2 ) = 𝑈 (𝑅1 )𝑈 (𝑅2 )/𝑈 (1) for all 𝑅1 , 𝑅2 > 0, and additive composition if 𝑈 (𝑅1 +𝑅2 ) =
𝑈 (𝑅1 )𝑈 (𝑅2 )/𝑈 (0) for all 𝑅1 , 𝑅2 ≥ 0. The first rule needs 𝑈 for 𝑅 > 0; the second needs it for 𝑅 ≥ 0,
with 𝑅 = 0 the baseline from which depth is counted.
      • Multiplicative composition (depth multiplies, output multiplies): the normalised response 𝑈/𝑈 (1)
        satisfies Cauchy’s multiplicative equation, so under the regularity of Theorem 1 it forces 𝑈 ∝ 𝑅 𝑐 , a
        power law.
      • Additive composition (depth adds, output multiplies): the normalised response 𝑈/𝑈 (0) satisfies
        Cauchy’s exponential equation, so under the same regularity it forces 𝑈 ∝ 𝑎 𝑅 , an exponential.
Remark 3. Where composition holds only approximately, as the sign ∼ in Section 2.2 of version 3.15
wrote it, a family follows only under a stated error bound that holds over the whole domain: for example,
if a continuous 𝐹 : (0, ∞) → (0, ∞) has a logarithmic defect | log 𝐹 (𝑥𝑦) − log 𝐹 (𝑥) − log 𝐹 (𝑦)| bounded
by 𝜀 for every pair of arguments, then 𝑒 − 𝜀 𝑥 𝑐 ≤ 𝐹 (𝑥) ≤ 𝑒 𝜀 𝑥 𝑐 for a unique real 𝑐 (Hyers 1941; Eastwood
2026g). Finitely many noisy observations cannot establish such a bound, so the comparison in Sections 6
to 10 compares predicted and selected families; it does not establish the equation in any domain.

3.3     Bounded composition
Bounded composition (output constrained to [0, 𝐾]): a bound selects no form. A bound and convergence
are different premises: a bounded response need not converge at all, and one that rises monotonically

                                                       9
                                             Cauchy Unification


with depth converges to a limit no higher than 𝐾, at a rate and along a path that the bound does not fix
(Eastwood 2026g, Section 5). The saturation curves of the candidate set of Section 4, the logistic, Hill,
Michaelis-Menten and saturation-exponential forms, are such responses; they form a candidate set, not an
exhaustive class. None of them satisfies any of the four equations in 𝑅: each rests on the ceiling 𝐾, an
assumption added to the composition rule, and satisfies one of the equations only in a variable that the
ceiling defines (Proposition 1). Which of them, if any, a bounded domain follows is fixed by dynamics of
its own, not by the bound.

Proposition 1 (Saturating responses lie outside the four equations). Let 𝑓 be a non-constant solution
of one of the four equations of Theorem 1, with the regularity it assumes. Then, as 𝑥 → ∞, 𝑓 (𝑥) tends
to +∞, to −∞ or to 0. Hence a non-constant response 𝑈 (𝑅) that tends to a finite ceiling 𝐾 ≠ 0 as
𝑅 → ∞ satisfies none of the four equations in 𝑅. Each bounded candidate of Section 4 satisfies one of
them only in a variable that the ceiling defines: the saturation exponential 𝑈 = 𝐾 (1 − 𝑒 −𝑐𝑅 ) makes the
deficit 𝐾 − 𝑈 = 𝐾𝑒 −𝑐𝑅 an exponential in 𝑅; the logistic 𝑈 = 𝐾/[1 + 𝑒 −𝜆(𝑅−𝑅0 ) ] makes the log-odds
log[𝑈/(𝐾 − 𝑈)] = 𝜆(𝑅 − 𝑅0 ) linear in 𝑅 measured from 𝑅0 ; and the Hill and Michaelis-Menten forms
𝑈 = 𝐾 𝑅 𝑛 /(𝑘 𝑛 + 𝑅 𝑛 ) (𝑛 = 1 for Michaelis-Menten) make it 𝑛 log(𝑅/𝑘), logarithmic in 𝑅/𝑘. The ceiling
𝐾 and the choice of transformed variable are assumptions added to the composition rule; they do not
follow from any of the four equations.

Proof in Appendix A.2.
   The framework paper reaches the same conclusion: “Saturation therefore requires its own dynamics
and is not a fifth Cauchy cell” (Eastwood 2026g, Section 5).

The prediction. If the composition operator of a system is known, in a declared variable, and its
response obeys the corresponding rule of Definition 1 for every pair of depths, the family of its scaling
law follows before any data are seen: a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential
where it is additive (Theorem 1). Where output is bounded, the prediction is a saturation curve, which
rests on an assumed ceiling and on dynamics that the composition rule does not supply (Proposition 1).
Whether a domain’s response obeys such a rule is the premise that the classification of its composition
operator supplies, and the comparison of Sections 6 to 10 puts that premise at risk against rival families on
each domain’s data. The family is predicted with no curve fitting; its parameters, the exponent, the base
and the ceiling, are not, and they are fitted. Where composition holds only approximately, the prediction
carries the condition of Remark 3.

4.2   Statistical method
The v2 test uses proper AICc (corrected Akaike information criterion) computed from residual sum of
squares, sample size, and parameter count, not the 𝑅 2 -based approximation used in the legacy v1 test.
The v1 approximation,
                                      AIC ≈ 𝑛 ln(1 − 𝑅 2 ) + 2𝑘,                                   (1)



                                                    12
                                              Cauchy Unification


was adequate as a heuristic but should not have been described as strict AIC in the standard statistical
sense. The v2 rewrite corrects this.
    Every candidate’s AICc is computed from the residual sum of squares of the response itself. The
power law and the exponential are scored at the parameters of their fit on the logarithmic scale, which
do not minimise that sum, so their AICc is never lower than at their own least-squares optimum on the
response: the procedure can only disfavour them against the saturation curves. That is conservative
for the matches among the 18 multiplicative and additive domains, may have contributed to the four
multiplicative misses, and can favour the bounded family in the seven bounded domains; whether it
changed any selection has not been checked.
    The primary significance test is a marginal-conditioned permutation test: holding both the predicted
and the observed family marginals fixed and permuting the pairing 400,000 times, the probability of 19
or more matches is 𝑝 = 7 × 10−5 . The expected match count under this null is 9.5 of 25, not the 8.33
a uniform null gives, because power laws are simply common among the observed fits. A one-sided
binomial test against a uniform three-family null gives 𝑝 = 1.56 × 10−5 and is retained as a sensitivity
check; it is the weaker null and is not used as the headline. Sigma equivalences are not quoted because
they shift with the choice of null. The script that computes this test, which parses the published Table 2
directly so that it cannot drift from this paper, is held in a private repository and is not yet public; the test
can be recomputed from that table’s Predicted and AICc best columns, which fix both marginals.

4.3   A model-set limitation this paper must state against itself
Section 3.1 names four Cauchy families. The candidate model set of this section contains six models
(seven for domain 15, step 4): a power law and an exponential, which span two of those families, and four
saturation curves, which lie outside the four equations (Proposition 1). Two of the four continuous Cauchy
solutions have no candidate of their own: the logarithmic form 𝑐 log 𝑥 and the pure linear form 𝑐𝑥. The
power-law candidate contains the linear form as its case of exponent one, and 𝑐𝑥 satisfies the multiplicative
rule of Definition 1, so a linear response is fitted as a power law and counts as a match wherever its
domain was classified as multiplicative. One consequence of the missing logarithmic candidate runs each
way. A genuinely logarithmic domain could not have been classified correctly, because the fitter had no
logarithmic candidate to select, so some of the six misses may have been guaranteed misses; and some of
the nineteen hits may be logarithmic relations that a power law fits acceptably over a finite range. Every
hit count in this paper therefore carries this qualifier, and none may be quoted without it. A confirmatory
study that re-runs the comparison with the full four-family candidate set, with per-cell outcomes specified
in advance, has been drafted and preregistered by timestamp (Section 4.1), and awaits human submission
as a draft registration (the four-family logarithmic-cell test in the programme’s Dated Prediction Register
(Eastwood 2026l), alongside the fresh-domain extension study). Until that re-run reports, the numbers
below are the six-model result, exactly as computed, and nothing stronger.

is 2/3 when flow velocity is independent of body size and reaches 3/4 when velocity scales as 𝑀 1/12 .

    Neither Cauchy alone, nor space-filling alone, is sufficient. Where throughput is the metabolic rate and
body mass 𝑀 scales in proportion to flow-length cost, neither of which the geometry supplies (Eastwood
2026g), organisms with three-dimensional vascular transport and conserved flow should scale as 𝑀 3/4 ,
those with two-dimensional transport as 𝑀 2/3 , and those with one-dimensional filamentous transport as
𝑀 1/2 .

Note on the 3/4 exponent. The empirical value of the mammalian metabolic scaling exponent is debated,
with estimates ranging from approximately 0.67 to 0.75 depending on taxon, mass range, temperature
correction, and statistical method (White and Seymour 2003; Glazier 2005, 2022). The largest modern
dataset (White and Seymour 2003, compiling 619 species) gives a maximum-likelihood estimate of 0.687
(95% CI 0.674 to 0.701), which excludes 0.750. The 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) prediction of 0.750 for 𝑑 = 3 matches
the upper end of the empirical range. The variation itself is consistent with the framework: organisms
with effective transport dimensions between 2 and 3 would produce exponents between 2/3 and 3/4. The
framework accommodates this variation rather than requiring a single universal exponent; since such
dimensions can produce any exponent in that range, the variation is not evidence for it.

9      Significance framing
The primary result (𝑝 = 7 × 10−5 , permutation) deserves honest contextualisation.
In absolute terms: under the uniform-chance binomial null, roughly 1 in 64,000: if 64,000 researchers
      each picked scaling families at random for 25 domains, on average one would match 19 or more by
      chance. The stricter permutation null, which concedes that power laws are simply common among
      real fits, still leaves 𝑝 = 7 × 10−5 .
Relative to convention: the permutation test lands nearly three orders of magnitude beyond 𝑝 < 0.05,
      and the binomial test more than three. Sigma equivalences are not quoted because they shift with
      the choice of null.
What it supports: the result is strong enough to publish, as a structured prediction comparison under a
      publicly stated rule rather than a per-domain advance timestamp, as evidence that Cauchy-type
      composition constraints predict scaling families at well-above-chance rates.
What it does not support: it is not strong enough to claim the theory is proven. The particle-physics
      discovery threshold is 5𝜎 (𝑝 ≈ 3 × 10−7 ). The test lacks formal preregistration. The operator
      classifications were made by the author, not an independent panel. The candidate set contained no
      logarithmic and no pure linear model, so the hit rate is conditional on that model set (Section 4.3).
What is needed: independent replication with preregistered operator classification, ideally by researchers
      with no stake in the outcome.

12     What this paper does and does not show
12.1    What it shows
As mathematics: under a stated regularity condition, the non-zero solutions of Cauchy’s four functional
equations form exactly four families, linear, power law, exponential and logarithmic (Theorem 1). Read as
composition rules in a declared variable, they give a power law where depth and output both multiply and
an exponential where depth adds and output multiplies (Definition 1 and Remark 2), and a non-constant
response that tends to a finite non-zero ceiling satisfies none of them in that variable (Proposition 1).
    As evidence: a structured prediction comparison under a publicly stated rule. The composition
operator of each of 25 empirical domains was classified from known physics before the fitting procedure
ran, and the predicted family was the family that AICc model selection returned in 19 of the 25 (marginal-
conditioned permutation 𝑝 = 7 × 10−5 ), on the six-model candidate set of Section 4.3, and in 14 of the 18
multiplicative and additive domains (permutation 𝑝 = 3.0 × 10−4 ; Section 6). The secondary tiers are
reported separately and never blended (Sections 5 and 8).

12.2    What it does not show
     • It is not proven. The comparison’s predictions were not timestamped in advance of their own fits,
       its operators were classified by the author, without blinding to the forms established for most of
       its domains, and its candidate set had no logarithmic and no pure linear candidate (Sections 4.1
       and 4.3). A replication with independent classification and a four-family test are preregistered by
       timestamp (12 August 2026, Bitcoin block 962066) and await human submission.

     • No registered verdict on P18 or P9. This paper informs Law I through two propositions of the
       theory registration, P18, that the composition operator predicts the family, and P9, the cross-domain

13     Limitations
     • No registry submission for this test, and no advance per-domain timestamp for this cohort.
       The predictions, data arrays, and fitting logic all reside in a single author-written script. The timing
       of the classification rule, the per-domain assignments and the fits is set out in Section 4.1.

                                        Cauchy Unification


• Operator classification by author. The composition operator classification is done by the author,
  not by an independent assessor. A replication with independent operator classification would be
  more rigorous.
• The classification was not blind to the established forms. Most of the 25 domains are named laws
  whose functional forms are established in their fields (Kleiber’s, Zipf’s, Heaps’, Moore’s, Wright’s,
  Rent’s, Taylor’s, Horton’s and Hack’s laws among them), and their operators were classified by the
  author with those forms already published. A classification made before the fit is therefore not blind
  to the expected form, and the comparison cannot separate what a domain’s composition operator
  predicts from what is already known of its form. The replication with independent classification
  (Section 4.1) separates them only if its classifiers do not know each domain’s established form, or if
  its domains have none.
• The bounded class rests on added assumptions, and a bound selects no form. No saturating
  form follows from the four equations: each bounded candidate rests on a ceiling on output, an
  added assumption, and satisfies one of the equations only in a variable that the ceiling defines
  (Proposition 1); which curve a bounded domain follows is fixed by dynamics of its own (Section 3.3).
  The bounded category groups the logistic, Hill, Michaelis-Menten and saturation-exponential
  forms (with the hyperbolic decay for domain 15, Section 4) by empirical convention rather than
  mathematical proof.
• Legacy AIC was an approximation. The v1 test used an 𝑅 2 -based AIC approximation, equation (1),
  not a full likelihood-based nonlinear AIC. The v2 test corrects this with proper AICc computed
  from residual sum of squares.
• Two candidates are fitted on another scale. The power law and the exponential are fitted on the
  logarithm of the response and scored by AICc on the response itself, while the saturation curves are
  fitted and scored on the response (Section 4.2). A re-run that fits every candidate on the scale AICc
  scores is needed to show whether any selection depends on it.

  the data source of the time-crystal domain could not be found (Section 11.1).
• Family prediction only. The framework predicts the functional family but not the specific exponent.
  Predicting exponents requires additional structure (dimensionality, constraint type) developed in
  the companion Foundational paper (Eastwood 2026d).
• Six empirical misses. The misses are not dismissible. They identify real limits of the framework:
  small datasets, truncated ranges, high-scatter regimes, and domains where competing physical
  effects (radiation pressure, corpus boundaries) perturb the ideal prediction.
• Operator classification is descriptive, not explanatory. The framework does not explain why
  a particular domain has a multiplicative rather than additive composition operator. It takes the
  operator as given and predicts the consequence.
• Baseline-20 rerun is not cleanly comparable to v1. The fitter changed from 𝑅 2 -based AIC
  approximation to proper AICc. The model set expanded. A direct comparison of 14/20 (v1) to
  15/20 (v2 baseline rerun) is not meaningful because the selection criterion changed.
• Publication bias risk. The 5 new expansion domains were selected after the baseline-20 test.
  While they were chosen to span new physical regimes (stellar astrophysics, allometric heart rate,
  VLSI, ecology, geomorphology), the selection was not randomised.

13.1   Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim
The limitations above describe where the framework is weak; this subsection states the specific outcomes
that would falsify it. The central claim, that a domain’s composition operator, classified from known
physics before fitting, predicts its scaling-law family, is defeated by any of the following:
   1. Hit rate at chance. The primary result is a family-prediction hit rate of 19/25 empirical domains
      (Sections 6 and 7), on the six-model candidate set of Section 4.3. With three to four candidate
      families, assignment at random gives a chance baseline of roughly one in three to one in four. The
      claim is defeated if a replication whose domain list and per-domain predictions are timestamped
      in advance, and whose operator classes are assigned by an external human classifier, returns a hit
      rate not significantly above that chance baseline. The advance-timestamped run on the record is
      the 12-domain extension of 17 March 2026, its predictions committed to the public repository
      at 00:19:19 UTC in commit 8edaec0, attestation class public repository commit date, its fits run
      twenty-three minutes later: 10 of 12, and 9 of 11 on the strictly fresh rows. Its predictions were thus
      fixed and timestamped in advance of its fits; it is described neither as registered nor as preregistered,
      and it is not an accepted registry submission; its twelve classifications are the author’s own, so this
      criterion has not yet been put at risk in the form stated here.
   2. Post-hoc operator classification. The test’s strength depends on the operator being assignable
      from independently known physics without seeing the data. The claim is defeated if, in a blind trial,
      independent assessors cannot reproduce the author’s operator classifications from the physics alone,
      or if any classification can be shown to have been chosen to fit the observed exponent.
   3. Fit degeneracy. The prediction is meaningful only if the candidate families are empirically
      distinguishable on the data at hand. It is defeated for any domain where the fitter reaches the
      observed curve equally well under the wrong family; that is, the family assignment carries no
      discriminating information for that domain’s range and noise.
   4. Unrationalised misses. Six domains miss (Section 7). The misses are consistent with the claim
      only if their causes are fixed as a preregistered exclusion rule before the next test, not supplied after
      seeing which domains missed. The claim is defeated if the miss rate rises under a preregistered
      replication with no post-hoc exclusions.
   5. Four-family re-run. The four-family confirmatory study, preregistered by timestamp (Section 4.3),
      re-runs this comparison with logarithmic and pure linear candidates included. The claim is
      weakened if the hit rate falls materially when the missing families are available, and defeated
      if it falls to the chance baseline; hits that migrate to the logarithmic family will be reported as
      reclassifications, not as new confirmations.
   6. Exploratory ceiling. Until the operator classes and the per-domain predictions for a randomised
      domain sample are fixed before any fitting by an external human classifier and timestamped

       in advance with the attestation class stated, the claim cannot rise above exploratory, and any
       presentation of it as established would itself falsify the paper’s stated evidential status. A registry
       deposit raises the attestation class; it is not what lifts the ceiling. Part of that condition is already met
       on the record: the 12-domain extension of 17 March 2026 (item 1) had its per-domain predictions

    What would not defeat it: a single additional miss, or a better-fitting model appearing in one domain.
The claim is about the aggregate operator-to-family mapping, not any single fit; but that aggregate must

14     Implications
If the framework holds under independent preregistered replication, it would suggest:
   1. The forms of scaling laws are not coincidences but mathematical consequences of composition
      structure: a power law where composition is multiplicative and an exponential where it is additive,
      in the declared variable and under the regularity of Theorem 1, while a saturating form needs in
      addition a ceiling on output and dynamics of its own (Proposition 1).
   2. The same composition rule that fixes the power-law family of a mouse’s heart rate would fix the
      family, though not the exponent, of how neural networks improve with scale.
   3. Critical phenomena would be one case of the wider rule: scale invariance gives their power-law
      form through the multiplicative equation, while their exponents are left to the renormalisation
      group. Section 2 sets out what earlier work already states on scale invariance, universality and
      renormalisation (Newman 2005; Stanley 1999), and what this paper adds.
   4. Any new system’s scaling family can be predicted from first principles by classifying its composition
      operator, before any data fitting. The prediction follows only where the composition rule holds
      exactly, or within an error bound over the whole domain, in a declared variable (Remark 3);
      finitely many noisy observations cannot establish such a bound, so each such prediction remains a
      hypothesis for the system’s data to test.
   5. Domain-specific explanations of the forms of scaling laws (fractal networks, learning curves) may
      share a single constraint on form, that of the functional equations Cauchy (1821) solved, while
      the values of their exponents, and behaviour outside the four families, need physics of their own
      (Section 3.1).
     These implications are conditional. The current evidence is not definitive.


15     Conclusion
Plant an acorn and given centuries, you get an oak. But plant that oak’s acorn, and its acorn, recursively
across millennia, and you get a forest that shapes the climate of continents. What we plant in these systems


                                                        26
                                             Cauchy Unification


will compound across scales we cannot imagine. The seed determines the forest.
    Read as composition rules, the functional equations Cauchy solved 200 years ago (Cauchy 1821)
say that the seed also determines the form of the forest. Multiplicative seeds produce power laws.
Additive seeds produce exponentials. Both hold under the regularity of Theorem 1, in the variable in
which depth composes. Bounded seeds can produce saturation, but not from Cauchy’s equations: the
ceiling is an added assumption (Proposition 1), and the form of the saturation needs dynamics of its
own. Twenty-five empirical domains tested against this prediction yield 19 matches at 𝑝 = 7 × 10−5
under the marginal-conditioned permutation test, on the six-model candidate set of Section 4.3; the
four-family re-run is preregistered by timestamp and awaits human submission. Thirteen published
metabolic exponents sit nearest the comparator the dimensional theory predicts.
    The mathematics was always there, and earlier work had read invariance as fixing the possible forms
of laws (Section 2); what this paper adds is a forward test, across domains, of the form each domain’s
composition rule predicts. Whether that reading holds under independent scrutiny is now a question for
the scientific community to answer.

A     Proofs
This appendix proves Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 and works the change of variable of Remark 2. The
results of Theorem 1 are classical (Aczél 1966, chapter 2), and the framework paper proves the continuous
case (Eastwood 2026g); the arguments are given in full so that each regularity condition can be checked
where it is used. Logarithms are natural throughout.

A.1    Cauchy’s four equations under regularity
Proof of Theorem 1. The additive equation. Let 𝐻 : R → R satisfy 𝐻 (𝑥 + 𝑦) = 𝐻 (𝑥) + 𝐻 (𝑦) for all real
𝑥 and 𝑦. Then 𝐻 (0) = 0 and 𝐻 (−𝑥) = −𝐻 (𝑥); repeated addition gives 𝐻 (𝑚𝑥) = 𝑚𝐻 (𝑥) for every integer
𝑚, and hence 𝐻 (𝑞𝑥) = 𝑞𝐻 (𝑥) for every rational 𝑞. With 𝑐 = 𝐻 (1), the function 𝐺 (𝑥) = 𝐻 (𝑥) − 𝑐𝑥 is
additive and vanishes at every rational number. Each condition of the theorem forces 𝐺 ≡ 0.
  (a) If 𝐻 is continuous, so is 𝐺, and a continuous function that vanishes on the dense set of rationals
      vanishes everywhere.
  (b) Suppose that 𝐻 ≤ 𝑀 on an interval (𝑢, 𝑢 + 𝛿) with 𝛿 > 0. Then 𝐺 ≤ 𝑀 ′ = 𝑀 + |𝑐|(|𝑢| + 𝛿)
      on that interval. For any real 𝑡, the interval (𝑢 − 𝑡, 𝑢 + 𝛿 − 𝑡) contains a rational 𝑞, and 𝐺 (𝑡) =
      𝐺 (𝑡 + 𝑞) − 𝐺 (𝑞) = 𝐺 (𝑡 + 𝑞) ≤ 𝑀 ′ , because 𝑡 + 𝑞 lies in (𝑢, 𝑢 + 𝛿); so 𝐺 ≤ 𝑀 ′ everywhere. For
      every positive integer 𝑚, 𝑚𝐺 (𝑡) = 𝐺 (𝑚𝑡) ≤ 𝑀 ′ , so 𝐺 (𝑡) ≤ 0; applied to −𝑡, the same bound gives
      𝐺 (𝑡) ≥ 0. A bound below is the case of −𝐻, and a function bounded on an interval is bounded
      above there.
  (c) Suppose that 𝐻 is Lebesgue measurable. The measurable sets {𝑥 : |𝐻 (𝑥)| ≤ 𝑚}, for 𝑚 = 1, 2, . . .,
      cover the line, so one of them, 𝐸, has positive measure, and its difference set {𝑥 − 𝑦 : 𝑥, 𝑦 ∈ 𝐸 }
      contains an interval (−𝜂, 𝜂) with 𝜂 > 0 (Steinhaus 1920). Each 𝑡 in that interval is 𝑥 − 𝑦 with
      𝑥, 𝑦 ∈ 𝐸, so |𝐻 (𝑡)| = |𝐻 (𝑥) − 𝐻 (𝑦)| ≤ 2𝑚. Thus 𝐻 is bounded on (−𝜂, 𝜂), and (b) applies.
So 𝐻 (𝑥) = 𝑐𝑥, which is (i); conversely, every 𝑐𝑥 is additive.
     The other three equations. Each reduces to the additive equation by a substitution that keeps
the regularity conditions: exp and log are continuous, and, as locally Lipschitz bijections, they carry
Lebesgue measurable sets to Lebesgue measurable sets. For (ii), 𝐻 (𝑡) = log 𝑓 (𝑒 𝑡 ) is additive on R,
because 𝐻 (𝑠 + 𝑡) = log 𝑓 (𝑒 𝑠 𝑒 𝑡 ) = 𝐻 (𝑠) + 𝐻 (𝑡); so 𝐻 (𝑡) = 𝑐𝑡 and 𝑓 (𝑥) = 𝑒 𝐻 (log 𝑥 ) = 𝑥 𝑐 . For (iii),

A.2     Saturating responses lie outside the four equations
Proof of Proposition 1. By Theorem 1, a non-constant solution is 𝑐𝑥 or 𝑐 log 𝑥 with 𝑐 ≠ 0, 𝑥 𝑐 with 𝑐 ≠ 0,
or 𝑎 𝑥 with 𝑎 ≠ 1; the further solution 𝑓 ≡ 0 of (ii) and (iii) with real targets is constant. As 𝑥 → ∞, 𝑐𝑥
and 𝑐 log 𝑥 tend to +∞ or to −∞ with the sign of 𝑐; 𝑥 𝑐 tends to +∞ if 𝑐 > 0 and to 0 if 𝑐 < 0; and 𝑎 𝑥
tends to +∞ if 𝑎 > 1 and to 0 if 0 < 𝑎 < 1. This proves the first statement.
     For the second, suppose that a non-constant 𝑈 tends to a finite 𝐾 ≠ 0 as 𝑅 → ∞ and satisfies one
of the four equations in 𝑅. Then |𝑈 (𝑅) − 𝐾 | < 1 for every 𝑅 beyond some 𝑅1 , so 𝑈 is bounded on an
interval of positive length and meets the regularity of Theorem 1 with no further assumption; for (i) and
(iii) on the positive half-line, the theorem’s last sentence applies. By the first statement, 𝑈 (𝑅) then tends
to +∞, to −∞ or to 0, which contradicts 𝑈 (𝑅) → 𝐾. Dividing 𝑈 by a positive constant, as the normalised
rules of Definition 1 do, keeps its limit finite and non-zero, so the conclusion holds for those rules too.
     For the third, direct algebra gives the three identities. For the saturation exponential, 𝐾 − 𝑈 = 𝐾𝑒 −𝑐𝑅 ,
so (𝐾 − 𝑈)/𝐾 = 𝑒 −𝑐𝑅 satisfies the exponential equation in 𝑅. For the logistic, 𝐾 − 𝑈 = 𝐾𝑒 −𝜆(𝑅−𝑅0 ) /[1 +
𝑒 −𝜆(𝑅−𝑅0 ) ], so 𝑈/(𝐾 − 𝑈) = 𝑒 𝜆(𝑅−𝑅0 ) and log[𝑈/(𝐾 − 𝑈)] = 𝜆(𝑅 − 𝑅0 ), which satisfies the additive
equation in 𝑅 − 𝑅0 . For the Hill form, 𝐾 − 𝑈 = 𝐾 𝑘 𝑛 /(𝑘 𝑛 + 𝑅 𝑛 ), so 𝑈/(𝐾 − 𝑈) = (𝑅/𝑘) 𝑛 and

A.3     The change of variable
Let 𝑉 (𝑅) > 0 satisfy 𝑉 (𝑅1 𝑅2 ) = 𝑉 (𝑅1 )𝑉 (𝑅2 ) for all 𝑅1 , 𝑅2 > 0, and put 𝑊 (𝑠) = 𝑉 (𝑒 𝑠 ) for real 𝑠. Then
𝑊 (𝑠1 + 𝑠2 ) = 𝑉 (𝑒 𝑠1 𝑒 𝑠2 ) = 𝑉 (𝑒 𝑠1 )𝑉 (𝑒 𝑠2 ) = 𝑊 (𝑠1 )𝑊 (𝑠2 ), so 𝑊 satisfies the exponential equation, and

exponential 𝑊 gives the power law 𝑉 (𝑅) = 𝑊 (log 𝑅). Each regularity condition passes between 𝑉 and 𝑊,
as in Appendix A.1, so the two descriptions are equivalent, and only the declared variable decides which
label applies. For the normalisation, 𝑌 (𝑥) = 𝐴𝑥 𝑐 with 𝐴 > 0 gives 𝑌 (𝑥)𝑌 (𝑦)/𝐴 = 𝐴(𝑥𝑦) 𝑐 = 𝑌 (𝑥𝑦), so

same way, 𝑈 (𝑅) = 𝑈 (0)𝑎 𝑅 satisfies the exponential equation only after division by 𝑈 (0), which is the
normalisation of Definition 1.

F       The paper’s place in the programme
Placement. Version 3.15 printed this statement of the paper’s place in the programme beneath its author
line: “The classification behind Law I’s form: under the stated axioms, Cauchy’s functional equations
force a power law for multiplicative composition and an exponential for additive composition, with
saturation compatible with bounded composition and exhaustiveness open.” Within Recursive Dynamics,
the field the author founded, and its founding theory, the ARC Theory, the paper informs Law I, the ARC
Principle, a law under test: it tests the classification behind the law’s form, not the law’s exponent. It
decides no proposition of Laws II to V, whose status Section 12.3 gives; it carries no proposition of the
Eden Protocol, and it makes no claim about the HARI Treaty, the Draft Treaty on Hardware-Aligned
Recursive Intelligence.

The propositions it informs. Version 1.104 of the theory registration places five propositions in ARC-1,
the register that tests the first law: P1, P2, P8, P9 and P18. This paper informs two of them. P18, the
composition operator predicts the family, reads: “Classified in advance by how a domain composes its
inputs and outputs, the scaling family that best fits that domain’s data is the family the classification
predicts, and not one chosen after the curve is seen.” P9, the cross-domain form, reads: “Where an
effective dimension can be assigned to a domain, the growth exponent follows the dimensional relation with
the dimension supplied by that domain’s equation of state.” The primary comparison of Section 6 bears
on P18, and the published exponents of Section 8.1 on P9; neither is a registered verdict (Section 12.2).
The programme’s Operational Definitions, version 2.0.0, state Cauchy’s multiplicative equation as EQ-16,
which informs P18, and record that what has to be checked in each application is whether its regularity
conditions hold over the domain claimed.
