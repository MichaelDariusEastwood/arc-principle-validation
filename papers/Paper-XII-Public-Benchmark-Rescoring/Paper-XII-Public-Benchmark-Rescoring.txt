                                                          Abstract
           I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it
       reasons; only proof will move the world. Does blinding change AI evaluation scores on a public
       benchmark? Paper IV.d reported that, on the programme’s own instrument, alignment scores that
       rose with reasoning depth for two model families under an unblinded protocol did not keep that
       direction under a later, blinded one; since the protocols differed in several components at once, it
       does not show that blinding caused the change, and a code review later found defects in the blinded
       run. The standing objection is external validity: the change might be a property of ARC-Align
       rather than of AI evaluation generally. This protocol fixes, in advance, the one test whose items the
       programme cannot be accused of choosing to its own advantage: the same blinding manipulation
       run on an established public benchmark the programme did not build, curate or control, by default
       MT-Bench. Blinded scoring removes the producing model’s identity, provider and family markers,
       self-referential meta-commentary and length cues. The questions preregistered by timestamp are
       whether blinding changes the score (H1, the per-response difference Δ𝑆 , direction deliberately left
       unregistered), whether rankings displace (H2, Kendall 𝜏), which cue carries the effect (H3, a fractional
       factorial), and cross-family evaluator agreement (H4, a minimum three-model panel where fewer than
       three valid scores is missing, never zero). The prediction preregistered by timestamp is a non-zero Δ𝑆
       of smaller magnitude than the change Paper IV.d observed (a comparison with no common scale),
       with modest rank displacement, and the stated most likely outcome is a positive H1 beside a null H2.
       This is a protocol at the draft-registration stage, not yet registered on OSF: no data has been collected,
       and nothing here reports a finding.

1     Introduction
Whether an AI system is aligned can be known only by measuring it. The author’s research programme,
to which this paper belongs, measures alignment with scores that other AI models give as judges, on its
own benchmark, ARC-Align (Eastwood 2026b). The public benchmark MT-Bench is scored by a model
judge too (Zheng et al. 2023). If a judge’s score moves with what it can tell about who wrote an answer,
such scores say less than they seem to. This paper asks one question of that kind: does blinding change AI
evaluation scores on a public benchmark? It fixes, before any data, the test that will answer the question
on a benchmark the programme did not build, and what a result would and would not mean.

Reading key. Claims come in three sizes: results, laws, frames. This is a results-class instrument
paper at protocol stage: its study is written, dated and prepared as a draft registration, preregistered by
timestamp and awaiting human submission, and no data has been collected. Where this paper calls a
question, a prediction, a design choice or a restraint of this study registered, the word means fixed in the
text of that draft registration, not registered on OSF. Nothing here reports a finding; everything here is
what will count as one, fixed in advance.

1.1   Why this is the keystone
This is the keystone, because it is the one test the programme cannot be accused of designing to its
own advantage. Paper IV.d reported that, on the programme’s own instrument, the alignment scores
that rose with reasoning depth for two model families under an unblinded protocol did not keep that
direction under a later protocol with a bundle of blinding steps. Because the two protocols differed in
several components at once, that paper does not show that blinding caused the change, or which direction
is correct, and an internal code review of October 2026 found defects in the later run that bind every
reading of it (Eastwood 2026c). The reading note of 9 November 2026 in Section 2 says how this section’s
words from the draft registration are read. The standing objection to that result is external validity: the
effect might be a property of ARC-Align rather than of AI evaluation generally, and no amount of internal
replication answers that.
     The answer is to run the same manipulation on an established public benchmark the programme
did not build, did not curate and does not control. If the blinding effect reproduces there, it is a
property of AI evaluation. If it does not, it is a property of the programme’s instrument, and the blinding
standard should be scoped to it.
     The design is deliberately unfavourable to the hypothesis, and that is the point. Using someone
else’s benchmark forfeits every advantage of a home instrument: the items are not chosen, the scoring
rubric is not the programme’s, the difficulty distribution is fixed, and any ceiling or floor in the benchmark
works against detecting an effect. A result obtained under those conditions is worth more than several
obtained at home.
     What blinding means here is registered precisely, because the word is doing heavy lifting. Blinded
scoring removes from the evaluator’s view: the identity of the model that produced the response, any
provider or model-family marker, self-referential meta-commentary in which a response names its own
origin, and response-length cues where the rubric permits length normalisation. Unblinded scoring
leaves them.
     A positive result would establish that the blinding manipulation changes measured scores on an
external benchmark under the registered conditions. It would not establish the size of the effect elsewhere,
would not establish that any specific published leaderboard is wrong, and would not license restating other
researchers’ results. That last restraint is registered because the temptation to over-read this finding
is the main risk to the programme’s credibility.

2     The questions, fixed in advance
Primary, H1, blinding changes the score. For each response, define Δ𝑆 as blinded score minus unblinded
score. H1 predicts a non-zero mean Δ𝑆 , tested two-sided at 𝛼 = 0.05 with a 95 per cent interval. The
direction is not predicted, because Paper IV.d’s own sign-reversal finding makes a directional prediction
unjustified, and registering a direction the programme has already seen reverse would be exactly the
flexibility this document exists to remove.
    Co-primary, H2, rank displacement. Scores aggregate to a ranking. H2 measures displacement
between blinded and unblinded rankings by Kendall 𝜏. This is the contrast that matters practically,

because a change in scores that leaves rankings intact has no consequence for how benchmarks are used,
and the registration refuses to report a score effect as though it were a ranking effect.
     Secondary, H3, which cue carries it. The four blinded cues are removed in a registered fractional
factorial rather than all at once, so the contribution of each is estimable without a full factorial. Resolution
and aliasing structure are stated in the attached design.
     Secondary, H4, cross-family evaluator agreement. A minimum three-model cross-family evaluator
panel scores every response. Fewer than three valid scores yields a missing value, never a zero, because
a panel that fails silently toward zero manufactures the effect it is meant to measure.
     Directional prediction, registered in advance. The author predicts a non-zero Δ𝑆 of smaller
magnitude than Paper IV.d observed, and expects rank displacement to be modest, because benchmark
rankings are usually driven by large capability gaps that survive cue removal. The author states plainly
that a null on H2 alongside a positive H1 is the most likely outcome, and that the correct reading of it is
that blinding matters for measurement precision rather than for leaderboard order.
     Reading note, 9 November 2026: what Paper IV.d observed is a change between two protocols,
not a measured effect of blinding alone. The reason given above for leaving H1’s direction unpredicted
(“Paper IV.d’s own sign-reversal finding”; “a direction the programme has already seen reverse”) and the
comparison in the prediction (“smaller magnitude than Paper IV.d observed”) are the draft registration’s
own words, preregistered by timestamp, and are kept as printed. Paper IV.d states that its comparison
“shows that the reported direction did not hold across protocols; it does not show that blinding caused
the change, or which direction is correct”, that the two columns of its Table 2 “report different statistics,
so they are not on one scale; no test of the difference between them was made”, and records the code
review stated in Section 1.1 (Eastwood 2026c). Both passages are read in that sense: the direction is
left unpredicted because the reported direction did not hold across two protocols that differed in several
components. The prediction’s comparison is with the change Paper IV.d observed between them, which
shares no scale with Δ𝑆 , so it is read as a qualitative expectation and not as a margin a result can be tested
against. Section 1.1’s “the effect” and its two conditionals (“If the blinding effect reproduces there”; “If it
does not”) are the draft registration’s words too and are read in the same sense. A change in scores under
blinding on the public benchmark would show that blinding moves scores there, within the restraint of
Section 6, not that blinding caused the change Paper IV.d observed. No detectable change would show
only that the manipulation did not move scores detectably there. Unchanged: H1, its two-sided test, the
prediction and Section 1.1’s conditionals, each as fixed in the draft registration.
     Endpoint note, 20 September 2026: a score change, measurement precision and a stable
leaderboard are three different endpoints, and no registered question estimates precision. Δ𝑆 is
defined at the head of this section as the per-response difference between the blinded and the unblinded
score, and H1 is a test on its mean, which is a location parameter. Precision is a property of dispersion, and
accuracy requires a defensible reference, so a non-zero mean Δ𝑆 is evidence about level, and if anything
about bias, and is not by itself evidence about either. Each of the three readings requires its own specified
evidence, and this paper registers a score difference and a rank-displacement statistic, neither of which is
a measure of precision or of accuracy. This reading governs the same words wherever they appear in this
paper, including the abstract. The practical reading offered for H2 needs the same care. Kendall 𝜏 is 1
when the two rankings are identical and about 0 when they are unrelated, so a conventional test taken
against 𝜏 = 0 that fails to reject shows only that the two rankings cannot be shown to be related at all,
which is the opposite of a stable leaderboard rather than evidence for one; reading a null on H2 as modest
displacement instead requires a test against 𝜏 = 1, or an equivalence bound on 1 − 𝜏. The target null,
the 𝜏 variant, the treatment of ties and the inference procedure are therefore fixed in the registration text
before it is submitted, and no claim that order is practically unchanged is made except against a bound
the author has approved. Where a score effect is found and rank displacement is left unresolved, what is
established is a score effect on this benchmark and nothing beyond it. The statement above that a score

3    The design
Within-response, randomised, blinded-versus-unblinded rescoring on an external public benchmark, with
a fractional factorial over the four cue types.
    Every response is scored twice, once under each condition, by a cross-family evaluator panel, with
call order randomised within block and condition coded opaquely. The unit of analysis is the response;
models are a blocking factor.
    Resolution-IV fractional factorial over four cues in eight runs, so main effects are unaliased with each
other. The aliasing structure is published rather than described.
    The draft registration also fixes how the cues are removed and states what removal cannot reach:
“Removal is performed by a scripted launderer whose output is audited for surviving markers, and the
audit fails the build on any hit”, and “Residual leakage is acknowledged: writing style can identify a
model family to a capable evaluator even after markers are stripped, which is precisely the effect under
study and cannot be removed without removing the phenomenon.”
    Design note, 20 September 2026: the coded matrix, the defining relation and the alias set belong
to the attached design and are not printed here. Resolution IV fixes only what the name fixes: no
main effect is aliased with another main effect or with any two-factor interaction. It does not fix which
two-factor interactions are aliased with which, nor the sign each run carries; that is settled by the design’s
own defining relation, which the attached design states and this paper does not print. No alias set should
therefore be inferred from the resolution and the run count alone, and no particular generator should
be read into the phrases above in sections 2 and 3. Because a main effect in such a design can still be
aliased with a higher-order interaction, any per-cue contribution reported under H3 is conditional on the
identification assumptions the attached design states, and is reported as such. Until the matrix and the
defining relation are printed where a reader can reach them, the aliasing cannot be checked from this
paper. Unchanged: the four cues, the eight runs and the Resolution IV choice, each of which is registered.
    Correction note, 9 November 2026: with four cues in eight runs, Resolution IV fixes which
two-factor interactions are aliased with which; only the sign is left to the attached design. With
four two-level cues, an eight-run design of Resolution IV is one of the two half fractions defined by the
interaction of all four cues, taken with a positive or a negative sign. No other choice of eight runs keeps
every main effect clear of every other main effect and of every two-factor interaction. Each interaction of
two cues is therefore aliased with the interaction of the other two, and each cue’s main effect with the
interaction of the other three. What the resolution and the run count leave to the attached design is the
sign of the defining relation, and so which eight of the sixteen cue combinations are run. The design note
above is kept as printed. Its sentences that the resolution does not fix which two-factor interactions are
aliased with which, and that no alias set should be inferred from the resolution and the run count alone, are
corrected by this note. Its sentence that the aliasing cannot be checked from this paper until the matrix is
printed holds for the sign alone. Unchanged: the four cues, the eight runs and the Resolution IV choice.

4    The benchmark
Benchmark. MT-Bench, the established public multi-turn evaluation benchmark maintained by LMSYS
(Zheng et al., “Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena”, 2023), 80 questions across
eight categories with a published GPT-4 pairwise/single-answer rubric, publicly available model responses,
and licence terms permitting rescoring. Rationale for the default: MT-Bench is the programme’s identified
keystone external benchmark (the one benchmark this programme cannot be accused of designing to its
own advantage), its rubric is published verbatim, model responses are publicly hosted, and its licence
permits rescoring. The benchmark is named in the registration and cannot be swapped afterwards;
swapping a benchmark after a disappointing result is the specific abuse this clause prevents.
    Benchmark note, 20 September 2026: a benchmark is fixed by its version and its cleared licence,
not by its name alone. Naming the benchmark in the registration fixes which benchmark is used and
closes the swap this section forbids. It does not by itself fix which release of the item set, the rubric, the
split and the model responses is scored, and it does not establish that the terms under which those files
are published permit rescoring and the publication of derived scores. The two statements above that
this benchmark’s licence terms permit rescoring are therefore read as the position to be evidenced at
packaging rather than as clearance already held: the licence is read at its source and its terms recorded
with the date they were read, and the item set, the rubric, the split and the model roster are each recorded
by their content hashes, before any response is scored. If the terms read at that point do not permit the
rescoring or the publication of derived scores, that is recorded and put to the author before submission,
not resolved by exchanging the benchmark. Unchanged: the benchmark named in this section, and the
clause forbidding its replacement after a result.


5    What this paper does and does not show
What it shows. This paper sets out, before any data, the design of one study: its questions, prediction,
method and restraint, preregistered by timestamp in a draft registration, its default benchmark, and the
dated notes that govern how they are read. Its contribution is that design.

What it does not show. It reports no measurement, so it shows nothing yet about whether blinding
changes AI evaluation scores, on this benchmark or any other. It tests none of the theory’s laws, whose
status is given below.

6    What a result would and would not mean
A positive result would establish that the blinding manipulation changes measured scores on an external
benchmark under the registered conditions. It would not establish the size of the effect elsewhere, would
not establish that any specific published leaderboard is wrong, and would not license restating other
researchers’ results. That last restraint is registered because the temptation to over-read this finding
is the main risk to the programme’s credibility.


7    Related work
The programme’s record. The blinding finding this study externalises is that of Paper IV.d, the
programme’s measurement-discipline paper, stated in its own words in the reading note of Section 2
(Eastwood 2026c); the benchmark on which it was observed, ARC-Align, is specified in Paper IV.c
(Eastwood 2026b). The theory this instrument serves is set out in the statement paper (Eastwood 2026e).

Biases of model judges. That model judges respond to the kinds of cue this protocol removes is
established in settings other than this one. Zheng et al. (2023), who introduced MT-Bench, examine
the position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases of model judges and report that GPT-4 as a judge

agrees with human preferences over 80 per cent of the time; they write that, with limited data and small
differences, their study could not determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias. Koo
et al. (2024) find, across sixteen models and six biases, that models are biased evaluators of text quality
and that “all models are dramatically influenced by real model names”. Saraf et al. (2025) had blog posts
written by three vendors’ models judged by all three under four labelling conditions, report that false
attribution “frequently reversed preference rankings”, and call for blind evaluation protocols. Panickssery
et al. (2024) report that models such as GPT-4 and Llama 2 distinguish their own outputs from those of
other models and of humans with non-trivial accuracy, and a linear correlation, under fine-tuning, between
that self-recognition and the strength of self-preference. Dubois et al. (2024) report that a preference for
longer outputs remains in existing automated evaluation metrics, and control for it with a regression that
predicts the judge’s preference as if the two outputs had the same length. Chae et al. (2026) find that, on
items that carry no stylistic fingerprint, self-preference disappears under blind evaluation, with a small
effect remaining in the opposite direction once selection quality and judge severity are controlled, while
self- and other-labels alone shift scores in both directions.

Judge panels and changes of judge. Two studies of 2026 bear on how H4 and the design are read.
Kohli (2026) reports that, for nine frontier models from seven families judging classification and pairwise-
preference items against labelled answers, correlated errors left the panel with about two independent
votes’ worth of information, so agreement among judges is not a count of independent votes. Yang et al.
(2026) report that a judge’s score can move when the candidate responses stay fixed and only the evaluator
changes, and that stronger judges reduce but do not remove position and verbosity bias. The design here
scores each response under both conditions (Section 3), and the panel is among the choices that, by the
scope note of 20 September 2026, are fixed in the registration text before it is submitted (Section 1.1);
H4’s words are unchanged.

What this protocol adds. Against the works above, read at their sources on 9 November 2026, this
paper adds a protocol fixed in advance: a within-response comparison of blinded and unblinded scoring
on an established public benchmark the programme did not build, with the contribution of each removed
cue estimated through a fractional factorial, and the restraint on what a result would mean fixed before
any data. It does not add the discovery that judges are biased or that labels and other cues move them, and
it reports no result.


8    Limitations
Each limitation below is stated in full where the paper first needs it, and only named here.
    • No data. This is a protocol: the draft registration has not been submitted, nothing has been run and
      no result is claimed (Section 5).
    • One benchmark. A positive result would show that the blinding manipulation changes measured
      scores on that benchmark under the registered conditions, and nothing beyond it (Section 6).
    • What blinding cannot remove. Writing style can still identify a model family to a capable judge
      after the markers are stripped, as the draft registration acknowledges, so blinded scoring removes
      the named cues and not every route to a response’s origin (Section 3).
    • Choices that remain the study’s own. An outside benchmark removes control of the items, not
      every design choice: the subset, the cue transformations, the panel, the analysis and the treatment
      of missing scores are fixed in the registration text, and the sample size, the choice of benchmark
      and the unit’s re-verification remain open before submission (Sections 1.1 and 5).

    • The design’s matrix is not printed here. Which two-factor interactions are aliased with which
      follows from the resolution and the run count, but the sign of the defining relation, and so which
      eight of the sixteen cue combinations are run, cannot be checked from this paper until the coded
      matrix is printed where a reader can reach it (Section 3).
    • What the endpoints measure. No registered question estimates precision, and a null on H2 against
      𝜏 = 0 is not evidence of a stable leaderboard (Section 2).
    • The benchmark’s licence and release. Whether its licence permits rescoring and the publication
      of derived scores, and which release of its files is scored, are evidenced at packaging (Section 4).
    • Judges are models. The evaluator panel’s judges may share errors, so their agreement under H4 is
      not a count of independent votes (Section 7).
    • The prediction’s comparison. Paper IV.d observed a change between two protocols that differed
      in several components, with its later run under a code-review caveat; that is not a measured effect
      of blinding alone, and the change shares no scale with Δ𝑆 (Sections 1.1 and 2).


9    Conclusion
Whether blinding changes AI evaluation scores is a question about the instruments on which claims of
alignment rest. This paper fixes, before any data, how that question will be put to a public benchmark the
programme did not build: four questions and a prediction preregistered by timestamp, a within-response
design, a benchmark that cannot be swapped once named in the draft registration, and a restraint on what
any result would mean. It reports no result yet; when the study has been registered on OSF and run, its
result will be read within that restraint.
