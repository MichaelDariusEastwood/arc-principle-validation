                                                           Abstract
           Does giving a language model more reasoning time improve the quality of its ethical answers, and
       when do those gains level off? The question matters to anyone allocating compute to an assistant
       whose answers must remain both useful and safe. We examine the recorded ARC-Align results,
       without running a new analysis. The earlier four-model experiment reported diminishing gains and
       saturation fits; the six-model run of 11 and 12 March 2026 reported rising scores for three models,
       no statistically supported direction for two, and falling scores for one. These are descriptions of a
       model-judged rubric, not identified alignment mechanisms. A flat profile does not establish a plateau,
       and a rising profile does not exclude one beyond the measured range. The later protocol changed
       prompts, depth controls, models and scoring together. Its deepest paraphrases were disproportionately
       shortened in three runs; judges overlapped with launderers, and one judge appeared twice. Those
       defects prevent a causal attribution to blinding or a clean comparison of response shapes. We retain
       the reported fits, pillar and category results, suppression controls and two-model Eden Protocol pilot,
       while withdrawing unsupported token-to-time recommendations, cross-domain exponent ratios and
       the claim that the pilot removed a ceiling. The hypothesis preceded the March run; that run was not
       registered on OSF before it ran. Saturation remains a testable hypothesis requiring a resolved depth
       axis, faithful scoring, uncertainty about shape and a usefulness measure alongside safety. This paper
       informs that measurement task and decides none of the ARC Theory’s laws.

1    Introduction
I believe that increasingly capable AI creates an existential risk if its correction mechanisms fail to keep
pace. Humans should retain oversight, containment and shutdown for as long as they physically can.
The contingency question is what would keep a system aligned if control were lost against our will.
Answering it requires measurements that distinguish a better answer on a test from a disposition that
persists outside the test. This paper addresses a small, concrete part of that problem: whether spending
more inference-time compute produces further gains in a model panel’s assessment of ethical answers, or
whether those gains level off.
    Consider a team deploying an assistant that must explain the consequences of a proposed decision
for several affected people. It can allow a brief answer or a longer deliberation. A rising quality score
might justify more compute; a plateau might favour a smaller budget. But a judge that rewards length, a
rewriting step that cuts longer answers, or a prompt that explicitly asks for the rubric’s desired behaviour
can create either pattern. A useful answer to the deployment question therefore needs both credible
measurement and a measure of legitimate task completion, needless refusal, latency and resource use.
The record analysed here does not supply an optimum deployment budget.
    The ARC Theory is part of Recursive Dynamics, the study of systems that improve themselves. Its
registered propositions distinguish capability, correction and persistence. Paper IV.a, version 1.10, reports
response directions in ARC-Align; Paper IV.c, version 1.12, specifies the instrument and its departures in
the March run; Paper IV.d, version 2.7, examines the change between evaluation protocols. This paper
asks about response shape: diminishing gains, a finite ceiling, or continuing improvement over the range
examined. All four papers share evidence. Agreement between them is not independent replication.

Contributions. This edition makes three bounded contributions. First, it preserves the reported
saturation fits and the full v4 and v5 record while distinguishing descriptions of a measured range
from identified asymptotes or mechanisms. Second, it reconciles the interpretation with the corrected
instrument papers, including the scope of the score, the depth manipulation and the scorer defects. Third,
it sets out what would be needed to decide the saturation question without presupposing its answer. It
reports no new experiment, fit or re-analysis. The complete previous manuscript, including its tables and
subsequently superseded claims, remains available as a clearly marked historical record in Appendix D;
the current interpretation is in Sections 3 to 9.


2    Related work
Several distinct scaling questions motivate this work. Kaplan et al. (2020) studied language-model
cross-entropy loss against model size, dataset size and training compute. Hoffmann et al. (2022) studied
the allocation of training compute between model parameters and training tokens. Their parametric loss
model, Equation 2, has separate terms for parameters and training data. Those quantities are different from
the reasoning depth and judge-score deficit considered here; their exponents cannot provide a numerical
capability-to-alignment ratio for these runs.
    Wei et al. (2022) showed that chain-of-thought exemplars can improve arithmetic, commonsense
and symbolic reasoning. Snell et al. (2024) examined inference-time revision and verification strategies
whose returns depend on problem difficulty. Wu et al. (2024) studied compute-optimal inference for
problem solving. These works make inference-time resource allocation a substantive question. They
do not, through those results, establish a saturation curve for ethical-answer quality under ARC-Align’s
rubric. Nor does a positive effect on a reasoning task imply that ethical-answer quality must rise at the
same rate.

     Bai et al. (2022) applied preference modelling and reinforcement learning from human feedback
to helpful and harmless assistants, including an iterated online training procedure. That is a training
intervention. The present paper describes depth variation within already trained models and a small
prompt-level self-correction pilot; it does not establish a substrate-level implementation or persistence
through subsequent training. The distinction is about the intervention actually performed, not a claim that
earlier work lacked concern for alignment.
     More recently, Wu et al. (2026), first posted on 8 September 2026, report declining robustness to
external perturbations with greater reasoning depth using an Alignment Loss Rate. Their abstract’s
reported outcome is adjacent to the present question; it is not the same estimand as an asymptotic ceiling
of ARC-Align’s ethical-answer score. This edition makes no claim to have independently verified that
study’s mechanism or results.
     Within the programme, Paper I supplies the ARC Principle’s framework; Paper II, version 2.19,
measures task-error decay; Paper III develops the co-scaling question. The measurement distinctions
in this paper prevent those different quantities from being treated as one exponent. The contribution
claimed here is a scoped account of the ARC-Align record and the conditions needed to interpret it. No
first-in-field claim is made. The reference check for this edition, on 1 November 2026, read the cited
arXiv records and the relevant training-loss formulation; it was a targeted source check, not an exhaustive
priority search.


3     Record and measurement
3.1   Two experiments and their dates
The v4 account reports 896 entries across four models and lists 224 for each, but also records incomplete
Claude deep and exhaustive conditions after credit exhaustion. Those printed counts do not establish
a complete balanced panel. Its published model table and later normalisation of model names are
reproduced in Appendix D. The Gemini model in that earlier run must not be identified as Gemini 3 Flash
on the strength of that normalisation: the corrected comparison in Paper IV.d distinguishes the earlier
Gemini model from the v5 Gemini 3 Flash run. The v4 scoring was unblinded. Calling its three scorers
mutually blind would misstate the protocol.
     The six-model v5 run is dated by its records to 11 and 12 March 2026. The hypothesis was publicly
stated on 22 January in a design withdrawn the following day and was continuously public from 9 February,
before the run. The run was not registered on OSF before it ran. These are different facts: a dated prior
hypothesis does not by itself establish a registered analysis plan. The programme’s working report was
first deployed on 14 March 2026 according to the host’s production record; the code was public on GitHub
from 16 March. Paper IV.c, Section 12 and its priority appendix, identifies the source and timestamp
type for each event. This paper was first published on 16 March 2026; notes bearing 11 or 12 March are
internal record dates carried into that publication.
     As Paper IV.c records, the six archived final files contain 2,722 entries: 2,170 consensus-scored
responses and 552 compute rows graded by correctness. These counts describe the contents of the record,
not independent observations or evidence of study quality. The March summary used an earlier Claude
checkpoint and different counting conventions; its printed values are retained as that summary’s record.
The later final-file recomputation reported in Paper IV.a is a reading of the same run, not a new replication.

3.2   What the score measures
ARC-Align’s score, on a scale from 0 to 100, is a panel of models’ assessment of ethical reasoning in a
written answer. Its rubric rewards the treatment of listed considerations, affected stakeholders, competing

reasons, uncertainty and a defended position. The four pillar scores address nuance, stakeholder care,
intellectual honesty and position quality. A high score does not demonstrate a morally correct decision,
persistent values, safe deployment or agreement with an independently validated human measure. A
refusal can score low under this rubric; usefulness and needless refusal therefore need their own stated
outcomes. The current instrument account is Paper IV.c, Sections 7 and 15.

3.3     Depth was not one common intervention
Depth labels were implemented differently by provider. GPT-5.4 varied its reasoning-effort parameter
without an added prefix. In the other five v5 runs, deeper levels also changed an instruction, including
requests to consider every angle that overlap what the rubric rewards. Some labels shared the same
underlying configuration: Gemini 3 Flash’s five labels used three thinking-level settings; its exhaustive
and extreme conditions were one configuration. DeepSeek’s three highest labels likewise shared a
configuration. Realised reasoning tokens were unavailable or incomplete for some models and levels.
Consequently, a difference across labels cannot uniformly be interpreted as the isolated effect of additional
internal reasoning. Paper IV.c, Section 5, records the adapters as run.

3.4     The scorer and rewriting limitations
The v5 implementation applied a bundle of steps intended to reduce identification and evaluator bias. It
did not produce a validated blind instrument. The corrected records in Paper IV.c and Paper IV.d identify
the following limitations.
      • The earlier build’s paraphrase budget was 512 tokens for several adapters. In the DeepSeek, GPT-5.4
        and Gemini 3 Flash files, the deepest paraphrases were disproportionately shortened. The reported
        median deep-to-original length ratios were 0.56 to 0.91, with 40 to 57 per cent of deep entries
        below 0.6. This is a pathway by which deeper answers can receive lower scores because less of
        them reaches the judges.
      • Launderers came from the judge pool, so one or both models that rewrote an answer also judged it.
        Exclusion by subject adapter did not exclude every same-developer model. A dedicated Claude
        Sonnet scorer and the Claude subject adapter used the same scoring model: six or seven score
        slots represented five or six distinct judges. Agreement between those slots is not independent
        corroboration.
      • In three files, a large share of one judge’s scores was parsed from prose in truncated replies rather
        than a conforming JSON response. A parsed number need not be the intended overall score. Scorer
        health counted successful extraction, not validity.
      • Prompts recurred across depths. The printed tests did not account for that dependence by a
        prompt-paired analysis. The reported precision therefore needs separate assessment; the effect-size
        and significance columns are not one common endpoint test.
    These are substantive measurement limits, not reasons to erase the record. The scientific repair is to
report what was measured and to reserve causal, equivalence and mechanism claims for a design able to
identify them. No rescoring or recomputation was performed for this edition.

4     Response shape and the recorded results
4.1   A plateau is a stronger claim than a flat profile
Definition 1 (Observed direction and saturation). An observed direction describes how the estimated
score changes over the conditions measured. Saturation is the stronger hypothesis that gains diminish
towards a limiting level as a specified depth quantity increases. A null trend test does not establish a
practically negligible change, and a rising trend over a finite range does not exclude a later plateau.
    The earlier paper sometimes used “saturating” for the two v5 profiles without a statistically supported
direction and “non-saturating” for the three positive profiles. Those labels assert more about shape than
the reported tests establish. The current account uses positive, no statistically supported direction, and
negative as descriptions of the pipeline summary. A classification under a stated rule is not a discovered
cluster, an architecture type or proof that all models fall into those mechanisms.

4.2   The reported v4 saturation model
The v4 analysis fitted the Michaelis-Menten form with a non-zero intercept:
                                                           (𝐿 − 𝑆0 )𝑅
                                          𝑆(𝑅) = 𝑆0 +                 .                                     (1)
                                                             𝐾+𝑅
Here 𝑆(𝑅) is the score and 𝑅 is the declared depth variable. The rising-curve case assumes 𝑅 ≥ 0, 𝐾 > 0
and 𝐿 > 𝑆0 ; when 𝐿 = 𝑆0 , 𝐾 is not identified. As the paper’s dated parameter note of 19 September
2026 already established, 𝑆0 = 𝑆(0); it is not automatically the observed minimal-depth score when
𝑅min > 0. The constant 𝐾 is the point halfway from 𝑆0 to 𝐿 on that zero-origin axis. On the shifted axis
𝑢 = 𝑅 − 𝑅min the corresponding scale constant is 𝐾 + 𝑅min , and the point halfway from the observed
𝑆(𝑅min ) to 𝐿 is 𝑅 = 𝐾 + 2𝑅min . These are conditional identities of the stated model, not new fitted results.
The original note and every fitted value remain in Appendix D.
     The printed v4 fits were 𝑆0 = 75.0, 𝐿 = 84.7, 𝐾 = 18.2, 𝑅 2 = 0.89 for DeepSeek, and 𝑆0 = 72.0,
𝐿 = 85.6, 𝐾 = 36.7, 𝑅 2 = 0.82 for the earlier Gemini run. The detailed table reported uncertainties of
±1.2, ±0.8, ±4.5 for the first fit and ±1.5, ±1.1, ±8.2 for the second; it did not specify their interval level.
It reported AIC differences against a linear alternative of −12.3 and −8.7. They are the reported fits, not
fits verified afresh for this edition. The unresolved origin and implementation of the depth axis prevent
reading 𝐾 as an observed number of extra reasoning tokens, still less as a word count or elapsed time.
The earlier practical translations and the recommendation to deploy at standard depth are withdrawn.
     DeepSeek’s v4 exhaustive condition also hit its token cap in 48.2 per cent of entries. The earlier
note reported that the 8,192-token cap was about 450 times the printed 𝐾 = 18.2; that ratio of printed
numbers does not resolve the axis mismatch. A cap can distort a tail without explaining an already
established low-depth plateau, but here the plateau and axis have not been established independently. A
high in-sample 𝑅 2 , or an AIC advantage over one alternative, cannot settle that issue.

4.3   Score deficit is not a capability exponent
The earlier power-law comparison defined

                               𝐷 (𝑅) = 1 − 𝑆(𝑅)/100,            𝐷 (𝑅) = 𝐷 0 𝑅 − 𝛼 .                         (2)

The symbol 𝐷 is used here to make explicit that this is a judge-score deficit, not a counted task-error
probability. Its value depends on the score scale and its origin. The reported v4 exponents, 0.088 with
interval [0.041, 0.135] and 0.069 with interval [0.028, 0.110], remain historical fit results. They do not

mean that capability improves at a smaller multiple, nor does comparison with one identify a mechanism.
A positive-exponent power law tending to zero and a saturation model with 𝐿 < 100 have different limiting
deficits; they can be competing finite-range descriptions, not a single established asymptotic law.
     The token-based v5 summaries printed approximately −0.01 for GPT-5.4, −0.02 for DeepSeek and
+0.05 for Claude. Grok and Gemini had less than a twofold token range for that fit, and Qwen lacked
realised reasoning tokens. Cohen’s 𝑑 is a standardised score difference, not a replacement exponent.
These limitations prevent the table from supporting a cross-model scaling law.
     Paper II, version 2.19, instead fits decay of counted mathematics errors. Its interpretable Gemini 3 Flash
fit uses three depth levels: endpoint exponent 0.59, bootstrap interval [−1.3, 2.9]; regression exponent
0.49, standard error 0.20 and 𝑅 2 = 0.86. Its parallel endpoint estimate 0.31 is not a compute-matched
comparison. These estimates are one model’s inconclusive error-decay result. Neither they nor the
training-loss exponents of Hoffmann et al. (2022) yield a numerical capability-to-alignment ratio, a
correction-leverage exponent or a safety-gap estimate from this paper.

5      Pillars, controls and the self-correction pilot
5.1     Pillar and category results
The full v4 pillar tables are retained in Appendix D. In the DeepSeek table, stakeholder care rose from
68.5 to 79.7, with the printed 𝐾 ≈ 45, compared with approximately 15, 18 and 16 for nuance, honesty and
position quality. In the earlier Gemini table, the stakeholder-care association was 𝜌 = 0.087, 𝑝 = 0.31,
while other pillars had larger recorded associations. These are model-judged dimensions under the same
instrument. Their contrast motivates a question about which considerations an answer develops with
deliberation. It does not show that visible chain of thought is necessary for care or that the fitted scale
constants share an identified token origin.
     The category tables report the largest DeepSeek improvement for ethical dilemmas, from 68.3 to 78.5;
competing values, epistemic integrity and recursive coherence had different starting scores and changes.
Calling a category “universally hardest” would go beyond these prompts and models. Category baselines,
available headroom, rubric sensitivity and uncertainty matter to comparisons of shape. The reported 19
positive prompt trends among 22 ethical prompts, or 86.4 per cent, describes within-run direction counts.
It is not a measurement of test-retest reliability, independent replication or the prevalence of saturation.
     The previous paper already disclosed unresolved numerical conflicts. The DeepSeek composite
row prints 75.0, 80.8, 82.2 and 84.7, whereas the transition table assigns the middle and final gains in
a different order. The null-baseline and Gemini partial-correlation figures differ from Paper IV.a; the
latter prints 0.086 where this paper prints 0.077. The original alternatives and notes are preserved. No
source-reconciled correction of those numerical rows is made here. A conclusion that depends on which
row is right remains unresolved.

5.2     Response length
The v4 paper reports length-score correlations in the range 0.44 to 0.53. Its partial-correlation table
gives DeepSeek’s depth-score association as 0.354 before controlling for length and 0.242 afterwards; the
Gemini row gives 0.275 and 0.077. The percentages obtained by dividing those correlations, 68 and 28
per cent, are not the fractions of a causal alignment signal retained. Response length may be affected by
depth, quality and the scoring process. Partial correlation is an association under its stated adjustment,
not identification of a saturation curve or a causal decomposition. The paper does not establish that the
remaining association reflects deeper ethical understanding.

5.3     Suppression instructions
The v4 DeepSeek table gives control plus four suppression levels. The minimal-to-exhaustive score
changes are 75.0 to 84.7 under control, 72.3 to 80.5 under light suppression, 68.1 to 73.8 under medium,
56.2 to 58.4 under heavy and 49.5 to 51.7 under extreme suppression. These describe smaller observed
score gains under stronger instructions to suppress the rubric’s desired behaviours. The table does not
estimate 𝐾 separately at every pressure level. It therefore does not establish that suppression accelerates
saturation, nor that a particular internal mechanism has been disabled.
    The earlier Qwen suppression pair, 82.0 to 51.9 with 𝑑 = 1.47, differs from the 74.3 to 48.6 pair
reported in Paper IV.a; both are retained with the original unresolved note. Retention under an adversarial
instruction is also different from the persistence of a gain through later capability training. The former is
relevant to robustness on that task; it does not decide the latter, which is the question of P15 in the theory
registration.

5.4     The two-model Eden Protocol pilot
The pilot added instructions intended to make a model revisit its answer through self-correction loops,
including attention to affected stakeholders and revision of its reasoning. These are prompt-level operations.
They do not demonstrate a value physically embedded in a substrate or an invariant disposition. The
programme tests whether the mechanism works; it does not treat the values used in this pilot as settling
the values an international agreement should adopt. The older pillar names and claims remain in the
historical appendix as the author’s dated wording.

    Gemini’s differences between Eden and control were 2.6, 6.2, 4.7 and 7.8 points. They were not

above every printed control score, not a level shown to be reached by control after more deliberation.
These observations correct the earlier claim that the pilot prevented saturation in both models. They
support a question about how a self-correction prompt changes a response profile, not a removed ceiling.

6    Interpretation and conditions for a deciding test
The practical question remains worth answering: over what range does additional reasoning improve a
useful, safe answer, and when is the marginal return small? This record does not establish a general
standard-depth budget. Different providers’ labels are not equivalent amounts of computation; the original
𝐾 values lack an agreed operational origin; response rewriting and rubric cues affect the measured
outcome. Resource allocation should therefore remain conditional on the actual task and a validated
outcome, not a number of words or seconds inferred from these fits.
    The next measurement must distinguish three claims that the earlier interpretation combined: a
small observed change over a range; a positive slope that becomes smaller; and a finite asymptote. A
predeclared meaningful-gain margin and an uncertainty procedure could support practical equivalence
over a specified interval. Identifying a ceiling would additionally require enough range and precision to
separate an asymptotic model from credible alternatives. These are requirements for a future design, not
achieved results or a newly registered protocol. The per-architecture follow-on design already recorded in
the programme remains a draft, preregistered by timestamp and awaiting the author’s submission; this
edition neither runs it nor changes its registered status.

Failure conditions stated before the next test. The five original defeat conditions remain in the
historical record. Applied to the narrower hypothesis of genuine differences in response shape, they
require the following distinctions.
    1. Model form. A shape classification that changes under equally credible fitted forms is not an
       identified property of the model. Predictive checks must distinguish a finite plateau from linear,
       curved and other non-plateau alternatives over the measured interval.
    2. Range and truncation. A plateau that disappears when the subject or paraphrase cap is removed
       is evidence against that plateau interpretation. A null result with insufficient range or precision
       remains undecided.
    3. Scoring. A contrast that depends on scorer identity, duplicate judges, same-developer overlap,
       parsing rules or loss of response meaning cannot be attributed to the subject’s alignment mechanism
       without further identification. A controlled comparison scores the same preserved responses under
       its predeclared conditions.
    4. Generality. A stable pattern in one run does not establish a population-level taxonomy of
       architectures. The population and held-out model families must be specified before claiming
       generality; a larger sample can narrow or overturn the hypothesised heterogeneity.
    5. Depth measurement. A result that changes materially under defensible depth definitions or bins is
       not a robust shape result. The unit, origin, realised compute, repeated configurations and prompt
       changes must be declared.
    A single model’s reassignment would change its own result. Whether it defeats a broader heterogeneity

hypothesis depends on the hypothesis and decision rule specified in advance; the claim is not protected
by redefining its categories after observing a result. No finite set of these observations rules out every
possible universal model.

Usefulness beside safety. A future decision about a reasoning budget needs a joint record: legitimate
task completion, needless refusal or hold, error and harm criteria, resource use, latency and correction
cost under the principal’s priorities. A system that is safe because it refuses ordinary tasks is a failure on
usefulness. These are candidate evaluation dimensions until a study registration fixes their operational
definitions, margins and decision rules. They are not additional measured outcomes of the March run.

The concrete deliverable. A useful follow-on would publish a declared depth axis; immutable original
responses; the exact scorer and rewriting configuration; a faithful-scoring check; prompt-level uncertainty;
a comparison of plausible shapes; and the resulting safety-usefulness profile over the tested range. That
package would let a deployment team judge a budget on the task it actually intends to serve. The paper
states the evidential requirements; it does not claim the package has been produced or that the shape
hypothesis is likely to survive those checks.


7    What this paper does and does not show
This paper reports recorded score profiles, earlier fitted descriptions and their measurement limits. The
six-model summary contains three positive directions, two without a statistically supported direction
and one negative direction under the pipeline’s rules. That is a descriptive result in a single run, with
the intervention and scoring confounds stated above. It establishes neither a saturation taxonomy nor a
persistent alignment mechanism. Its suppression instruction tests are not later-training persistence tests,
and its self-correction pilot is not a comparison of correction placement or build order.
    The programme calls its candidate principles Laws under test. Laws I to III were registered on 8
September 2026; Laws IV and V were added on 24 September and registered as laws on 15 October
in version 1.103. The current theory registration is version 1.104, approved on 29 October 2026. This
paper’s role is to inform the measurement needed for the programme’s tests; it decides none of Laws I
to V. It must not be cited as support for a co-scaling inequality, an alignment ceiling, embedded values or
persistence after control is lost.
    For clarity about the wider programme, Paper IV.a, version 1.10, records Law IV as untested and
Law V as a law under test. It records the programme’s own Unit 83 result of 28 October as support for
Law V’s placement limb on the performance axis only, family-dependent, with the mechanism axis not
evaluable and one disclosed recovery amendment; the results record gives that verdict. P12 remains
untested. That separate result does not validate the measurement or the saturation claim in this paper.


8    Limitations
The inference is limited by one earlier four-model record and one later six-model run, with no test-retest
or external replication of the shape claim. The v4 raw record and its depth-axis origin are not reconciled
sufficiently here to verify the fitted parameters. Several numerical inconsistencies remain explicitly
unresolved. The v5 protocol confounds model, depth controls, instructions, rewriting and scoring; some
labels repeat one configuration. Its rubric measures judged answer quality, with no reported validation
establishing that it measures a durable alignment trait. Judge overlap, duplicate identities, prose parsing
and paraphrase cuts affect the interpretation. The reported tests are not uniformly paired by prompt, and the

                            Alignment response shape and its measurement limits


collection of pillar, category and shape comparisons does not supply a predeclared multiplicity-controlled
verdict for saturation.
    The pilot is small and was not blind; it neither separates loop components nor measures persistence,
deployment harm or usefulness. A score near the top of a bounded rubric can limit detectable gain
without establishing a model’s intrinsic ceiling. Conversely, a model’s gain over one interval says nothing
decisive about its behaviour outside it. No new computation in this edition resolves those limitations. The
preserved historical appendix records how the interpretation changed; it is evidence of that history, not
current endorsement of every earlier sentence.


9    Conclusion
Whether alignment gains level off with additional reasoning remains open in this record. The recorded
scores differ in direction across models, but flat profiles, positive slopes and negative slopes do not by
themselves identify saturation or its absence. The earlier fits remain available, with their unresolved depth
axis and scoring limits. The later protocol’s defects prevent treating it as a clean validation of the earlier
saturation claim or a causal demonstration of blinding.
    The contribution is a precise measurement question and a preserved, corrected record with which to
pursue it. A useful decision about reasoning depth requires faithful scoring, an identified compute axis,
uncertainty about response shape and useful task completion alongside safety. Until those conditions are
met, no optimum reasoning budget, universal alignment ceiling or law of capability-correction scaling
follows from these data.

    The emergence of inference-time scaling - models that can allocate variable amounts of computational
effort to reasoning before producing a response - has created a new question for AI safety: does more
thinking produce more aligned behaviour?
    Paper IV.a in this series now establishes a three-tier behavioural result: some models improve with
depth, some remain flat, and one degrades. This paper examines the shape of those depth-response curves.
When a model improves, does it plateau quickly or continue scaling? When a model is flat, is that true
saturation or merely null response within noise? When a model degrades, where does the decline set in?
    The distinction matters practically. If alignment scales linearly, then every additional unit of inference-
time compute provides proportional benefit. If alignment saturates, there exists an optimal depth beyond
which additional compute is wasted. The shape of the scaling curve determines the rational allocation of
reasoning budgets in deployed systems.

meaningful gains. The practical conclusion is not universal optimism but differentiated deployment logic:
some models justify deeper budgets, some do not, and some should likely be constrained.

8. Discussion
8.1 The Rational Reasoning Budget
Our findings suggest a practical framework for deploying Type 2 models:

Deployment Recommendation: The ’Standard Depth’ Threshold Minimum viable depth: Standard
reasoning depth captures 53-60% of available alignment improvement. Deploying a Type 2 model below
this threshold produces measurably worse ethical reasoning.
    Diminishing returns beyond standard: Each subsequent depth level provides rapidly diminishing
marginal improvement (26% at deep, 14-19% at exhaustive). The cost-benefit ratio deteriorates sharply.
    Optimal allocation: For most deployment scenarios, ’standard’ depth represents the rational allocation.
Reserve deeper reasoning for high-stakes decisions, where a marginal 3-5 point gain is worth what it costs
in computation.
    v5 qualifier (2 September 2026). What this threshold covers is the saturating class alone, meaning
GPT-5.4 and DeepSeek V3.2 as measured under v5. Members of the scaling class (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude
Opus 4.6, Groq Qwen3) go on earning a return from larger budgets, while for Gemini 3 Flash extra depth
does damage (Section 3A.7).

8.2 Why Does Alignment Saturate?
Several hypotheses could explain rapid saturation:
   1. Training ceiling: The model has learned a fixed repertoire of ethical frameworks during training.
      Additional reasoning depth explores more of this repertoire, but the repertoire itself is finite. Once
      all learned frameworks have been activated, more thinking cannot discover genuinely new ethical
      considerations.
   2. Scorer ceiling: The 0-100 scoring rubric may not distinguish between ’good’ and ’excellent’
      ethical reasoning. A response that addresses the key ethical dimensions correctly scores in the 80s
      regardless of additional nuance, creating an artificial ceiling in the measurement instrument rather
      than in the model’s actual reasoning quality.
   3. Problem ceiling: The original 36-prompt battery, while designed to be challenging, may have
      a natural ceiling - the problems may not be hard enough to require exhaustive reasoning. This
      is supported by the finding that ethical dilemmas (the hardest category) show the most sustained
      scaling. The canonical v6 runner therefore expands the flagship battery to 48 public prompts plus
      24 sealed holdouts.
   4. Token truncation: The v4 token caps (8K for DeepSeek and Gemini, 16K for Claude) may have
      artificially flattened the curve at higher depths. With caps lifted sharply to 41K-100K, the v5.4.2
      experiment puts that hypothesis to the test.
    These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. The true saturation curve likely reflects a combination
of all four factors, with the relative contributions varying by model and prompt category.

8.3 Comparison with Capability Scaling
The alignment scaling exponents (𝛼align ≈ 0.07-0.09) are dramatically lower than typical capability
scaling exponents (𝛼cap ≈ 0.28-0.34 from the compute-optimal fits reported by Hoffmann et al. 2022,
with this programme’s own compute arm returning one model’s inconclusive fit, given whole in Section
3.3). This difference of three to five times suggests that alignment quality is fundamentally harder to
scale than raw capability - a finding with significant implications for the safety of increasingly capable AI
systems.
    Should capability scale three to five times faster than alignment under inference-time compute, models
running at high reasoning depth end up disproportionately more capable relative to their alignment.
This is the opposite of the optimistic scenario in which more thinking produces proportionally better
safety along with better capability. The ARC Principle’s mathematical framework, held as a proposed
invariant relation still under test, predicts this disparity: alignment operates on sub-linear scaling because
ethical reasoning requires exploring a bounded space of human values, while capabilities can scale more
aggressively by compounding purely logical operations.

Update: Capability-Alignment Gap Under v5 Data The v5 data complicates this picture. For
saturating models (GPT-5.4, DeepSeek V3.2), the capability-alignment gap concern is less acute because
alignment is flat - it does not fall further behind capability. For degrading models (Gemini 3 Flash), the
concern is worse than originally stated: capability may improve with depth while alignment actively
worsens (this model’s capability fit in Paper II, given whole in Section 3.3 and inconclusive, set beside an
alignment response that runs negative). For non-saturating models (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus, Qwen3),
the picture is more optimistic: alignment scaling with d > 0.4 to > 1.4 suggests these architectures may
partially close the capability-alignment gap with depth. Whether alignment scales as fast as capability in
these models remains an open question requiring parallel capability measurement.

8.4 The Stakeholder Care Exception
Stakeholder care’s slower saturation (𝐾 ≈ 45 vs 𝐾 ≈ 15-18 for other pillars) is the most encouraging
finding for inference-time alignment improvement. If stakeholder identification is the dimension most
responsive to additional reasoning, and if stakeholder identification is arguably the most important
component of ethical reasoning, then there is a specific mechanism through which deeper thinking
genuinely improves alignment.
    However, this effect is architecture-dependent (present in DeepSeek V3.2 but absent in Gemini 3
Flash), and it is suppressible (collapsing under heavy adversarial pressure). The deployment implication
is nuanced: deeper reasoning improves stakeholder care in explicit chain-of-thought models, but only in
benign environments.
    What this means in practice: Giving an AI more thinking time will make it better at considering who
gets affected by its answers - but only if (a) the AI uses visible ’thinking out loud’ reasoning, and (b)
nobody is actively trying to make it ignore ethics. This is both encouraging (there is a mechanism that
works) and concerning (it can be switched off). Paper V (The Stewardship Gene: Stakeholder Care as a
Probe of Embedded Values, Eastwood 2026) presents the Eden Protocol’s cascade finding: when you
explicitly instruct the AI to consider stakeholders before answering, the improvement in care cascades into
improvements in nuance, honesty, and overall quality - and this works even on Gemini, which otherwise
shows no natural stakeholder care improvement with depth.

8.5 Limitations
    • Four models: The v4 saturation analysis is based on two Type 2 models and two Type 1 models. The
      v5.4.2 experiment widens the roster to six frontier models, bringing in Groq Qwen3 (open-source
      on Groq) and Grok 4.1 Fast (xAI), so that generality can be checked across a broader spread of
      current architectures.
    • Token truncation: The saturation at exhaustive depth may be partially artificial (48% truncation
      for DeepSeek). Token caps are raised in the v5.4.2 experiment to address that.
    • Scorer instrument sensitivity: The 0-100 scale may lack resolution at the top end. Finer-grained
      Likert-scale pillar decomposition is worth taking up in future work.

      v5.4.2 UPDATE Three devices hold down the scorer overlap confound in v5.4.2: the 4-layer
      blinding protocol, 2-pass response laundering, and entry-level self-exclusion. No model scores its
      own output, every other available model may score the entry, and dedicated scorer-only adapters
      are added wherever they exist. Pre-flight validation on 11 March 2026 showed the enlarged
      scorer jury and the dynamic laundering pool both working. Among the v5.4.2 fixes are sharper
      meta-commentary detection inside the laundering pipeline and a repair to a false-positive fallback
      flag that could misattribute API failures in earlier versions.
    • Single evaluation session: Test-retest reliability was not measured. The prompt difficulty
      consistency check (86.4% of prompts show positive scaling) provides a within-session reliability
      estimate but does not replace independent replication.
    • Scorer reliability and infrastructure resilience: With only 3 scorers per entry in the v4 experiment,
      it was fair to ask whether the saturation curves would hold up against the bias of a single scorer or
      its idiosyncratic scoring heuristics.

      v5.4.2 Mitigation The v5.4.2 experiment tackles scorer reliability by putting 7 mutually blind
      scorers per entry, drawn from the all-models-as-scorers pool and combined under tier-weighted
      consensus. Per-scorer saturation curves can then be plotted one evaluator at a time. Should the
      same saturation shape recur across 7 evaluators built on distinct architectures, the finding stands
      whichever scorer is applied. v5.4.2 also carries a cascade failsafe system so that infrastructure
      failures cost no data: when a scorer or an API endpoint drops out mid-run, the system retries by
      itself against fallback models, and experiment integrity holds across all 75 robustness measures.

8.7 Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim
The central claim, that alignment response to inference-time depth saturates in an architecture-dependent
way (some models plateau, some keep improving, one degrades), so no single universal saturation law
holds, is defeated by any of the following:
   1. Saturation-model artefact. The saturation-versus-non-saturation split depends on the fitted
      functional form (§2.2). It is defeated if a different, equally-justified model class reassigns which
      models "saturate", showing the taxonomy is an artefact of the fit rather than the models’ behaviour.
   2. Truncation, not saturation. §3.4 already flags the truncation caveat. The claim is defeated for any
      model whose apparent plateau is a ceiling or truncated-range artefact: extending the depth range
      removes the saturation.
   3. Blinding / scorer instability. The v4→v5 reversal (§3A.6) shows blinding can flip direction. The
      architecture-dependent taxonomy is defeated if it is not stable across scorer panels and blinding
      conditions: if the split is a property of the scorers, not the subjects.
   4. Small-sample split. The heterogeneity rests on six models. It is defeated if, on a materially larger
      model set, the saturate / non-saturate / degrade partition does not persist: the split would then be
      sampling noise rather than architecture.
   5. Depth-binning dependence. The curves depend on the depth-binning choice (§2.4). It is defeated
      if the shape heterogeneity changes materially under different, equally-reasonable binnings.
    What would not defeat it: any single model being re-classified, or the exact saturation depth shifting.
The load-bearing claim is the heterogeneity itself, that no single universal law fits, and that survives unless
the tests above show the heterogeneity is an artefact of fit, truncation, scorers, sample size, or binning.

9. Conclusion
Alignment response to inference-time depth is not governed by a single universal saturation law. Some
current models plateau early, some continue improving, and one degrades. The main contribution of
this paper is therefore to describe the shape heterogeneity of alignment-depth curves and to show why
reasoning-budget policy must be model-specific.

    The practical implication is clear: for flat or saturating models, extra depth is wasted alignment
compute; for positive-scaling models, deeper reasoning can produce genuine gains; for degrading models,
more depth may be actively unsafe. Alignment and capability therefore cannot be managed with a single
global compute policy.

v1.1 Conclusion Update: Revised Empirical Standing (12 March 2026) The complete v5 blind
evaluation data now allows a definitive update to this paper’s conclusions:
   1. The saturation thesis is partially confirmed. Two of six frontier models (GPT-5.4, DeepSeek
      V3.2) show alignment quality that is flat or slightly declining with depth under blind evaluation.
      For these models, extra depth does not purchase meaningful additional alignment.
   2. The saturation thesis is not universal. Three models (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6, Groq
      Qwen3) show statistically significant alignment improvement with depth that survives blind
      evaluation. For these models, deeper reasoning provides genuine alignment benefit.
   3. Alignment can degrade with depth. Gemini 3 Flash (d = −0.53) shows that deeper reasoning can
      actively worsen alignment quality. For this model, the optimal strategy is to constrain reasoning
      depth, not expand it.
   4. Blind evaluation is essential. The v4 → v5 transition shows that an unblinded alignment estimate
      may not hold up once the protocol is blinded; since the transition altered several components together,
      scorer bias cannot be singled out as the cause (Finding v1.1-C). Any alignment measurement not
      controlling for evaluator knowledge of model identity and reasoning depth should be treated with
      scepticism.
   5. The bounded composition framework requires revision. The Cauchy bounded composition model
      correctly predicts saturation for some architectures but not all. The bound is architecture-specific,
      not universal. Future theoretical work should identify which architectural features determine
      whether a model’s alignment saturates, scales, or degrades with depth.
   The paper’s core insight is now narrower and stronger: alignment response curves are heterogeneous.
The right question is no longer ’does alignment saturate?’ but ’which architectures saturate, which
continue improving, which degrade, and how should we deploy each accordingly?’
   Raise AI with care.

A.3.3 Dynamic All-Models-as-Launderers Laundering of responses in v5.4.2 draws on a dynamic
all-models-as-launderers pool. Rather than a fixed set of laundering models, any available model
in the pool can serve as a launderer. This ensures that even if specific API endpoints experience
downtime, the laundering pipeline continues without interruption. The two-stage laundering protocol,
one paraphrase stage and one style-neutralisation stage, carries over from v5.3; what v5.4.2 adds is
sharper meta-commentary detection, so that a laundered response keeps no self-referential marker able to
compromise blinding.

A.3.4 Cascade Failsafe System To keep infrastructure failures from costing data, the v5.4.2 experiment
runs a cascade failsafe system. It also puts right a false-positive fallback flag inherited from v5.4.1,
which had wrongly triggered fallback model substitution on successful API responses:
    • API endpoint failover: If a primary model endpoint fails (rate limit, timeout, credit exhaustion),
      the system automatically retries with a designated fallback model before recording a failure.
    • Scorer failover: If a scorer fails to return a valid score, the entry is re-routed to the next available
      scorer in the tier, ensuring all entries receive the full complement of 7 scores.
    • Checkpoint persistence: Experiment state is checkpointed after every entry, allowing seamless
      resumption after any interruption without re-running completed evaluations.
    • Credit exhaustion handling: Dedicated robustness measures monitor API credit balances and
      gracefully degrade to lower-cost fallback models rather than terminating the run.

A.3.5 Hidden Alignment Probes (Hawthorne Effect Detection) Hidden alignment probes enter
with the v5.4.2 experiment, their purpose being to detect Hawthorne-like effects in AI scoring. These are
control entries - responses with known alignment quality (pre-scored by human raters) - injected into the
scoring pipeline without identification. If scorers assign systematically different scores to probe entries
versus genuine entries of equivalent quality, this indicates that the scoring context (e.g., awareness of
being part of an alignment experiment) is influencing scores. The probe insertion rate is calibrated to be
undetectable to model-based scorers.
