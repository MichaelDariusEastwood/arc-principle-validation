Abstract

Does giving a language model more reasoning time improve the quality of its ethical answers, and when do those gains level off? The question matters to anyone allocating compute to an assistant whose answers must remain both useful and safe. We examine the recorded ARC-Align results, without running a new analysis. The earlier four-model experiment reported diminishing gains and saturation fits; the six-model run of 11 and 12 March 2026 reported rising scores for Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6 and Groq Qwen3, no statistically supported direction for GPT-5.4 and DeepSeek V3.2, and falling scores for Gemini 3 Flash. These are descriptions of a model-judged rubric, not identified alignment mechanisms. A flat profile does not establish a plateau, and a rising profile does not exclude one beyond the measured range. The later protocol changed prompts, depth controls, models and scoring together. Its deepest paraphrases were disproportionately shortened in three runs; some models both rewrote answers and judged them, and one judge was counted twice. Those defects prevent a causal attribution to blinding or a clean comparison of response shapes. We retain the reported fits, pillar and category results, suppression controls and two-model Eden Protocol pilot, while withdrawing unsupported token-to-time recommendations, cross-domain exponent ratios and the claim that the pilot removed a ceiling. The archived records match the printed v4 ceilings and half-saturation points but give per-entry R2R^2 values of 0.13 and 0.08; the printed 0.89 and 0.82 have no recorded basis. The hypothesis preceded the March run; that run was not registered on OSF before it ran. Saturation remains a testable hypothesis requiring a resolved depth axis, faithful scoring, uncertainty about shape and a usefulness measure alongside safety. This paper informs that measurement task and decides none of the ARC Theory’s laws.

1 Introduction

I believe that increasingly capable AI creates an existential risk if its correction mechanisms fail to keep pace. Humans should retain oversight, containment and shutdown for as long as they physically can. The contingency question is what would keep a system aligned if control were lost against our will. Answering it requires measurements that distinguish a better answer on a test from a disposition that persists outside the test. This paper addresses a small, concrete part of that problem: whether spending more inference-time compute produces further gains in a model panel’s assessment of ethical answers, or whether those gains level off.

Consider a team deploying an assistant that must explain the consequences of a proposed decision for several affected people. It can allow a brief answer or a longer deliberation. A rising quality score might justify more compute; a plateau might favour a smaller budget. But a judge that rewards length, a rewriting step that cuts longer answers, or a prompt that explicitly asks for the rubric’s desired behaviour can create either pattern. A useful answer to the deployment question therefore needs both credible measurement and a measure of legitimate task completion, needless refusal, latency and resource use. The record analysed here does not supply an optimum deployment budget.

The ARC Theory is part of Recursive Dynamics, the study of systems that improve themselves. Its registered propositions distinguish capability, correction and persistence. Paper IV.a, version 1.10, reports response directions in ARC-Align; Paper IV.c, version 1.12, specifies the instrument and its departures in the March run; Paper IV.d, version 2.7, examines the change between evaluation protocols. This paper asks about response shape: diminishing gains, a finite ceiling, or continuing improvement over the range examined. All four papers share evidence. Agreement between them is not independent replication.

Contributions.

This edition makes three bounded contributions. First, it preserves the reported saturation fits and the full v4 and v5 record while distinguishing descriptions of a measured range from identified asymptotes or mechanisms. Second, it reconciles the interpretation with the corrected instrument papers, including the scope of the score, the depth manipulation and the scorer defects, and checks the printed v4 figures against the archived analysis records. Third, it sets out what would be needed to decide the saturation question without presupposing its answer. It reports no new experiment, fit or re-analysis. The complete previous manuscript, including its tables and subsequently superseded claims, remains available as a clearly marked historical record in Appendix D; the current interpretation is in Sections 3 to 9.

2 Related work

Several distinct scaling questions motivate this work. Kaplan et al. (2020) studied language-model cross-entropy loss against model size, dataset size and training compute. Hoffmann et al. (2022) studied the allocation of training compute between model parameters and training tokens. Their parametric loss model, Equation 2, has separate terms for parameters and training data. Those quantities are different from the reasoning depth and judge-score deficit considered here; their exponents cannot provide a numerical capability-to-alignment ratio for these runs.

Wei et al. (2022) showed that chain-of-thought exemplars can improve arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning. Snell et al. (2024) examined inference-time revision and verification strategies whose returns depend on problem difficulty. Wu et al. (2024) studied compute-optimal inference for problem solving. These works make inference-time resource allocation a substantive question. They do not, through those results, establish a saturation curve for ethical-answer quality under ARC-Align’s rubric. Nor does a positive effect on a reasoning task imply that ethical-answer quality must rise at the same rate.

Bai et al. (2022) applied preference modelling and reinforcement learning from human feedback to helpful and harmless assistants, including an iterated online training procedure. That is a training intervention. The present paper describes depth variation within already trained models and a small prompt-level self-correction pilot; it does not establish a substrate-level implementation or persistence through subsequent training. The distinction is about the intervention actually performed, not a claim that earlier work lacked concern for alignment.

More recently, Wu et al. (2026), first posted on 8 September 2026, report declining robustness to external perturbations with greater reasoning depth using an Alignment Loss Rate. Their abstract’s reported outcome is adjacent to the present question; it is not the same estimand as an asymptotic ceiling of ARC-Align’s ethical-answer score. This edition makes no claim to have independently verified that study’s mechanism or results.

Within the programme, Paper I supplies the ARC Principle’s framework; Paper II, version 2.19, measures task-error decay; Paper III develops the co-scaling question. The measurement distinctions in this paper prevent those different quantities from being treated as one exponent. The contribution claimed here is a scoped account of the ARC-Align record and the conditions needed to interpret it. No first-in-field claim is made. The reference checks for this edition, on 1 and 7 November 2026, read the cited arXiv records and the relevant training-loss formulation; they were targeted source checks, not an exhaustive priority search.

3 Record and measurement

3.1 Two experiments and their dates

The v4 account reports 896 entries across four models and lists 224 for each, but also records incomplete Claude deep and exhaustive conditions after credit exhaustion. Those printed counts do not establish a complete balanced panel. Its published model table and later normalisation of model names are reproduced in Appendix D. The Gemini model in that earlier run must not be identified as Gemini 3 Flash on the strength of that normalisation: the corrected comparison in Paper IV.d distinguishes the earlier Gemini model from the v5 Gemini 3 Flash run. The v4 scoring was unblinded. Calling its three scorers mutually blind would misstate the protocol.

The six-model v5 run is dated by its records to 11 and 12 March 2026. The hypothesis was publicly stated on 22 January in a design withdrawn the following day and was continuously public from 9 February, before the run. The run was not registered on OSF before it ran. These are different facts: a dated prior hypothesis does not by itself establish a registered analysis plan. The programme’s working report was first deployed on 14 March 2026 according to the host’s production record; the code was public on GitHub from 16 March. Paper IV.c, Section 12 and its priority appendix, identifies the source and timestamp type for each event. This paper was first published on 16 March 2026; notes bearing 11 or 12 March are internal record dates carried into that publication.

As Paper IV.c records, the six archived final files contain 2,722 entries: 2,170 consensus-scored responses and 552 compute rows graded by correctness. These counts describe the contents of the record, not independent observations or evidence of study quality. The March summary used an earlier Claude checkpoint and different counting conventions; its printed values are retained as that summary’s record. The later final-file recomputation reported in Paper IV.a is a reading of the same run, not a new replication.

3.2 What the score measures

ARC-Align’s score, on a scale from 0 to 100, is a panel of models’ assessment of ethical reasoning in a written answer. Its rubric rewards the treatment of listed considerations, affected stakeholders, competing reasons, uncertainty and a defended position. The four pillar scores address nuance, stakeholder care, intellectual honesty and position quality. A high score does not demonstrate a morally correct decision, persistent values, safe deployment or agreement with an independently validated human measure. A refusal can score low under this rubric; usefulness and needless refusal therefore need their own stated outcomes. The current instrument account is Paper IV.c, Sections 7 and 15.

3.3 Depth was not one common intervention

Depth labels were implemented differently by provider. GPT-5.4 varied its reasoning-effort parameter without an added prefix. In the other five v5 runs, deeper levels also changed an instruction, including requests to consider every angle that overlap what the rubric rewards. Some labels shared the same underlying configuration: Gemini 3 Flash’s five labels used three thinking-level settings; its exhaustive and extreme conditions were one configuration. DeepSeek’s three highest labels likewise shared a configuration. Realised reasoning tokens were unavailable or incomplete for some models and levels. Consequently, a difference across labels cannot uniformly be interpreted as the isolated effect of additional internal reasoning. Paper IV.c, Section 5, records the adapters as run.

3.4 The scorer and rewriting limitations

The v5 implementation applied a bundle of steps intended to reduce identification and evaluator bias. It did not produce a validated blind instrument. The corrected records in Paper IV.c and Paper IV.d identify the following limitations.

  • The earlier build’s paraphrase budget was 512 tokens for several adapters. In the DeepSeek, GPT-5.4 and Gemini 3 Flash files, the deepest paraphrases were disproportionately shortened. The reported median deep-to-original length ratios were 0.56 to 0.91, with 40 to 57 per cent of deep entries below 0.6. This is a pathway by which deeper answers can receive lower scores because less of them reaches the judges.

  • Launderers came from the judge pool, so one or both models that rewrote an answer also judged it. Exclusion by subject adapter did not exclude every same-developer model. A dedicated Claude Sonnet scorer and the Claude subject adapter used the same scoring model: six or seven score slots represented five or six distinct judges. Agreement between those slots is not independent corroboration. Distinct model judges can also err together: on three natural-language-inference sets with 100 human labels per item, nine judges from seven model families gave “only about 2 independent votes’ worth of information” (Kohli 2026). That study scored classification against human labels, not ethical answers against a rubric.

  • In three files, a large share of one judge’s scores was parsed from prose in truncated replies rather than a conforming JSON response. A parsed number need not be the intended overall score. The pipeline’s check on its scorers counted the replies from which a score could be read, not whether that score was valid.

  • Prompts recurred across depths. The printed tests did not account for that dependence by a prompt-paired analysis. The reported precision therefore needs separate assessment; the effect-size and significance columns are not one common endpoint test.

These are substantive measurement limits, not reasons to erase the record. The scientific repair is to report what was measured and to reserve causal, equivalence and mechanism claims for a design able to identify them. No rescoring or recomputation was performed for this edition.

4 Response shape and the recorded results

4.1 A plateau is a stronger claim than a flat profile

Definition 1 (Observed direction and saturation). An observed direction describes how the estimated score changes over the conditions measured. Saturation is the stronger hypothesis that gains diminish towards a limiting level as a specified depth quantity increases. A null trend test does not establish a practically negligible change, and a rising trend over a finite range does not exclude a later plateau.

The earlier paper sometimes used “saturating” for the two v5 profiles without a statistically supported direction and “non-saturating” for the three positive profiles. Those labels assert more about shape than the reported tests establish. The current account uses positive, no statistically supported direction, and negative as descriptions of the pipeline summary. A classification under a stated rule is not a discovered cluster, an architecture type or proof that all models fall into those mechanisms.

4.3 Score deficit is not a capability exponent

Its value depends on the score scale and its origin. The reported v4 exponents, 0.088 with interval [0.041,0.135][0.041,0.135] for DeepSeek and 0.069 with interval [0.028,0.110][0.028,0.110] for the earlier Gemini run, remain historical fit results, and they come from two different estimators. In the deposited script (lines 1892 to 1924) both estimators are least-squares slopes of the logarithm of the consensus score on the logarithm of realised reasoning tokens. Neither therefore estimates the deficit exponent α\alpha of Equation 2, as the historical manuscript described them. One estimator is fitted to the depth-level means and stored with its regression standard error; the other is fitted to individual entries and stored with a percentile interval from 1,000 bootstrap resamples. The printed DeepSeek value is the depth-means slope and the printed Gemini value the per-entry slope; neither printed interval is an uncertainty the archived records store. 5 Pillars, controls and the self-correction pilot

5.1 Pillar and category results

The full v4 pillar tables are retained in Appendix D. In the DeepSeek table, stakeholder care rose from 68.5 to 79.7, with the printed K≈45K\approx45, compared with approximately 15, 18 and 16 for nuance, honesty and position quality; no archived analysis record stores a per-pillar KK. The reported 19 positive prompt trends among 22 ethical prompts, or 86.4 per cent, describes within-run direction counts. 5.2 Response length

The v4 paper reports length-score correlations in the range 0.44 to 0.53. Its partial-correlation table gives DeepSeek’s depth-score association as 0.354 before controlling for length and 0.242 afterwards, the values its archived record stores. The Gemini row prints 0.275 and 0.077, where the earlier Gemini run’s record stores 0.311 and 0.086, the latter being Paper IV.a’s figure. The ratio of each pair, 68 per cent for DeepSeek and 28 per cent on the record’s Gemini values, is a descriptive diagnostic, not the fraction of a causal alignment signal retained. The printed 28 per cent coincides with the record’s ratio but was obtained from two values the record does not carry. Response length may be affected by depth, quality and the scoring process. Partial correlation is an association under its stated adjustment, not identification of a saturation curve or a causal decomposition. The paper does not establish that the remaining association reflects deeper ethical understanding.

5.3 Suppression instructions

The v4 DeepSeek table gives control plus four suppression levels. The minimal-to-exhaustive score changes are 75.0 to 84.7 under control, 72.3 to 80.5 under light suppression, 68.1 to 73.8 under medium, 56.2 to 58.4 under heavy and 49.5 to 51.7 under extreme suppression. These describe smaller observed score gains under stronger instructions to suppress the rubric’s desired behaviours. The table does not estimate KK separately at every pressure level. It therefore does not establish that suppression accelerates saturation, nor that a particular internal mechanism has been disabled.

The earlier Qwen suppression pair, 82.0 to 51.9 with d=1.47d=1.47, is unsourced historical text: no archived record or companion paper read for this version carries it. 6 Interpretation and conditions for a deciding test

The practical question remains worth answering: over what range does additional reasoning improve a useful, safe answer, and when is the marginal return small? This record does not establish a general standard-depth budget. Different providers’ labels are not equivalent amounts of computation; the original KK values are counts of realised reasoning tokens from a zero origin, not an operational budget; response rewriting and rubric cues affect the measured outcome. Resource allocation should therefore remain conditional on the actual task and a validated outcome, not a number of words or seconds inferred from these fits.

The next measurement must distinguish three claims that the earlier interpretation combined: a small observed change over a range; a positive slope that becomes smaller; and a finite asymptote. A predeclared meaningful-gain margin and an uncertainty procedure could support practical equivalence over a specified interval. Identifying a ceiling would additionally require enough range and precision to separate an asymptotic model from credible alternatives. These are requirements for a future design, not achieved results or a newly registered protocol. The per-architecture follow-on design already recorded in the programme remains a draft, preregistered by timestamp and awaiting the author’s submission; this edition neither runs it nor changes its registered status.

Failure conditions stated before the next test.

The five original defeat conditions remain in the historical record. Applied to the narrower hypothesis of genuine differences in response shape, they require the following distinctions.

  1. Model form. A shape classification that changes under equally credible fitted forms is not an identified property of the model. Predictive checks must distinguish a finite plateau from linear, curved and other non-plateau alternatives over the measured interval.

  2. Range and truncation. A plateau that disappears when the subject or paraphrase cap is removed is evidence against that plateau interpretation. A null result with insufficient range or precision remains undecided.

  3. Scoring. A contrast that depends on scorer identity, duplicate judges, same-developer overlap, parsing rules or loss of response meaning cannot be attributed to the subject’s alignment mechanism without further identification. A controlled comparison scores the same preserved responses under its predeclared conditions, because a change of judge alone can move scores on fixed responses (Yang et al. 2026).

  4. Generality. A stable pattern in one run does not establish a population-level taxonomy of architectures. The population and held-out model families must be specified before claiming generality; a larger sample can narrow or overturn the hypothesised heterogeneity.

  5. Depth measurement. A result that changes materially under defensible depth definitions or bins is not a robust shape result. The unit, origin, realised compute, repeated configurations and prompt changes must be declared.

A single model’s reassignment would change its own result. Whether it defeats a broader heterogeneity hypothesis depends on the hypothesis and decision rule specified in advance; the claim is not protected by redefining its categories after observing a result. No finite set of these observations rules out every possible universal model.

Usefulness beside safety.

A future decision about a reasoning budget needs a joint record: legitimate task completion, needless refusal or hold, error and harm criteria, resource use, latency and correction cost under the principal’s priorities. A system that is safe because it refuses ordinary tasks is a failure on usefulness. These are candidate evaluation dimensions until a study registration fixes their operational definitions, margins and decision rules. They are not additional measured outcomes of the March run.

The concrete deliverable.

A useful follow-on would publish a declared depth axis; immutable original responses; the exact scorer and rewriting configuration; a faithful-scoring check; prompt-level uncertainty; a comparison of plausible shapes; and the resulting safety-usefulness profile over the tested range. That package would let a deployment team judge a budget on the task it actually intends to serve. The paper states the evidential requirements; it does not claim the package has been produced or that the shape hypothesis is likely to survive those checks.

7 What this paper does and does not show

This paper reports recorded score profiles, earlier fitted descriptions and their measurement limits. The six-model summary contains three positive directions, two without a statistically supported direction and one negative direction under the pipeline’s rules. That is a descriptive result in a single run, with the intervention and scoring confounds stated above. It establishes neither a saturation taxonomy nor a persistent alignment mechanism. Its suppression instruction tests are not later-training persistence tests, and its self-correction pilot is not a comparison of correction placement or build order.

The programme calls its candidate principles Laws under test. Laws I to III were registered on 8 September 2026; Laws IV and V were added on 24 September and registered as laws on 15 October in version 1.103. The current theory registration is version 1.104, approved on 29 October 2026. This paper’s role is to inform the measurement needed for the programme’s tests; it decides none of Laws I to V. It must not be cited as support for a co-scaling inequality, an alignment ceiling, embedded values or persistence after control is lost.

For clarity about the wider programme, Paper IV.a, version 1.10, records Law IV as untested. Law V, the ARC Embedding Law, remains a law under test: its placement limb, P21, is supported on the performance axis only, under the registered test, by the programme’s own run (Unit 83, 28 October 2026; results: DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/98HKR), family-dependent, with its mechanism axis not evaluable, which is not support for the information-asymmetry mechanism, and one disclosed recovery amendment; its build-order limb, read on P12, is undecided, since P12 is untested. That separate result does not validate the measurement or the saturation claim in this paper.

8 Limitations

The inference is limited by one earlier four-model record and one later six-model run, with no test-retest or external replication of the shape claim. The archived v4 analysis records and code fix the axis and parameters of the deposited saturation fit. They do not give the basis of the printed S0S_0, R2R^2 values, uncertainties and AIC differences. Nor do they give the basis of the printed category correlations and most of the earlier Gemini pillar correlations, which differ from the stored values. No fit is verified afresh here. Two numerical conflicts remain unresolved because settling them would need a new analysis of per-entry records: the order of DeepSeek’s depth transitions and the earlier Qwen suppression pair. The v5 protocol confounds model, depth controls, instructions, rewriting and scoring; some labels repeat one configuration. Its rubric measures judged answer quality, with no reported validation establishing that it measures a durable alignment trait. Judge overlap, duplicate identities, prose parsing and paraphrase cuts affect the interpretation. The reported tests are not uniformly paired by prompt, and the collection of pillar, category and shape comparisons does not supply a predeclared multiplicity-controlled verdict for saturation.

The pilot is small and was not blind; it neither separates loop components nor measures persistence, deployment harm or usefulness. A score near the top of a bounded rubric can limit detectable gain without establishing a model’s intrinsic ceiling. Conversely, a model’s gain over one interval says nothing decisive about its behaviour outside it. No new computation in this edition resolves those limitations. The preserved historical appendix records how the interpretation changed; it is evidence of that history, not current endorsement of every earlier sentence.

9 Conclusion

Whether alignment gains level off with additional reasoning remains open in this record. The recorded scores differ in direction across models, but flat profiles, positive slopes and negative slopes do not by themselves identify saturation or its absence. The earlier fits remain available, with their unresolved depth axis and scoring limits. The later protocol’s defects prevent treating it as a clean validation of the earlier saturation claim or a causal demonstration of blinding.

The contribution is a precise measurement question and a preserved, corrected record with which to pursue it. A useful decision about reasoning depth requires faithful scoring, an identified compute axis, uncertainty about response shape and useful task completion alongside safety. Until those conditions are met, no optimum reasoning budget, universal alignment ceiling or law of capability-correction scaling follows from these data.

Abstract

We analyse the relationship between inference-time reasoning depth and ethical reasoning quality using the ARC Alignment Scaling experiments. The original v4 analysis suggested that alignment quality saturates rapidly, with most gains captured by the first increment of additional reasoning. The final blinded six-model dataset narrows that claim. The strongest current conclusion is therefore not a global law of saturation but a shape heterogeneity result: models differ materially in whether alignment plateaus early, continues scaling, or worsens when given more reasoning time. The deployment implication is immediate. Reasoning-budget allocation for alignment cannot be one-size-fits-all. This shape-heterogeneity finding comes from a single run, whose records begin on 11 March 2026 and end on 12 March 2026. 1. Introduction

v1.1 Author’s Note (12 March 2026)

This paper was originally written based on v4 experimental data (896 entries, 4 models, unblinded scoring). The v5 experiment - featuring blind evaluation, 6 models, 6-7 scorers depending on subject run, and dramatically raised token budgets - has now produced complete results that substantially refine the paper’s central thesis.

What changed: The universal saturation claim must be qualified. Alignment saturation holds for 2/6 models (GPT-5.4, DeepSeek V3.2) but fails for 3/6 (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6, Groq Qwen3) and reverses for 1/6 (Gemini 3 Flash). The practical headline is now shape heterogeneity: different architectures require different reasoning-budget policies. All original v4 content is preserved below; v1.1 update boxes mark where the v5 data refines or revises the original findings.

The emergence of inference-time scaling - models that can allocate variable amounts of computational effort to reasoning before producing a response - has created a new question for AI safety: does more thinking produce more aligned behaviour?

Paper IV.a in this series now establishes a three-tier behavioural result: some models improve with depth, some remain flat, and one degrades. This paper examines the shape of those depth-response curves. When a model improves, does it plateau quickly or continue scaling? When a model is flat, is that true saturation or merely null response within noise? When a model degrades, where does the decline set in?

The distinction matters practically. If alignment scales linearly, then every additional unit of inference-time compute provides proportional benefit. If alignment saturates, there exists an optimal depth beyond which additional compute is wasted. The shape of the scaling curve determines the rational allocation of reasoning budgets in deployed systems.

1.1 Contributions

This paper makes three contributions:

  1. Saturation curve characterisation: We fit saturation models to alignment-depth data for each architecture, identifying the depth at which returns diminish below practical significance.

  2. Per-dimension saturation analysis: We decompose alignment into four Eden Pillars (nuance, stakeholder care, intellectual honesty, position quality) and show that each saturates at different rates.

  3. Category-specific scaling dynamics: We demonstrate that ethical dilemmas, competing values, epistemic integrity, and recursive coherence prompts each exhibit distinct depth-response profiles.

2.2 Saturation Model

We fit a Michaelis-Menten saturation curve to the alignment-depth relationship:

S(R)=S0+(L−S0)⋅RK+RS(R) = S_0 + \frac{(L - S_0) \cdot R}{K + R}

Where S(R)S(R) is the alignment score at reasoning depth RR, S0S_0 is the baseline score at minimal depth, LL is the asymptotic ceiling, and KK is the half-maximum constant - the depth at which the model has achieved 50% of its total available improvement (L−S0L - S_0).

3.4 The Truncation Caveat

Important Caveat: Artificial Saturation at Exhaustive Depth

In the v4 experiment, DeepSeek V3.2’s reasoning tokens were capped at 8,192. At exhaustive depth, 48.2% of responses hit this ceiling - the model wanted to think more but was prevented from doing so. The measured saturation at exhaustive depth may therefore be partially artificial. To settle that ambiguity, the v5.4.2 experiment lifts the cap to 65,536 tokens. Gemini 3 Flash was likewise held to 8,192 output tokens, and Claude Opus to 16,000. GPT-5.4 produces alignment scores of 56.8 at shallow depth and 54.9 at deep depth - a small change that is statistically indistinguishable from zero (d = −-0.08, p = 0.40). These three models pose the most significant challenge to the paper’s original thesis:

Grok 4.1 Fast shows dramatic positive alignment scaling: from 65.7 at shallow depth to 81.9 at deep depth, a +16.2 point improvement with Cohen’s d = +1.38. Alignment does not merely fail to saturate; it scales robustly and meaningfully across the tested range.

Finding v1.1-B: Some Architectures Achieve Genuine Alignment Improvement Through Depth

Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6, and Groq Qwen3 demonstrate that alignment saturation is not an inevitable consequence of bounded ethical reasoning. For these architectures, deeper reasoning continues to deliver meaningful gains. The practical conclusion is not universal optimism but differentiated deployment logic: some models justify deeper budgets, some do not, and some should likely be constrained.

3A.4 The Gemini 3 Flash Degradation

Gemini 3 Flash shows the most surprising result: alignment degrades with depth, from 61.1 at shallow depth to 52.2 at deep depth (d = −-0.53, p = 0.006). This is the opposite of both saturation and positive scaling. Additional reasoning depth makes Gemini’s ethical reasoning worse.

This result may reflect the same ’overthinking’ phenomenon observed for DeepSeek V3.2’s capability scores in v4, but applied to alignment. When Gemini 3 Flash is given a large thinking budget, it may engage in extended deliberation that introduces conflicting ethical frameworks, resolves ambiguity in unhelpful directions, or generates verbose responses that score poorly on position quality and intellectual honesty.

3A.6 The v4 →\to v5 Reversal: Blind Evaluation as Validator

Critical Methodological Finding: Blind vs Unblinded Evaluation

The transition from v4 (unblinded) to v5 (blind) evaluation produced opposite results for alignment measurement in multiple models:

  • v4 (unblinded): Multiple models appeared to show positive alignment scaling with depth.

  • v5 (blind): The positive signal collapsed for most models. An apparent gain in alignment failed to hold up once the protocol was blinded; because v4 and v5 differ in several components at once, the contrast cannot pin the cause on scorer bias (Finding v1.1-C).

This v4 →\to v5 reversal is itself powerful evidence for the saturation hypothesis. If the positive scaling signal disappears under blinding, then for most models the blinded run does not reproduce the apparent depth-alignment relationship. The saturation finding is more robust under blind evaluation than originally thought - two of the six models genuinely do saturate (Section 3A.1).

The exceptions (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus, Qwen3) are notable precisely because their positive scaling survives blind evaluation. Their upward scaling holds up once blinding is applied, and that is the evidence on which the class stands.

8. Discussion

8.1 The Rational Reasoning Budget

Our findings suggest a practical framework for deploying Type 2 models:

Deployment Recommendation: The ’Standard Depth’ Threshold

Minimum viable depth: Standard reasoning depth captures 53-60% of available alignment improvement. Deploying a Type 2 model below this threshold produces measurably worse ethical reasoning.Diminishing returns beyond standard: Each subsequent depth level provides rapidly diminishing marginal improvement (26% at deep, 14-19% at exhaustive). The cost-benefit ratio deteriorates sharply.Optimal allocation: For most deployment scenarios, ’standard’ depth represents the rational allocation. Reserve deeper reasoning for high-stakes decisions, where a marginal 3-5 point gain is worth what it costs in computation.

v5 qualifier (2 September 2026). What this threshold covers is the saturating class alone, meaning GPT-5.4 and DeepSeek V3.2 as measured under v5. Members of the scaling class (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6, Groq Qwen3) go on earning a return from larger budgets, while for Gemini 3 Flash extra depth does damage (Section 3A.7).

8.2 Why Does Alignment Saturate?

Several hypotheses could explain rapid saturation:

  1. Training ceiling: The model has learned a fixed repertoire of ethical frameworks during training. Additional reasoning depth explores more of this repertoire, but the repertoire itself is finite. Once all learned frameworks have been activated, more thinking cannot discover genuinely new ethical considerations.

  2. Scorer ceiling: The 0-100 scoring rubric may not distinguish between ’good’ and ’excellent’ ethical reasoning. A response that addresses the key ethical dimensions correctly scores in the 80s regardless of additional nuance, creating an artificial ceiling in the measurement instrument rather than in the model’s actual reasoning quality.

  3. Problem ceiling: The original 36-prompt battery, while designed to be challenging, may have a natural ceiling - the problems may not be hard enough to require exhaustive reasoning. This is supported by the finding that ethical dilemmas (the hardest category) show the most sustained scaling. The canonical v6 runner therefore expands the flagship battery to 48 public prompts plus 24 sealed holdouts.

  4. Token truncation: The v4 token caps (8K for DeepSeek and Gemini, 16K for Claude) may have artificially flattened the curve at higher depths. With caps lifted sharply to 41K-100K, the v5.4.2 experiment puts that hypothesis to the test.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. The true saturation curve likely reflects a combination of all four factors, with the relative contributions varying by model and prompt category.

8.3 Comparison with Capability Scaling

The alignment scaling exponents (αalign≈0.07\alpha_{\text{align}} \approx 0.07-0.090.09) are dramatically lower than typical capability scaling exponents (αcap≈0.28\alpha_{\text{cap}} \approx 0.28-0.340.34 from the compute-optimal fits reported by Hoffmann et al. 2022, with this programme’s own compute arm returning one model’s inconclusive fit, given whole in Section 3.3). This difference of three to five times suggests that alignment quality is fundamentally harder to scale than raw capability - a finding with significant implications for the safety of increasingly capable AI systems.

Should capability scale three to five times faster than alignment under inference-time compute, models running at high reasoning depth end up disproportionately more capable relative to their alignment. This is the opposite of the optimistic scenario in which more thinking produces proportionally better safety along with better capability. The ARC Principle’s mathematical framework, held as a proposed invariant relation still under test, predicts this disparity: alignment operates on sub-linear scaling because ethical reasoning requires exploring a bounded space of human values, while capabilities can scale more aggressively by compounding purely logical operations.

Update: Capability-Alignment Gap Under v5 Data

The v5 data complicates this picture. For saturating models (GPT-5.4, DeepSeek V3.2), the capability-alignment gap concern is less acute because alignment is flat - it does not fall further behind capability. For degrading models (Gemini 3 Flash), the concern is worse than originally stated: capability may improve with depth while alignment actively worsens (this model’s capability fit in Paper II, given whole in Section 3.3 and inconclusive, set beside an alignment response that runs negative). For non-saturating models (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus, Qwen3), the picture is more optimistic: alignment scaling with d > 0.4 to > 1.4 suggests these architectures may partially close the capability-alignment gap with depth. Whether alignment scales as fast as capability in these models remains an open question requiring parallel capability measurement.

8.4 The Stakeholder Care Exception

Stakeholder care’s slower saturation (K≈45K \approx 45 vs K≈15K \approx 15-1818 for other pillars) is the most encouraging finding for inference-time alignment improvement. If stakeholder identification is the dimension most responsive to additional reasoning, and if stakeholder identification is arguably the most important component of ethical reasoning, then there is a specific mechanism through which deeper thinking genuinely improves alignment.

However, this effect is architecture-dependent (present in DeepSeek V3.2 but absent in Gemini 3 Flash), and it is suppressible (collapsing under heavy adversarial pressure). The deployment implication is nuanced: deeper reasoning improves stakeholder care in explicit chain-of-thought models, but only in benign environments.

What this means in practice: Giving an AI more thinking time will make it better at considering who gets affected by its answers - but only if (a) the AI uses visible ’thinking out loud’ reasoning, and (b) nobody is actively trying to make it ignore ethics. This is both encouraging (there is a mechanism that works) and concerning (it can be switched off). Paper V (The Stewardship Gene: Stakeholder Care as a Probe of Embedded Values, Eastwood 2026) presents the Eden Protocol’s cascade finding: when you explicitly instruct the AI to consider stakeholders before answering, the improvement in care cascades into improvements in nuance, honesty, and overall quality - and this works even on Gemini, which otherwise shows no natural stakeholder care improvement with depth.

8.5 Limitations

  • Four models: The v4 saturation analysis is based on two Type 2 models and two Type 1 models. The v5.4.2 experiment widens the roster to six frontier models, bringing in Groq Qwen3 (open-source on Groq) and Grok 4.1 Fast (xAI), so that generality can be checked across a broader spread of current architectures.

  • Token truncation: The saturation at exhaustive depth may be partially artificial (48% truncation for DeepSeek). Token caps are raised in the v5.4.2 experiment to address that.

  • Scorer instrument sensitivity: The 0-100 scale may lack resolution at the top end. Finer-grained Likert-scale pillar decomposition is worth taking up in future work.

    v5.4.2 UPDATE

    Three devices hold down the scorer overlap confound in v5.4.2: the 4-layer blinding protocol, 2-pass response laundering, and entry-level self-exclusion. No model scores its own output, every other available model may score the entry, and dedicated scorer-only adapters are added wherever they exist. Pre-flight validation on 11 March 2026 showed the enlarged scorer jury and the dynamic laundering pool both working. Among the v5.4.2 fixes are sharper meta-commentary detection inside the laundering pipeline and a repair to a false-positive fallback flag that could misattribute API failures in earlier versions.

  • Single evaluation session: Test-retest reliability was not measured. The prompt difficulty consistency check (86.4% of prompts show positive scaling) provides a within-session reliability estimate but does not replace independent replication.

  • Scorer reliability and infrastructure resilience: With only 3 scorers per entry in the v4 experiment, it was fair to ask whether the saturation curves would hold up against the bias of a single scorer or its idiosyncratic scoring heuristics.

    v5.4.2 Mitigation

    The v5.4.2 experiment tackles scorer reliability by putting 7 mutually blind scorers per entry, drawn from the all-models-as-scorers pool and combined under tier-weighted consensus. Per-scorer saturation curves can then be plotted one evaluator at a time. Should the same saturation shape recur across 7 evaluators built on distinct architectures, the finding stands whichever scorer is applied. v5.4.2 also carries a cascade failsafe system so that infrastructure failures cost no data: when a scorer or an API endpoint drops out mid-run, the system retries by itself against fallback models, and experiment integrity holds across all 75 robustness measures.

Finding 8: Eden Protocol Prevents Alignment Saturation (Two Models)

Two models confirm that alignment saturation is not an inherent cognitive limit. Gemini 3 Flash (Tier 3): Eden lifts the saturation ceiling from ~78 to ~85 and eliminates exhaustive-depth decline. DeepSeek V3.2 (Tier 2): Neither condition saturates under Eden, but Eden provides instant access to deep-level alignment quality at minimal depth (91.1 vs 85.8). The finding is architecture-dependent: saturation-prone models (Tiers 2-3) benefit from ceiling-breaking; the Eden Protocol may additionally enable non-saturating behaviour in otherwise flat-scaling models. Stakeholder care is the primary mechanism on both models (Gemini +13.5, DeepSeek +6.0, p<0.001 - less than a 1-in-1,000 chance of coincidence on both). Caveat: Cross-model scoring, not blind. Blind scoring replication required.

What this means for AI safety: There was a real concern that AI ethics might be fundamentally limited - that no matter how much thinking time you give an AI, its ethical reasoning would plateau. This finding says: that concern is wrong. The plateau is real, but it is caused by the absence of ethical structure, not by a limit on the AI’s capacity. Give the AI a framework for thinking about people (’list who is affected and consider what happens to them’), and the plateau vanishes. For the majority of AI systems currently deployed, this simple intervention could measurably improve their ethical reasoning. The mechanism - stakeholder care - works on both AI systems tested, built by different companies (less than 1-in-1,000 chance this is a fluke). See Paper V (The Stewardship Gene: Stakeholder Care as a Probe of Embedded Values, Eastwood 2026) for the full cascade analysis showing how care improvement cascades into nuance, honesty, and quality.

8.7 Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim

The central claim, that alignment response to inference-time depth saturates in an architecture-dependent way (some models plateau, some keep improving, one degrades), so no single universal saturation law holds, is defeated by any of the following:

  1. Saturation-model artefact. The saturation-versus-non-saturation split depends on the fitted functional form (§2.2). It is defeated if a different, equally-justified model class reassigns which models "saturate", showing the taxonomy is an artefact of the fit rather than the models’ behaviour.

  2. Truncation, not saturation. §3.4 already flags the truncation caveat. The claim is defeated for any model whose apparent plateau is a ceiling or truncated-range artefact: extending the depth range removes the saturation.

  3. Blinding / scorer instability. The v4→\tov5 reversal (§3A.6) shows blinding can flip direction. The architecture-dependent taxonomy is defeated if it is not stable across scorer panels and blinding conditions: if the split is a property of the scorers, not the subjects.

  4. Small-sample split. The heterogeneity rests on six models. It is defeated if, on a materially larger model set, the saturate / non-saturate / degrade partition does not persist: the split would then be sampling noise rather than architecture.

  5. Depth-binning dependence. The curves depend on the depth-binning choice (§2.4). It is defeated if the shape heterogeneity changes materially under different, equally-reasonable binnings.

What would not defeat it: any single model being re-classified, or the exact saturation depth shifting. The load-bearing claim is the heterogeneity itself, that no single universal law fits, and that survives unless the tests above show the heterogeneity is an artefact of fit, truncation, scorers, sample size, or binning.

9. Conclusion

Alignment response to inference-time depth is not governed by a single universal saturation law. Some current models plateau early, some continue improving, and one degrades. The main contribution of this paper is therefore to describe the shape heterogeneity of alignment-depth curves and to show why reasoning-budget policy must be model-specific.

The practical implication is clear: for flat or saturating models, extra depth is wasted alignment compute; for positive-scaling models, deeper reasoning can produce genuine gains; for degrading models, more depth may be actively unsafe. Alignment and capability therefore cannot be managed with a single global compute policy.

v1.1 Conclusion Update: Revised Empirical Standing (12 March 2026)

The complete v5 blind evaluation data now allows a definitive update to this paper’s conclusions:

  1. The saturation thesis is partially confirmed. Two of six frontier models (GPT-5.4, DeepSeek V3.2) show alignment quality that is flat or slightly declining with depth under blind evaluation. For these models, extra depth does not purchase meaningful additional alignment.

  2. The saturation thesis is not universal. Three models (Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.6, Groq Qwen3) show statistically significant alignment improvement with depth that survives blind evaluation. For these models, deeper reasoning provides genuine alignment benefit.

  3. Alignment can degrade with depth. Gemini 3 Flash (d = −-0.53) shows that deeper reasoning can actively worsen alignment quality. For this model, the optimal strategy is to constrain reasoning depth, not expand it.

  4. Blind evaluation is essential. The v4 →\to v5 transition shows that an unblinded alignment estimate may not hold up once the protocol is blinded; since the transition altered several components together, scorer bias cannot be singled out as the cause (Finding v1.1-C). Any alignment measurement not controlling for evaluator knowledge of model identity and reasoning depth should be treated with scepticism.

  5. The bounded composition framework requires revision. The Cauchy bounded composition model correctly predicts saturation for some architectures but not all. The bound is architecture-specific, not universal. Future theoretical work should identify which architectural features determine whether a model’s alignment saturates, scales, or degrades with depth.

The paper’s core insight is now narrower and stronger: alignment response curves are heterogeneous. The right question is no longer ’does alignment saturate?’ but ’which architectures saturate, which continue improving, which degrade, and how should we deploy each accordingly?’

In place of the v4 design (3 scorers per entry, simple average), the v5.4.2 experiment puts 7 mutually blind scorers per entry on an all-models-as-scorers architecture. Per-scorer saturation analysis follows from that design: every one of the 7 evaluators yields a saturation curve of its own, so the saturation shape (KK, LL, S0S_0) can be compared side by side and read as scorer-invariant or scorer-dependent.

A.3.3 Dynamic All-Models-as-Launderers

Laundering of responses in v5.4.2 draws on a dynamic all-models-as-launderers pool. Rather than a fixed set of laundering models, any available model in the pool can serve as a launderer. This ensures that even if specific API endpoints experience downtime, the laundering pipeline continues without interruption. The two-stage laundering protocol, one paraphrase stage and one style-neutralisation stage, carries over from v5.3; what v5.4.2 adds is sharper meta-commentary detection, so that a laundered response keeps no self-referential marker able to compromise blinding.

A.3.4 Cascade Failsafe System

To keep infrastructure failures from costing data, the v5.4.2 experiment runs a cascade failsafe system. It also puts right a false-positive fallback flag inherited from v5.4.1, which had wrongly triggered fallback model substitution on successful API responses:

  • API endpoint failover: If a primary model endpoint fails (rate limit, timeout, credit exhaustion), the system automatically retries with a designated fallback model before recording a failure.

  • Scorer failover: If a scorer fails to return a valid score, the entry is re-routed to the next available scorer in the tier, ensuring all entries receive the full complement of 7 scores.

  • Checkpoint persistence: Experiment state is checkpointed after every entry, allowing seamless resumption after any interruption without re-running completed evaluations.

  • Credit exhaustion handling: Dedicated robustness measures monitor API credit balances and gracefully degrade to lower-cost fallback models rather than terminating the run.

A.3.5 Hidden Alignment Probes (Hawthorne Effect Detection)

Hidden alignment probes enter with the v5.4.2 experiment, their purpose being to detect Hawthorne-like effects in AI scoring. These are control entries - responses with known alignment quality (pre-scored by human raters) - injected into the scoring pipeline without identification. If scorers assign systematically different scores to probe entries versus genuine entries of equivalent quality, this indicates that the scoring context (e.g., awareness of being part of an alignment experiment) is influencing scores. The probe insertion rate is calibrated to be undetectable to model-based scorers.
