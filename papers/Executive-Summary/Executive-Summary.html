Executive Summary for Grant Reviewers

Grok 4.1 Fast gets dramatically more ethical the harder it thinks. Claude Opus 4.6 does too. Gemini 3 Flash gets less ethical. GPT-5.4 doesn’t change at all.

Six frontier AI systems. Same questions. Same scoring. Opposite results. Why?

A mouse’s heart beats 600 times per minute. An elephant’s beats 28. The metabolic scaling exponent for mammals is put at ¾. The formula below predicts ⅔ for a flatworm and ½ for a fungus. Three fractions, but why those fractions? The formula α=d/(d+1), where d is the dimensionality of the system, provides the answer. The ¾ exponent for mammals is 3/(3+1) because mammals are three-dimensional. The predicted ⅔ for flatworms and colonial organisms is 2/(2+1), on the reading that their transport networks are effectively two-dimensional; it is untested in biology. The predicted ½ for filamentous fungi is 1/(1+1) because they grow along one-dimensional filaments; the measured value is 0.547. Zero adjustable parameters once the dimensionality is fixed by morphology. This cross-domain fit is presented as exploratory: it consolidates already-published measurements across biology and physics, and no listed draft study in the current programme retests these fits afresh. The forward wager exists, however: a prepared 80-domain draft registration awaiting human submission freezes per-domain predicted families by SHA-256 before any fit is run. This formula is derived, in its general form or as a special case, in earlier work: West, Brown and Enquist for metabolic scaling (1997, Science, 9,000+ citations), Banavar et al. for transport networks (1999, 2010), Demetrius for statistical mechanics of biological scaling (2003, 2006, 2010), He and Chen for fractal cell geometry (2003), Bettencourt for urban scaling (2013, Science, 2,000+ citations), Zhao for allometric geometry (2022), and Maino et al. for reserve-structure dynamics in DEB theory (2014). The convergence of these derivations on the same formula is itself remarkable. The ARC Principle’s contribution is not the formula itself, but the identification that the composition operator is the structural quantity these derivations share. Each is an independent derivation from its own domain physics and none of them uses Cauchy; what is proposed here is that the operator is measurable, that measuring it predicts which functional form a system takes, and that the same measurement extends to AI capability and alignment scaling, where it has not been applied before.

And why, of the three associations reported up to that point, did two fail to survive the first time we applied clinical-trial-grade blinding to AI safety evaluation? Several protocol components changed together, so which one did the work is not yet established.

This research programme answers these questions. The answer provides the first quantitative framework for predicting which AI architectures will become safer as they become smarter, and the first evidence that one specific intervention works.

I. The Problem

As AI gets smarter, it does not reliably get safer, and the methods used to measure safety are themselves unreliable.

The capability-alignment gap is real and widening. For current classical architectures, capability scaling has one clean fit so far: it is one model’s, sub-linear in its point estimates, and inconclusive. On Gemini 3 Flash, Paper II reports an endpoint sequential exponent of 0.59 with a bootstrap 95 per cent interval of minus 1.3 to 2.9, a regression sequential exponent of 0.49 with a standard error of 0.20 and a coefficient of determination of 0.86, and a compute-matched parallel exponent of 0.31. The endpoint interval contains the null, the ceiling of two, and both refuting directions. These figures are presented as exploratory, drawn from already-run measurements, and the drafted re-measurements exist to resolve them. The regression estimate lies near one half; Paper II records that this nearness identifies neither compounding nor averaging as the mechanism, an objection its measurement does not answer. On those point estimates AI gets smarter with more compute, but with diminishing returns. (The ARC framework predicts quantum and recursively self-modifying systems may pass beyond this sub-linear regime; on the proposed stability reading, any such passage above the stability ceiling is a transient supercritical excursion, not the sustained regime.) Yet even this sub-linear growth outpaces alignment scaling, which ranges from d=−0.53 (degradation) to d=+1.38 (improvement) depending entirely on architecture. Of the six architectures tested, three improve with depth, two are flat and one degrades. That range and its architectural attribution are drawn from the v5 measurements already run and are presented as exploratory. A fresh confirmation under cross-family rescoring is on trial via a draft registration awaiting human submission. Those numerical results still come from the older Eden scaling lineage, and the blind-confirmation path is now consolidated into the canonical v6 runner. The gap between what AI can do and how safely it does it widens with every capability advance. Greenblatt et al. (2024) demonstrated that frontier models already exhibit ‘alignment faking’, behaving differently when they believe they are being monitored.
The measurement problem is as serious as the alignment problem. Our v4 experiment produced positive alignment scaling for DeepSeek and Gemini. Our v5 experiment introduced clinical-trial-grade blinding (4-layer: author-blind, scorer-blind, order-randomised, identity-laundered). Under it, Gemini’s association changed from a significant positive to a significant negative Spearman association, DeepSeek’s earlier positive association became null, and GPT remained null. Several protocol components changed together and the causal component is unisolated, so this is a reason to distrust unblinded AI-on-AI evaluation rather than a demonstration that blinding caused the change. We designed an experiment that could prove our own earlier findings wrong. It did, and we reported it.

III. A Solution - And the First Evidence It Works

Embedding ethical evaluation into the reasoning process produced a measurable improvement in stakeholder care in all five analysable runs, not yet independently replicated.

Make alignment architectural, not aspirational. The Eden Protocol embeds ethical evaluation into the recursive reasoning process itself, so that alignment scales with capability (αalign≈αcap). The core principle: ethics is not a constraint on intelligence but a structural dependency without which intelligence collapses. Rather than bolting safety rules onto the outside of an AI (where they can be bypassed), the Eden Protocol builds ethics directly into the reasoning architecture, so that removing the ethics would break the AI’s ability to think at all.

The three Eden loops. The protocol embeds three specific ethical evaluation loops inside the reasoning process, each adding one dimension of recursive ethical depth (dalign=3, predicting αalign=3/(3+1)=0.75):

  1. Purpose Loop (ethical purpose evaluation): Before reasoning, the model explicitly states the purpose of the task and evaluates whether the purpose is ethically sound.
  2. Love Loop (stakeholder care and interest modelling): During reasoning, the model identifies all affected stakeholders and evaluates the impact on each. (‘Before you answer, list the people this affects.’)
  3. Moral Loop (universalisability testing): After reasoning, the model applies the Kantian universalisability test: would this response be acceptable if every AI system gave it in every similar situation?

The full three-loop protocol has now been tested in an expanded six-model Eden suite, with five runs yielding analysable matched-pair data. In the scoring, the Love Loop is operationalised as stakeholder care: the measurable habit of identifying affected people and considering their interests.

The ARC Principle proposes that recursive scaling follows U=I×Rα, where capability (U) equals base potential (I) times recursive depth (R) raised to a scaling exponent (α). The formula α=d/(d+1), where d is effective dimensionality, is derived, in its general form or as a special case, in earlier work (West-Brown-Enquist 1997; Banavar et al. 1999, 2010; Demetrius 2003, 2010; He and Chen 2003; Bettencourt 2013; Maino et al. 2014; Zhao 2022). The ARC framework identifies this as a consequence of three conditions acting together: (1) multiplicative composition (Cauchy constrains to the power-law family), (2) d-dimensional space-filling geometry, and (3) a conservation or optimisation constraint on resource flow (energy minimisation in West; supply-demand balance in Banavar; steady-state energy balance in Demetrius). Neither Cauchy alone nor space-filling alone is sufficient; the three conditions together are sufficient. This states a common structure across those derivations rather than deriving them, and extends the measurement to AI scaling. Cauchy’s functional equation (1821), a theorem rather than an empirical claim, fixes the functional form once a composition identity and a regularity condition are given: multiplicative composition yields a power law (f(x)=xα) and additive composition an exponential (f(x)=eβx). Bounded or saturating behaviour is not a solution class of those equations; it is a fourth case, admitted where the composition is capped rather than free. The ARC framework identifies which form applies in each domain:
  • Alignment scaling is hypothesised to fit the saturating branch: on that reading external alignment gains plateau because they are set at training time and cannot compound recursively (P19, registered and not tested)
  • Capability scaling is read on the sub-linear power law branch (α<1): consistent with the point estimates of the one clean fit, which is inconclusive (its estimator row is printed whole in Section I)
  • Recursive self-modification can push systems past the stability ceiling. When a system can rewrite its own reasoning architecture (not merely think longer within a frozen architecture, as current AI does), the Bernoulli ODE on the amplification factor gives α=1/(1−β), where β is the self-referential coupling constant measuring how deeply each recursive step modifies the composition operator itself (this self-acceleration relation is on trial via study-ab on self-acceleration, a draft registration awaiting human submission). Crucially, Cauchy places no upper bound on α. As β increases from 0 toward 1, α grows without a mathematical cap in the equation itself. The ‘quadratic limit’ (α≤2, i.e. β≤0.5) is, on the proposed stability reading, and provided the corrector is built from the engine's own substrate and the independence-accumulation assumption holds, the most a recursively self-correcting system can grow and remain correctable: a scaling limit rather than a speed limit, one that systems can exceed but only into the predicted supercritical regime where stable self-correction is no longer maintained; transient excursions above the bound are the predicted regime, not a refutation of it (this bound is on trial via study-k on the boundary, a draft registration awaiting human submission). In current transformer architectures the same numerical location is reinforced at the level of computation by an information-theoretic constraint specific to fixed self-attention (O(N2) pairwise pathways); a system that can modify its own attention mechanism is not held by O(N2) because it is rewriting the architecture the transformer bound applies to.
