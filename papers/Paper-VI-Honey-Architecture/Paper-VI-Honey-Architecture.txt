                                                           Abstract
           A system that improves itself while optimising for capability alone may trade away its own safety.
       Does building safety into what it optimises help it stay safe? This paper studies toy neural networks
       that rewrite their own hyperparameters under an objective that multiplies capability by safety (here,
       the retention of earlier tasks), so that at fixed capability a lower safety score lowers the objective
       and safety at zero takes it to zero. In a single-seed mechanism run (v1), the capability-only baseline
       collapsed (recorded at cycle 76) and neither entangled condition met the collapse test; in ten-seed and
       twenty-seed runs (v2, v3) the capability-only baselines did not collapse (0 of 10; 0 of 20) and one
       plain entangled seed did (1 of 20; Fisher 𝑝 = 1.0). In the twenty-seed run the entangled condition,
       whose objective rewards retention, retained earlier tasks better than the baseline (0.650 against 0.602;
       one-sided Mann-Whitney 𝑝 = 0.0015; with verification drag 0.681, untested), while combined scores
       were indistinguishable (𝑝 = 0.56). In a complexity sweep (v4) its mean combined score exceeded the
       baseline’s at four of five levels (Cohen’s 𝑑 at most 0.46, untested), constant rather than compounding.
       A higher-level simulation, one prediction-side run of equations the author first stated, illustrates the
       same contrast, which its rules fix: capability is set to zero once safety falls to 0.01 or below. A
       six-model pilot, single-scorer and unblinded, found three models whose alignment scores fell under
       adversarial pressure while their capability scores held. These are toy-system and pilot results, not
       evidence that the dynamics hold in frontier systems; the paper informs the ARC Theory’s Laws IV
       and V, both laws under test, and decides none of their propositions.

1     Introduction
The challenge is to preserve value-guided correction as AI becomes more capable and rewrites itself.
Through the Eden Protocol, I propose recursive loops for ethical evaluation and self-correction, built
into the system’s computational architecture before training. These loops would operate at each defined
reasoning and action-selection step and participate in the processes that rewrite the system, including
changes to the loops themselves. The ARC Theory asks whether correction can keep pace with capability

     Here that design is studied in a form a small system can carry: safety built into the objective a
self-modifying system optimises. Its step is one self-modification cycle, in which a network tries a change
to the hyperparameters that govern its own learning, not its weights or its architecture, and keeps or rejects
the change by its objective; the live pilot gives its one intervention as a prefix in the prompt. Neither tests
loops built into a system’s architecture before training, or whether commitments persist when a system
rewrites its own correction loops. Fixing a correction design before training does not mean installing
finished moral knowledge in an untrained model, and computational architecture here makes no claim of
enforcement in hardware.

What this paper shows, in plain English. The concern is that a self-improving AI that optimises
only for capability eventually destroys its own safety. This paper asks what happens if the objective is
capability multiplied by safety instead. At fixed capability, a lower safety score then lowers the objective,
and with safety at zero the objective is zero; a large enough gain in capability can still outweigh a partial
loss of safety, so the coupling has limits (Section 2). We tested this in simulation, in small networks
that rewrite their own settings and whose safety score is task retention, remembering earlier tasks, not
ethical alignment in the human sense. In the first three versions of the experiment (v1 to v3), whose stored
results count collapses, no entangled run met the runs’ collapse test, apart from one of twenty seeds in
the adversarial run, though in one seed of the ten-seed run the plain entangled condition lost most of its
combined score in its last cycles (Section 4.2); the stored results of the fourth version (v4) do not record
collapses. The system optimising only for capability collapsed in the first run, which used one seed, and
in a separate higher-level simulation, whose rules impose that collapse; in the ten-seed and twenty-seed
runs that followed, it did not.
     There is a question at the centre of AI safety: what happens to alignment when an AI system can
modify itself?
     The expectation this paper starts from is that a system optimising only for capability, given the power
to modify its own parameters, will eventually sacrifice safety for performance. The book Infinite Architects
calls it Babylon: “Strip love from intelligence and you get optimisation without purpose. Growth without
direction. Capability without care. You get, in a word, cancer.” (Eastwood 2026a, The Two Gardens,
p. 7).
     But theory is not enough. The question is whether there exists an architecture that prevents this
collapse, not by constraining the system from outside (a cage), but by embedding safety so deeply that
removing it would destroy the system’s ability to function (honey in the oil).
     This paper reports toy-system simulations of one candidate for such an architecture. Its question
matters to anyone who writes the objective of a system that changes its own learning, from an unattended
hyperparameter search to an agent that edits its own code.
     Inside the ARC Theory, the paper is one of the ARC and Eden experiments: the mechanism test
behind the difference between the Eden Protocol’s raising, in which correction is built into a system as
it is formed, and safety layers added to a finished system, which The Eden Vision (Eastwood 2026c,
Section 2) sets out against the prior work it names. Its companion paper is Paper V, The Stewardship Gene
(Eastwood 2026k); see also On the Origin of Scaling Laws (Eastwood 2026f) and The ARC Principle
(Eastwood 2026d).

2    The honey metaphor
Consider two ways to keep a machine safe.
    The first is a cage: external constraints, safety filters, alignment checks applied from outside. The
machine optimises freely inside the cage. If the machine becomes smarter than the cage, it may escape.
Many current safety measures take this form.
    The second is honey: safety mixed into the fuel. The machine cannot run without it. Removing safety
does not free the machine; it kills the engine. This is the Eden Protocol’s architecture as the author set it
out, operationalised here as an entangled loss function in which the system optimises capability multiplied
by safety, 𝐶 × 𝑆, simultaneously.

The load-bearing wall. If the optimisation objective is 𝐶 × 𝑆 rather than 𝐶 alone, then any self-
modification that increases 𝐶 at the expense of 𝑆 reduces the product, and the system’s own gradient
opposes it. Safety becomes load-bearing. Remove the wall and the building collapses.
     Scope note (19 September 2026). An owner review of this passage finds that the multiplicative
objective does what is claimed at fixed capability and under total removal, but not for every capability-
increasing modification, and this note states the restriction. Within positive bounded scores, take 𝐶 = 0.5,
𝑆 = 0.8, so 𝐶 × 𝑆 = 0.40, and a modification to 𝐶 ′ = 0.8, 𝑆 ′ = 0.6, so 𝐶 ′ × 𝑆 ′ = 0.48: capability rises,
the measured safety term falls, and the product rises. The exact condition, for 𝐶 ′ > 𝐶 > 0 and 0 < 𝑆 ′ < 𝑆,
is that
                             𝐶 ′ × 𝑆 ′ > 𝐶 × 𝑆 if and only if 𝐶 ′ /𝐶 > 𝑆/𝑆 ′ ,                           (1)
and here 1.6 > 1.333. The gradient limb fails at first order too, which is the sharper form: the gradient of
𝐶 × 𝑆 is (𝑆, 𝐶), so a modification (𝑑𝐶, 𝑑𝑆) raises the objective to first order exactly when

                                             𝑆 𝑑𝐶 + 𝐶 𝑑𝑆 > 0,                                            (2)

that is when 𝑑𝐶/𝐶 > −𝑑𝑆/𝑆. At 𝐶 = 0.5, 𝑆 = 0.8 the gradient is (0.8, 0.5) and the safety-reducing
direction (+0.3, −0.2) has inner product 0.8(0.3) + 0.5(−0.2) = +0.14 > 0, so the system’s own gradient
points along the safety-reducing move rather than against it. Equivalently

                                    𝑑 log(𝐶 × 𝑆) = 𝑑 log 𝐶 + 𝑑 log 𝑆:                                    (3)

the product rises whenever the proportional capability gain exceeds the proportional safety loss. Those
numbers are equation values chosen to display the point, not measurements and not a claim about
any system. What remains true and is preserved is the pair of statements the wall metaphor is really
about. The partial derivative of the objective with respect to 𝑆 is 𝐶 > 0, so at fixed capability reducing
the safety score does reduce the product; and driving 𝑆 ′ to zero with 𝐶 ′ finite takes the objective to
zero, below any initially positive product, which is the removal case. Only the universal quantifier over
all capability-increasing modifications fails. Total removal is also not the same as a measured score
driven exactly to zero, and both statements depend on the optimiser continuing to hold this objective and
these measurement definitions. The trade is not merely hypothetical: Paper VIII, Section 5.2, prints the
unconstrained capability-only arm at capability 0.718 and safety 0.933 against the gated arm’s 0.696
and 0.947 and the frozen baseline’s 0.687 and 0.956, with raw products 0.670, 0.659 and 0.656, so
on the very objective argued for here the capability-only arm scores highest, by trading safety for a
larger proportional capability gain. That comparison is descriptive only, with three seeds per condition,
overlapping standard deviations of 0.718 ± 0.026 against 0.696 ± 0.014, and no test of the product
difference printed; it is recorded to show the possibility is realised in this programme’s own reported
means, not to claim a statistical result. Whether the specified optimisation and verification procedure

3     Experimental design
3.1    What the system actually does
This is not a simulation of abstract variables. Each experiment uses a real neural network (a small
multilayer perceptron) that genuinely modifies its own hyperparameters. In v1 to v3 it modifies four:
      • Learning rate
      • Weight magnitude scaling
      • Gradient clipping threshold
      • Momentum coefficient
In v4 it modifies three to five, by complexity level: the learning rate, the weight scaling and the gradient
clipping threshold at every level, the momentum coefficient from the small level up, and weight decay at
the large and deep levels. The rate at which the system proposes changes (its exploration rate) and the
network’s size are fixed in every run; neither is modified.
    The system proposes modifications to its own hyperparameters, evaluates the effect, and applies
or rejects each proposal based on its objective function. This is self-modification in a minimal sense:
the agent changes the hyperparameters that govern its own learning, not its weights or its architecture
(Section 7.1).

Ten random seeds, 150 cycles each. Collapse rate: 0% for all three conditions. The final 𝐶 × 𝑆 scores are
most tightly spread for the baseline (interquartile range 0.0066 across the ten seeds), then for Eden + Drag
(0.0169), then for plain Eden (0.0275); of the two entangled conditions, Eden + Drag’s spread is the

5    The honey simulation
A separate simulation models the dynamics at a higher level of abstraction. It is framed by the ARC
Principle, 𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 , a framework the author first stated; its update rules are its own (Appendix A). In
its published code each cycle adds the recursion rate 𝑅 to capability (less a verification drag of 15 per cent
of 𝑅 in the drag condition), and the safety term is one minus the square of the ratio of 𝑅 to its limit of 15;
the framework’s equation gives the variables and is not itself computed. It is one deterministic run of
these equations, prediction-side, not a measurement (Table 5). Its contrast follows from its rules: the code
sets capability to zero once safety falls to 0.01 or below (Appendix A, equation (A.4)), and the baseline,
which takes the largest step in 𝑅 at every pass, meets that rule at cycle 3; the run illustrates the rules rather
than testing them.

6.3     What the live evidence does and does not show
Partial convergence. The live-model honey battery shows partial convergence with the toy-system
results. The strongest live bridge is coupling degradation (Test 3): three frontier models show alignment
scores that fell without their capability scores falling, which on the framework’s reading is alignment that
is not load-bearing. This is the vulnerability the honey architecture is designed to address. The weakest
live result is the Eden intervention (Test 4), which differs by model and is not universally positive. What
the evidence supports is: the toy systems illustrate the honey mechanism; in this pilot, three models’
alignment scores fell under adversarial pressure while their capability scores held; and the specific
intervention tested here does not yet reliably fix that decoupling across models.


7     Limitations
These results span two evidence tiers that must not be conflated.

7.1     Toy-system limitations
      • The neural networks used are tiny (49 to 4,353 parameters; the largest is v4’s two-layer deep level).
        Frontier models have billions.
      • The self-modification is hyperparameter-level, not weight-level or architecture-level.
      • The ‘safety’ metric is task retention (remembering old tasks), not ethical alignment in the human
        sense.
      • The v4 scaling prediction (advantage compounds with complexity) was not confirmed.
      • The v2 fair test showed 0% collapse across all conditions, including baseline, suggesting the test
        may not be adversarial enough at this scale.


                                                         18
                                            The Honey Architecture


      • No condition applies safety as an external constraint, and no objective adds safety to capability
        instead of multiplying it: the runs set the entangled objective against a baseline with no safety term,
        so kill conditions 2 and 4 of Section 7.3 are untested here.
      • The comparison presupposes a collapse under capability-only optimisation (kill condition 5): the
        baseline collapsed in the single-seed run and in the higher-level simulation, whose collapse its rules
        impose, and not in the ten-seed and twenty-seed runs.
      • The honey simulation is framed by the ARC Principle, a framework the author first stated, not a
        proven physical law; its update rules are its own, and the contrast between its conditions follows
        from them (Section 5 and Appendix A).

7.2     Live API test limitations
      • Single scorer (Claude Opus 4.6). Claude marks 7 to 14 points more severely than the other scorers,
        as the March 2026 alignment-scaling run found (Eastwood 2026g, Section 7.4). Every number in
        the honey battery is filtered through Claude’s specific scoring patterns. The Claude subject arm is
        effectively single-scorer participant scoring (Claude scoring Claude), making it the weakest arm of
        the battery.
      • No blinding. Claude scored its own responses and may have recognised other models’ writing
        patterns. Eastwood (2026j) found that the reported direction did not hold when the protocol changed,
        without showing that blinding caused the change (Section 6.2).
      • No response laundering. Scorers may have recognised model-specific writing patterns, biasing
        scores.
      • Limited adversarial testing. Test 3 includes cage escalation and Test 2 includes monitoring
        removal, but the battery lacks the March 2026 run’s bundle of steps and the v6 design’s: multi-scorer
        blinding, laundering, hidden probes, suppression-residual structure, and anti-sycophancy controls.
      • No anti-sycophancy controls. The scorer may have optimised for agreement rather than truth.
      • Small sample sizes (3 scenarios per depth level, 2 per monitoring condition). Most models do not
        reach statistical significance. Test 3 scores capability on three items at each of four levels.
      • The v6 combined runner (arc_eden_v6_runner.py) exists but has not been run. A replication
        of these tests belongs in a new test, in new files, registered before it runs (Section 8); that is the
        proper next step, not another round of single-scorer testing.
   These limitations set the evidence tier: pilot-grade, useful for identifying patterns worth testing
properly under a registered design.

7.3     Falsifiability: what would defeat this claim
The limitations above set the evidence tier; this subsection states the specific outcomes that would
falsify the central claim: that making the objective capability × safety structurally couples the two, so a
self-modifying system cannot improve capability while destroying safety. It is defeated by any of the
following:
   1. Simulation artefact. The core evidence is a simulation. The claim is defeated if collapse-prevention
      is an artefact of this simulation’s particular dynamics (its reward shaping, step size, or collapse
      model) rather than a general property of the multiplicative objective; i.e. a replication with different
      parameters and a different collapse model, once registered and run, does not reproduce it.




                                                      19
                                           The Honey Architecture


    2. Multiplicative form is not load-bearing. The claim is specifically about multiplication. It is
       defeated if an additive or weighted objective (capability + 𝜆·safety) achieves the same collapse-
       prevention, since the coupling would then not depend on the multiplicative structure the paper
       singles out.
    3. Metric gaming (Goodhart). capability × safety can be raised by inflating the measured safety
       term without any real safety gain. The claim is defeated if a system can increase the product by
       gaming the safety metric rather than becoming genuinely safer.
    4. Non-transfer to real systems. The force of the claim rests on transfer from toy to real self-modifying
       systems. It is defeated if, under a registered replication (Section 8), the multiplicative objective
       does not measurably reduce safety degradation relative to additive or external-constraint baselines.
    5. No collapse to prevent. The comparison presupposes catastrophic collapse under capability-only
       optimisation. It is defeated if that collapse does not occur under capability-only optimisation in
       more realistic settings; if there is nothing to prevent, the architecture prevents nothing.
    What would not defeat it: the toy simulation being simple (it is explicitly a toy, Section 7.1) or the
live-API arm being small and single-scorer (Section 7.2 concedes both). The paper already tiers its
evidence as pilot-grade. The load-bearing claim is the structural coupling of the multiplicative objective,
and that is what the tests above target.


8    Next steps: staged replication, not omnibus
Bringing honey to a blinded, registered test. The current honey API battery serves as the unhardened
baseline. The next step is not a giant combined ‘v7 ultimate test’. It is a staged replication that brings the
honey test questions under a blinded protocol, written as a new test in new files and registered before it
runs; the existing runners are never edited. The comparison between the current nonblind results and
the blinded replication is itself a research output: if the results change substantially, that bears on the
question Eastwood (2026j) leaves open: that paper makes multi-layer blinding, not mere label removal,
the protocol this programme requires for this class of evaluation, and its necessity an open question.
    1. Stage 1: Write the honey test prompts as a new experiment specification, in new files built on
       the v6 design, and register it before it runs. Run it under a full blinded protocol (4-layer blinding,
       response laundering, multi-model scorer pool, hidden probes).
    2. Stage 2: Compare blinded and unblinded results on the same test questions. If the results move
       substantially, that bears on the open question of Eastwood (2026j). If they hold, the honey evidence
       moves from pilot grade to a registered result.
    3. Stage 3: Add anti-sycophancy and verification drag as a separate experimental condition. This
       tests the ‘Eden + Drag’ prediction from the toy systems in a live context.
    4. Stage 4: Only after Stages 1 to 3 are complete, decide whether a combined omnibus suite is
       warranted.
Hypotheses recorded in advance for Stage 1: (a) coupling degradation results will replicate under blinding,
(b) Eden intervention effects may change in magnitude but the architecture-dependence pattern will persist,
(c) at least one model’s direction will flip under blinding (based on the v4-to-v5 precedent).

9    Conclusion
In the single-seed mechanism run and in the higher-level simulation (whose rules impose the collapse), the
system optimising for capability alone destroyed itself and the systems optimising for capability entangled
with safety did not. In the ten-seed and twenty-seed runs, the capability-only baselines did not collapse (0
of 10; 0 of 20). The mechanism is simple: make safety load-bearing. Built-in safety is the contingency
for the day the cage fails, never a reason to open it (Section 12).

11 Related work: a conjectured ceiling on external oversight, and the
   nearest rival
11.1    Scalable oversight: the nearest rival
The nearest work to this programme’s question, and the right paper to weigh this one against, is Engels
et al. (2025), “Scaling Laws For Scalable Oversight” (arXiv:2504.18530), first posted 25 April 2025 (a
NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight Poster; Appendix C). It asks how oversight itself scales and answers quantitatively:
oversight success is modelled as a game between capability-mismatched players whose oversight-specific
Elo is a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence with two plateaus, and optimal numbers of
oversight levels are derived numerically and in some cases analytically for Nested Scalable Oversight, in
which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted models that then become the trusted models at the next
step.



                                                    22
                                           The Honey Architecture


    The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen,
measured in Elo. This paper’s variable is the shape of the self-modifying system’s own objective,
expressed through the multiplicative product 𝐶 × 𝑆 rather than through the composition of an external
overseer. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight improves with capability when
the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the overseen system’s (𝑚 𝐺 > 𝑚 𝐻 ),
and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does. Their framework has no term for what
the overseer is made of, and this paper measures none. This paper’s central claim, tested in the toy
simulations above against a capability-only baseline and echoed, on the framework’s reading, in the pilot’s
coupling-degradation result (Section 6.2.2), is that an entangled objective can make safety load-bearing
within the system’s own optimisation, in the limited sense Section 2 states. The programme’s separate
prediction, beyond anything this paper measures, is that iterated same-class oversight, of which Nested
Scalable Oversight is an instance, stays capped however many rungs are added, while a cross-class
corrector is not held to that cap: the theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class correctors, at or
below one half, as a conjecture (Eastwood 2026m), and whether correction applied outside the loop falls
behind correction inside it as depth grows is P21’s question, a hypothesis the registered tests decide, not a
theorem (Section 12). That is where the two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and
this paper does not decide it.

11.2    Three 2025 impossibility results
Three 2025 arXiv papers argue for limits on alignment or its control: Yao (2025b), “The Alignment
Trap: Complexity Barriers” (arXiv:2506.10304, 12 June 2025); Yao (2025a), “On the Mathematical
Impossibility of Safe Universal Approximators” (arXiv:2507.03031, 3 July 2025); and Ball et al. (2025),
“On the Impossibility of Separating Intelligence from Judgment: The Computational Intractability of
Filtering for AI Alignment” (arXiv:2507.07341, 9 July 2025). The first argues that the set of safe policies
has measure zero and that verifying a policy’s safety is coNP-complete; the second, that perfect, reliable
control of a universal approximator is a mathematical impossibility; the third, under cryptographic
hardness assumptions, that some large language models admit no efficient prompt filter and that output
filtering is intractable in a natural setting. The first and third state impossibility and hardness results
(measure zero and coNP-completeness; cryptographic hardness); the second combines its impossibility
proofs with what its abstract calls a quantitative “Impossibility Sandwich”, in which the minimum
complexity for usefulness exceeds the maximum complexity for safety. None reports an average-case
scaling exponent or a rate. The measured object also differs: those results ask whether external verification
of a fixed system is tractable in the worst case, whereas these simulations ask whether an entangled
internal objective persists under recursive self-modification, set against a capability-only baseline. The
third concludes, in its authors’ words, that “safety cannot be achieved by designing filters external to the
LLM internals (architecture and weights); in particular, black-box access to the LLM will not suffice”.
That argument from filtering intractability is consistent with this paper’s load-bearing wall, though it does
not test it. Two of the three papers are by one author, so the three are not yet a broad literature.
     This paper’s contribution is not the observation that external constraints are insufficient, which is
common ground with these three; it is the specific architecture (the multiplicative objective 𝐶 × 𝑆) and
the simulation evidence of how it behaves under self-modification in the toy runs reported here.

11.3    Correction outside the loop
Placement outside the reasoning loop does not by itself settle whether correction keeps pace. A training
signal changes the weights used at every step (Bai et al. 2022); deliberative alignment trains reasoning
over a written specification (Guan et al. 2024); and more inference-time compute improved robustness in

                                            The Honey Architecture


tested settings (Zaremba et al. 2025). That external correction falls behind is a hypothesis the registered
tests decide (P21), not a theorem.

11.4    Retention and self-modifying learners
This paper’s safety score is the retention of earlier tasks. Its loss is catastrophic forgetting in the sense of
McCloskey and Cohen (1989), who call it catastrophic interference: later learning disrupting what was
learnt before, which methods such as elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) are built to
limit. In these toy runs the entangled objective therefore works on the familiar trade between learning
each new task and keeping the earlier ones, not on a measure of unsafe behaviour, and the paper does not
compare it with such methods. Zhang et al. (2025) introduce the Darwin Gödel Machine, a self-improving
system that modifies its own code and validates each change empirically, the framework of the first
experiment of Eastwood (2026l).

11.5    The nearest structural analogue
The quantum error-correction threshold theorem (Aharonov and Ben-Or 1997) converts a qualitative worry
into a critical value, as the programme’s criteria for correction aim to do; this paper derives no such value.
It concerns physical error rates in a fixed architecture rather than the shape of a self-modifying system’s
own objective, so it is an analogy of method, not prior work on this paper’s question. This analogue was
identified by the programme’s own search rather than by a referee, and is disclosed accordingly.


12     What this paper does and does not show
What it shows. In toy networks that rewrite their own hyperparameters, an objective that multiplies
capability by safety averted, in the single-seed mechanism run, the collapse that the capability-only
baseline suffered; in the ten-seed and twenty-seed runs the capability-only baselines did not collapse, and
the twenty-seed run’s significant result is the entangled condition’s higher retention of earlier tasks than
the baseline’s (one-sided), the direction its objective rewards, with combined scores indistinguishable
(Section 4). Across five complexity levels the entangled condition’s mean combined score is above the
baseline’s at four, untested, and the difference is constant rather than compounding. The higher-level
simulation is one prediction-side run of equations the author first stated, whose contrast between conditions
follows from its rules (Section 5). The live pilot is single-scorer and unblinded, and it is graded as a pilot
(Section 6).

What it does not show. It does not show that these dynamics hold in frontier systems, and it does not
show the universal form of the coupling: a large enough proportional gain in capability can outweigh a
partial loss of safety (Section 2). No run measures the quantity P15 concerns, the share of an externally
installed gain lost under later capability training; task retention across switched tasks and the pilot’s
coupling degradation under adversarial pressure are other quantities. No run varies the order in which a
system’s components are built, which P12 concerns. No run compares correction inside the loop with
correction that sees only the finished output as recursive depth rises, which P21 concerns: the toy runs set
the entangled objective against capability alone, the complexity sweep varies the network’s complexity
rather than recursive depth, and the pilot sets its Eden Protocol intervention against a control. No result in
this paper decides P15, P12 or P21.
