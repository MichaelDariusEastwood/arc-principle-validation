                                                        Abstract
           I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it
       reasons; only proof will move the world. This paper joins two programme models by resolving their
       notation and specifying a clock. On a fixed substrate, capability 𝐶 grows with recursive depth with
       exponent 𝛼 = 1/(1 − 𝛽 𝐿 ), while cycle duration scales as Δ𝑡 ∝ 𝐶 − 𝛿 . Their continuous-time growth
       exponent is exactly 𝑘 = 𝛿 − 1/𝛼. The boundary 𝛿 = 1/𝛼 separates power-law from super-exponential
       growth, with exponential growth at equality. In the gain-drift correction model, with positive drift and
       no level or compounding drift, the relative misalignment 𝐷/𝐶 tends to zero exactly when 𝛽 𝑋 > 𝑘.
       Absolute misalignment can still grow; neither quantity is a validated measure of harm. If correction
       and capability accumulate as aligned or asymptotic powers of one depth, 𝛽 𝑋 = (1 − 𝛽 𝐿 )/(1 − 𝛽′𝐿 ).
       The model’s hard-takeoff window with vanishing relative misalignment is 1/𝛼 < 𝛿 < 1/𝛼 + 𝛽 𝑋 , of
       width 𝛽 𝑋 . A bound of one on that width additionally requires the unproved ceiling 𝛽′𝐿 ≤ 𝛽 𝐿 . A
       constant throughput multiplier leaves the strict exponent criterion unchanged; an additive latency
       can change the growth regime. No paired measurement establishes these exponents here: Paper II’s
       reported error-decay estimates do not identify capability growth. Estimating 𝛿 requires an admissible
       loop, paired cycle times, an identified capability scale and a validated evaluation protocol. Frozen
       weights do not determine its sign. Finite observations can test a specified empirical model, but
       a falling error fraction alone cannot refute an asymptotic boundary. The governor discussion is a
       conditional modelling contribution, with historical priority unestablished.

1    Introduction
I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only
proof will move the world. The programme’s position is that people keep oversight, containment and
shutdown of advanced AI for as long as they physically can, and never give them up by choice; correction
built into a system is the contingency for control lost against our will, not a reason to let go of it. Whether
that contingency can keep pace with a system that improves itself is a question worth deciding with
mathematics before events decide it.
    A concrete question for a laboratory running a recursive improvement loop is whether effective
correction strengthens fast enough as the loop becomes more capable and its cycles shorten. A clock
relation can identify which quantities a measurement would need; it cannot certify a deployed system’s
safety.
    This paper joins two conditional models. Their criterion compares the growth exponent with the
correction exponent, under the drift assumptions stated in Section 4. The new clock variable is the
self-acceleration exponent 𝛿. Estimating it needs paired cycle durations and capability on an identified
scale; existing logs are sufficient only if they satisfy that measurement protocol. The resulting criterion
concerns a vanishing relative misalignment fraction, not vanishing absolute misalignment or harm. No
exponents are measured here. Section 12 separates mathematical counterexamples from empirical tests of
the models’ applicability.
    This paper belongs to the ARC Theory (the Theory of Artificial Recursive Creation), the founding
theory of Recursive Dynamics, the science of systems that improve themselves, which the author founded
(the founding paper). The theory is carried by five laws, each a law under test; its laws and twenty-two
propositions are registered on OSF (the theory registration), and the statement paper states the theory
whole. The two frameworks joined here are the scaling framework of Law I, the ARC Principle, set out in
the statement paper, and the corrective framework of Law II, the ARC Co-Scaling Law, set out in Paper X.
The paper decides none of the laws; Section 14 gives their status, and Appendix D places the paper in the
programme.
    Our contributions are:
    1. The identification and resolution of two notation collisions that kept the scaling framework of Law I
       and the corrective framework of Law II apart (Section 4).
    2. The clock relation 𝜏 = 𝛼 ln 𝑟, exact in the form its premise states (Result 1, Section 5).
    3. The correction exponent as a ratio of leverage complements, 𝛽 𝑋 = (1 − 𝛽 𝐿 )/(1 − 𝛽′𝐿 ), under
       scope condition S1, with a decision tree over the correction mechanisms modelled here and the
       conditional result that constant correction strength yields vanishing relative misalignment only
       under sub-exponential growth (Results 2a and 2, Section 6).
    4. The definition of the self-acceleration exponent 𝛿, the derivation 𝑘 = 𝛿 − 1/𝛼 and the threshold
       𝛿 = 1/𝛼 (Results 3 and 4, Section 7).
    5. The two-boundary structure 1/𝛼 < 𝛿 < 1/𝛼 + 𝛽 𝑋 and the identity of its gap with 𝛽 𝑋 (Results 5
       and 6), with the bound 𝛽 𝑋 ≤ 1 of Result 7 flagged as resting on a conjecture (Section 8).
    6. The distinction between a throughput multiplier and a cap on self-acceleration, with additive serial
       latency treated separately (Result 8, Section 9), and a scoped model of an embedded, mutable
       governor (Section 10).
    7. A measurement design for 𝛿, including inconclusive outcomes, and separate criteria for mathematical
       refutation and empirical model rejection (Sections 11 and 12).
    Section 2 sets the work against prior work and says what is not claimed as new. Section 3 states
the premise at fixed throughput, Section 4 resolves the notation and Section 5 derives the clock relation.

2     Related work, and what is not claimed
2.1   What is not claimed as new
Not claimed as new. The problem of making regulation adequate to the disturbances it faces is established.
Ashby (1956, Sections 11/5 to 11/11) bounds the variety of possible outcomes using the varieties of
disturbances and regulatory responses, under the stated restrictions on their mapping. Conant and Ashby
(1970, Sections 2 to 4) define success by minimum outcome entropy and show that a simplest optimal
regulator can map the reguland’s events to regulatory actions, for a specified outcome mapping and
distribution of system events. These are precise results about variety and modelling, not derivations
of this paper’s capability/correction exponent criterion. The scalable-oversight comparison is stated
separately in Section 2.2. The criterion 𝛽 𝑋 > 𝑘, in the gain-only drift model with positive gain drift,
belongs to the corrective framework paper in this programme; the present paper joins that model to
the stated growth and clock assumptions. Nor the scaling exponent 𝛼 = 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) in its physical form:
West, Brown and Enquist (1997) derive the three-quarter power law for metabolic rates from a model of
transport through space-filling fractal networks of branching tubes, and note that, because the network
must fill the entire 𝐷-dimensional volume, the result generalises to 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1); West, Brown and Enquist
(1999) obtain 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1) in 𝐷 dimensions again, from a more general model based on the geometry
rather than the hydrodynamics of hierarchical networks. Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) derive
a general relationship between size and flow rates in arbitrary networks with local connectivity, and
find that in the most efficient class of networks in 𝐷 dimensions metabolic rate scales as mass to the
power 𝐷/(𝐷 + 1), which gives quarter-power scaling for 𝐷 = 3; Banavar et al. (2010) show, in two
models of resource distribution networks, that the exponent of metabolic rate on body mass is 2/3 if the
velocity of flow remains constant and can attain a maximum of 3/4 if the velocity scales with its maximum
exponent, 1/12. Zhao (2022) derives the 𝑛/(𝑛 + 1) power scaling law for an 𝑛-dimensional system from
producer-consumer dynamics. Demetrius and Tuszynski (2010) derive the exponent 𝑑/(𝑑 + 1) for the
scaling of metabolic rate with body size in the regime they call quantum, where 𝑑 is the dimension of
the physical space in which their network of coupled energy-transducing oscillators is embedded; in the
regime they call classical the scaling is isometric. Bettencourt (2013) sets out a theoretical framework
that predicts the average social, spatial and infrastructural properties of cities as a set of scaling relations.
In it the land area of a city scales as its population to the power 𝐷/(𝐷 + 𝐻) in 𝐷 dimensions, where 𝐻
is the fractal dimension of travel paths; the exponent is 2/3 for 𝐷 = 2 and 𝐻 = 1, and it has the form
𝐷/(𝐷 + 1) only when 𝐻 = 1. Nor the mathematical study of governors’ disturbance stability, treated by
Maxwell (1868).
     The contribution made here. The paper identifies and resolves the two notation collisions; derives the
clock relation 𝜏 = 𝛼 ln 𝑟 under its stated premise; expresses the correction exponent as a ratio of leverage
complements under S1’s form condition; defines the self-acceleration exponent 𝛿 and derives 𝑘 = 𝛿 − 1/𝛼;
locates the boundary 𝛿 = 1/𝛼; and relates the gap between the two boundaries to 𝛽 𝑋 . Its governor
model applies that comparison to a corrector whose strength can change with the system it governs.

2.2   The rival instrument
Engels et al. (2025), “Scaling Laws For Scalable Oversight”, arXiv:2504.18530, first posted 25 April 2025
at 17:54:27 UTC (a time taken from arXiv’s record alone, arXiv Atom; a NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight Poster,
verified at the NeurIPS virtual page for poster 115536 on 15 August 2026 in the statement paper), stands
closest to the question this programme asks, and is the work against which this paper is best weighed.
It asks how oversight itself scales and answers quantitatively: oversight success is modelled as a game
between capability-mismatched players whose oversight-specific Elo is a piecewise-linear function of
general intelligence with two plateaus, and optimal numbers of oversight levels are derived numerically
and in some cases analytically for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger
untrusted models that then become the trusted models at the next step.
     The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen,
measured in Elo. This programme’s variable is the composition class of the corrector, measured through
the corrector’s own scaling exponent. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight
improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the
overseen system’s (𝑚 𝐺 > 𝑚 𝐻 ), and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does; that is a
comparison of two slopes, in the shape of the criterion 𝛽 𝑋 > 𝑘, though the objects differ. Their framework
has no term for what the overseer is made of: no substrate, no error-correlation structure, no reciprocal
identity between a correction exponent and a critical growth rate, and no architecture dependence. In
this paper that term is the substrate of the corrector’s own history, which separates classes 3 and 4 of
the corrector taxonomy (Section 6.2); it enters the results only through scope condition S1 and through
the substrate-ceiling conjecture behind Result 7. The programme’s separate conjecture, discussed in
Section 12.1, is a cap for correctors admitted as same-class by a frozen taxonomy. A stack of model
overseers is not classified by its number of rungs alone: external grounding and failure-mode dependence
are separate axes. The absence of that conjectured bound for a cross-class corrector would not establish
an advantage. The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class correctors, at or below one half,
as a conjecture on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III (the ARC Ceiling, a law under test), an
exponent the registration keeps distinct from Law II’s correction exponent, this paper’s 𝛽 𝑋 . That is where
the two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it.

2.3   Antecedents and near-misses
On the question. Hutter asked directly whether intelligence can explode (Hutter 2012, arXiv, 28 February
2012, read at its source), undertaking to separate speed from intelligence explosion and to consider
possible bounds on intelligence, augmenting the analysis of Chalmers (2010); the question itself goes back
to Good (1966). The distinction between speed and structure is therefore at least fourteen years old. This
paper does not establish that this literature lacks an architecture-dependent ceiling. Growth exponents of
the form used here are older: the scaling framework’s 𝛼 = 1/(1 − 𝛽 𝐿 ) is the exponent of the elementary
solution of a separable growth equation, the structure of the knowledge production function of Jones
(1995), whose long-run growth rate carries the factor 1/(1 − 𝜙). The open comparison is whether an
earlier source derives the same capability/correction relation under comparable assumptions; the present
contribution is the explicit model relation, not an established priority claim.

     On impossibility. Three 2025 arXiv papers argue for limits on alignment or its control: Yao (2025b),
“The Alignment Trap: Complexity Barriers” (arXiv:2506.10304, version 1 of 12 June 2025 at 02:30:30
UTC, a time taken from arXiv’s record alone, independently observed by the Internet Archive on 13 June
2025; cited by Spizzirri (2025)); Yao (2025a), “On the Mathematical Impossibility of Safe Universal
Approximators” (arXiv:2507.03031, 3 July 2025, the only paper in the arXiv abstract corpus containing
the phrase “irreducible uncontrollability”, abstract-search total of one, measured 12 August 2026); and
Ball et al. (2025), “On the Impossibility of Separating Intelligence from Judgment” (arXiv:2507.07341, 9
July 2025). All three are worst-case and qualitative: measure zero, coNP-completeness, cryptographic
hardness. None reports an average-case scaling exponent or a rate. The third concludes that “safety cannot
be achieved by designing filters external to the LLM internals (architecture and weights)” and argues that
“an aligned AI system’s intelligence cannot be separated from its judgment”; that argument from filtering
intractability is consistent with this programme’s architecture dependence, though it does not test it. Two
of the three are by one author. The measured object of all three is verification of a fixed system, and it is
closed within its own terms; this programme’s measured object is a different one, persistence of alignment
as capability compounds under recursive amplification, and closure of the first does not settle the second.
     On the mechanism. Anti-correlated estimates can reduce averaging variance more than independent
estimates. That observation does not show that sharing a substrate prevents anti-correlation, nor does an
uncertainty exponent determine a correction-capacity exponent. The programme’s same-class cap is a
conjecture with additional scaling and measurement premises (Section 12.1), and this paper does not
derive an architecture-wide impossibility of decorrelation.
     The nearest structural analogue. The quantum error-correction threshold theorem (Aharonov and
Ben-Or 2008) also converts a qualitative worry into a critical value. It concerns physical error rates in a
fixed architecture, not a corrector’s scaling exponent, so it is a near-miss rather than an occupant; the
analogy is one of method. This analogue was identified by the programme’s own search rather than by a
referee, and is disclosed accordingly.
     On recursive creation. Smolin’s cosmological natural selection (Smolin 1992) is the antecedent for
selection-shaped universes, and the programme’s own December-era notes cite it contemporaneously
(“Echoing Smolin’s cosmological natural selection, AI could create recursive universes with their own
laws”, read at its source in the author’s notes). This antecedent is noted for the wider programme’s parity;
it is not load-bearing for Paper XIII’s central results.
     No priority race is claimed. The competing elements, the reciprocal identity and the architecture
dependence, are 2026 work in this programme; the honest position against Engels et al and the impossibility
papers is the occupied-gap analysis of Section 2.2 and this section, not a race won on submission dates.
No margin in days against an arXiv date is quoted here.


3    The pipes premise, at fixed throughput
The fixed-substrate premise separates an artefact loop’s use of accumulated outputs from growth bought
by enlarging its hardware. It does not establish that software is the first or only system able to improve
at fixed throughput. Physical resource limits still apply; the astronomical example previously cited, the
Eddington limit, is a luminosity balance rather than a universal accretion ceiling (Bachetti et al. 2014).
     Biological evolution is an analogy here, not evidence for the model or a measured low correction
exponent. The theory registration explicitly places biological systems among those “for which the
framework offers an analogy and no measurement” (Eastwood 2026h). No biological exponent or
exhaustive classification of growth mechanisms is inferred in this paper.
     The operational target is an artefact loop on a fixed substrate, where each pass works on accumulated
output from previous passes. A model merely thinking longer does not establish this recursive mechanism.

The power-law equations are idealised limits: a finite machine has finitely many states and physical
processing limits (Margolus and Levitin 1998; Lloyd 2000). An unbounded limit in those equations is
therefore not a demonstration that a fixed physical device can sustain unbounded capability. An empirical
application must state the range over which its approximation is tested.
    Terminology note. The bare phrase “runaway growth” is not used in this paper without qualification:
in the physics corpus it denotes planetesimal accretion in planet formation. Where a runaway is meant
here, the phrase is “runaway self-improvement” or “uncorrectable growth”, per the terminology audit
summarised in Section 4.1.

10     The governor as a scoped modelling contribution
The mechanism considered here is a governor: a restraining mechanism whose effect changes with
the output it restrains. Watt’s centrifugal governor is the familiar mechanical illustration, with flyballs
responding to engine speed and adjusting the throttle. The comparison motivates a question about
recursive systems: can correction remain effective while both the governed system and the corrector
change?
    Maxwell (1868, pp. 270 to 272) studies regulation of machine speed under changing drive or
resistance. His stability test requires disturbances to decay; the characteristic roots’ real parts decide
whether motions decay or grow, including oscillatory motions. He also describes how stronger governing
action can produce growing oscillations. Wiener (1948, Chapter IV, pp. 95 to 98 of the second edition)
treats feedback of actual performance, including Watt’s governor, and the possibility that badly adjusted
feedback produces oscillation. These comparisons concern the dynamics of deviation from a controlled
quantity. Here 𝛽 𝑋 − 𝑘 compares the scaling of correction strength with the specific capability-growth rate,
within the stated drift model, and decides whether the relative misalignment 𝐷/𝐶 vanishes. It is not a
characteristic root, and that relative conclusion does not by itself bound the absolute burden 𝐷 (Section 4).

guarantee that the initial utility captures human values; off-policy behaviour lies outside that conclusion.
    Preserving a chosen objective is also distinct from accepting correction by people. Soares et al.
(2015, Sections 1.1, 2 and 4 to 6) state corrigibility desiderata that include accepting shutdown, avoiding
incentives to cause or prevent it, and passing shutdown behaviour to successors. Its examples expose
failures of candidate utility combinations, including utility indifference, rather than establish a general
solution. Orseau and Armstrong (2016, Assumption 9 and Theorems 14 and 17) prove asymptotic safe
interruptibility for Q-learning and a modified Sarsa rule under finite communicating Markov decision
processes, bounded rewards, the stated learning-rate conditions, a base policy greedy in the limit, and an
interruption-probability bound tending to one while every state-action pair remains infinitely explored.
The result concerns learning the non-interrupted task policy while an interruption mechanism remains
available; it does not guarantee objective preservation under arbitrary code changes, or alignment after
that mechanism is lost.

an exhaustive history nor a priority verdict for the joined exponent relation. Its positive distinction is
the question asked: how correction strength and capability growth scale together under the clock and S1
premises. Goal preservation, corrigibility and interruptibility impose additional requirements that the
exponent comparison alone does not settle. The scalable-oversight comparison in Section 2.2 supplies a
further, instrument-specific comparison.

11     Measuring the self-acceleration exponent
𝜹 is the negative slope of cycle duration against capability on logarithmic axes. It is a model parameter
with an operational interpretation, not an already validated instrument.
     A measurement needs an admissible self-improvement loop; a fixed or explicitly accounted substrate;
a definition of each cycle and its stopping rule; paired durations and a positive capability measure on an
identified scale; and an evaluation protocol protected against training leakage, changing difficulty and
score saturation. A held-out battery alone does not establish a ratio scale for capability. The chosen scale
and sensitivity to admissible alternatives must be declared before measurement. Regressing log Δ𝑡 on
log 𝐶 estimates the negative slope only where the functional form is adequate; uncertainty must account
for error in both variables, dependence across cycles and departures from a constant exponent. Attributing
the slope to endogenous improvement additionally requires separating external changes in resources or
procedures.
     Existing logs may be sufficient, but that is a condition to check. Recording time needs no special
clock. Identifying capability, the cycle and effective correction may require a new benchmark, instrument
or evaluation protocol. No census of available records is supplied here, so this paper claims neither that
suitable pairs are absent everywhere nor that every loop already records them. Suitable records could also
identify 𝛼 or 𝛽 𝑋 ; 𝛿 has no established exclusivity as the only accessible parameter.
     Frozen weights do not predict a nonpositive exponent. A fixed model or rule can generate
faster algorithms, reusable code or changing external memory, so effective cycle time may fall without
a weight update. Conversely, a self-modifying loop may slow down. Neither sign is forecast here.
Release-to-release intervals are not automatically the cycle times this model requires: changing resources,
evaluation, selection and release policy can confound them. No shortening trend or typical number of
releases is established in this paper.

12.1    The eclipse: falsification numbers beside the prediction
The same-class ceiling is a conjecture, not a consequence of shared substrate alone. P6 of the
theory registration, read at version 1.104, conjectures a correction-leverage exponent at or below one
half for correctors admitted as same-class by its frozen taxonomy. Independence of comparable readings
gives an uncertainty scaling in the number of readings. To obtain the registered capacity exponent, the
argument additionally needs capacity to be inverse uncertainty, uncorrelated channels of comparable
variance and quality, and channel count proportional to capability. The registration also requires the
inverse-uncertainty and misalignment-removed elasticities to agree within a registered equivalence margin
before one substitutes for the other. Shared substrate does not prove the needed error structure, and
independently reducing uncertainty does not by itself establish a bound on correction service.
    The class contrast and P6 have different verdict rules. The drafted contrast asks whether a
cross-class correction exponent exceeds a same-class one. Where the ratio is identifiable, the null ratio is
1.00. An interval merely containing 1.00 does not refute an advantage. A practical-equivalence verdict
requires the interval to lie wholly within a symmetric region fixed before data, with the unit of analysis,
sensitivity demonstration and separate support, refutation and inconclusive rules specified in the deciding
registration. A comparison too imprecise to distinguish the registered difference is inconclusive. No
numerical margin is supplied by this paper, and P8’s margin on an exponent is not transferred to a ratio.
Preliminary evidence described in earlier versions as adverse remains precursor evidence, not a verdict
under those rules.

15     Limitations
15.1    The burden, stated in the direction that costs the author
The following are material limits on applying the results. They are not an exhaustive list of possible
model failures:

                                        The self-acceleration exponent


   1. Effective correction does not satisfy S1. A fixed procedure can produce accumulating corrections;
      its fixed implementation does not settle the question. If the specified accumulation and form
      premises fail, Result 2 is inapplicable to that system.
   2. Iteration time does not follow a constant-exponent law. If Δ𝑡 falls in a way not captured by a
      single 𝛿, Result 3 holds only locally and the threshold is not a number but a moving target.
   3. The scaling framework’s exponent is not what governs real recursive capability growth. The
      reported error-decay regression estimate of 0.49 is a different estimand and cannot decide this
      capability-growth claim (Section 7.3). No paired measurement of the model’s capability-growth
      and cycle-time exponents is supplied here.
   4. The corrective framework’s two-variable model is too coarse. Its criterion may not survive a
      treatment with realistic state.
   5. 𝜹 may be unmeasurable in practice if capability and cycle time cannot be separated, because a
      cycle that also raises capability confounds both axes.
   6. The hard-takeoff window may never be reached, if no system sustains a sufficiently positive 𝛿.
      Relative correction can still fail below it when 𝛽 𝑋 ≤ 𝑘, so this does not make every corrective
      question disappear.
    The burden remains with the model application. Its premises and measurement mapping need
evidence; listing possible failures does not transfer that burden to a critic. A demonstrated derivation
error or failed registered model test requires the corresponding correction or rejection, recorded on the
programme’s public corrections page.

15.2    Scope, stated in one place
The results hold inside the stated models and are no general guarantee. Results 2, 5, 6 and 7 apply to
class 3 correctors only (scope condition S1); the core relative-correction conclusions use the standing
gain-only drift assumptions of Section 4, with equality and extended drift treated separately in Section 12;
every quantitative claim is in the substrate-fixed clock (Section 7); and Result 3 assumes a single constant
exponent for iteration time, from which an additive serial latency departs (Section 9.2). The cited test-time
estimates concern error decay and establish no value of the leverage fraction 𝛽 𝐿 (Section 7.2). Two
original conditions set for the paper’s deposit remain open (Section 14.1). The primary-source comparison
in Section 10.1 establishes the scope of the cited results; it establishes neither historical priority for this
model nor objective preservation, corrigibility or interruptibility for an implementation.


16     Conclusion
The clock relation 𝑘 = 𝛿 − 1/𝛼 connects two programme models. Under the stated constant-exponent
clock, 𝛿 = 1/𝛼 is the growth boundary. Under the separate gain-drift correction assumptions, 𝐷/𝐶 → 0
exactly when 𝛽 𝑋 > 𝑘. With S1’s form condition, the hard-takeoff window satisfying that relative criterion
has width 𝛽 𝑋 ; its conjectural bound of one needs the additional ceiling conjecture. These results do
not establish bounded absolute misalignment, a measure of harm or a universal corrector limit. Their
practical use is to identify quantities and assumptions a laboratory would need to test on an admissible
recursive improvement loop. No paired measurement is supplied here, frozen weights predict neither sign
of 𝛿, and finite observations require a registered empirical model test rather than an inference about an
unobserved limit.
