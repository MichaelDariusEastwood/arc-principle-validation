Abstract

I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only proof will move the world. This paper joins two programme models by resolving their notation and specifying a clock. On a fixed substrate, capability CC grows with recursive depth with exponent α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L), while cycle duration scales as Δt∝C−δ\Delta t \propto C^{-\delta}. Their continuous-time growth exponent is exactly k=δ−1/αk = \delta - 1/\alpha. The boundary δ=1/α\delta = 1/\alpha separates power-law from super-exponential growth, with exponential growth at equality. In the gain-drift correction model, with positive drift and no level or compounding drift, the relative misalignment D/CD/C tends to zero exactly when βX>k\beta_X > k. Absolute misalignment can still grow; neither quantity is a validated measure of harm. If correction and capability accumulate as aligned or asymptotic powers of one depth, βX=(1−βL)/(1−βL′)\beta_X = (1-\beta_L)/(1-\beta'_L). The model’s hard-takeoff window with vanishing relative misalignment is 1/α<δ<1/α+βX1/\alpha < \delta < 1/\alpha + \beta_X, of width βX\beta_X. A bound of one on that width additionally requires the unproved ceiling βL′≤βL\beta'_L \le \beta_L. A constant throughput multiplier leaves the strict exponent criterion unchanged; an additive latency can change the growth regime. No paired measurement establishes these exponents here: Paper II’s reported error-decay estimates do not identify capability growth. Estimating δ\delta requires an admissible loop, paired cycle times, an identified capability scale and a validated evaluation protocol. Frozen weights do not determine its sign. Finite observations can test a specified empirical model, but a falling error fraction alone cannot refute an asymptotic boundary. The governor discussion applies the exponent comparison, as a conditional model, to a corrector whose strength can change with the system it governs.

1 Introduction

I believe advanced AI is an existential risk unless values and correction are built into how it reasons; only proof will move the world. The programme’s position is that people keep oversight, containment and shutdown of advanced AI for as long as they physically can, and never give them up by choice; correction built into a system is the contingency for control lost against our will, not a reason to let go of it. Whether that contingency can keep pace with a system that improves itself is a question worth deciding with mathematics before events decide it.

A concrete question for a laboratory running a recursive improvement loop is whether effective correction strengthens fast enough as the loop becomes more capable and its cycles shorten. A clock relation can identify which quantities a measurement would need; it cannot certify a deployed system’s safety.

This paper joins two conditional models. Their criterion compares the growth exponent with the correction exponent, under the drift assumptions stated in Section 4. The new clock variable is the self-acceleration exponent δ\delta. Estimating it needs paired cycle durations and capability on an identified scale; existing logs are sufficient only if they satisfy that measurement protocol. The resulting criterion concerns a vanishing relative misalignment fraction, not vanishing absolute misalignment or harm. No exponents are measured here. Section 12 separates mathematical counterexamples from empirical tests of the models’ applicability.

2 Related work, and what is not claimed

2.1 What is not claimed as new

Not claimed as new. The problem of making regulation adequate to the disturbances it faces is established. Ashby (1956, sect. 11/5 to 11/11) bounds the variety of possible outcomes using the varieties of disturbances and regulatory responses, under the stated restrictions on their mapping. Conant and Ashby (1970, sect. 2 to 4) define success by minimum outcome entropy and show that a simplest optimal regulator can map the reguland’s events to regulatory actions, for a specified outcome mapping and distribution of system events. These are precise results about variety and modelling, not derivations of this paper’s capability/correction exponent criterion. The scalable-oversight comparison is stated separately in Section 2.2. The criterion βX>k\beta_X > k, in the gain-only drift model with positive gain drift, belongs to the corrective framework paper in this programme; the present paper joins that model to the stated growth and clock assumptions. Nor the scaling exponent α=d/(d+1)\alpha = d/(d+1) in its physical form: West, Brown and Enquist (1997) derive the three-quarter power law for metabolic rates from a model of transport through space-filling fractal networks of branching tubes, and note that, because the network must fill the entire DD-dimensional volume, the result generalises to D/(D+1)D/(D+1); West, Brown and Enquist (1999) obtain D/(D+1)D/(D+1) in DD dimensions again, from a more general model based on the geometry rather than the hydrodynamics of hierarchical networks. Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo (1999) derive a general relationship between size and flow rates in arbitrary networks with local connectivity, and find that in the most efficient class of networks in DD dimensions metabolic rate scales as mass to the power D/(D+1)D/(D+1), which gives quarter-power scaling for D=3D = 3; Banavar et al. (2010) show, in two models of resource distribution networks, that the exponent of metabolic rate on body mass is 2/3 if the velocity of flow remains constant and can attain a maximum of 3/4 if the velocity scales with its maximum exponent, 1/12. Zhao (2022) derives the n/(n+1)n/(n+1) power scaling law for an nn-dimensional system from producer-consumer dynamics. Demetrius and Tuszynski (2010) derive the exponent d/(d+1)d/(d+1) for the scaling of metabolic rate with body size in the regime they call quantum, where dd is the dimension of the physical space in which their network of coupled energy-transducing oscillators is embedded; in the regime they call classical the scaling is isometric. Bettencourt (2013) sets out a theoretical framework that predicts the average social, spatial and infrastructural properties of cities as a set of scaling relations. In it the land area of a city scales as its population to the power D/(D+H)D/(D+H) in DD dimensions, where HH is the fractal dimension of travel paths; the exponent is 2/3 for D=2D = 2 and H=1H = 1, and it has the form D/(D+1)D/(D+1) only when H=1H = 1. 2.2 The rival instrument

Engels et al. (2025), “Scaling Laws For Scalable Oversight”, arXiv:2504.18530, first posted 25 April 2025 at 17:54:27 UTC (a time taken from arXiv’s record alone, arXiv Atom; a NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight Poster, verified at the NeurIPS virtual page for poster 115536 on 15 August 2026 in the statement paper), stands closest to the question this programme asks, and is the work against which this paper is best weighed. It asks how oversight itself scales and answers quantitatively: oversight success is modelled as a game between capability-mismatched players whose oversight-specific Elo is a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence with two plateaus, and optimal numbers of oversight levels are derived numerically and in some cases analytically for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted models that then become the trusted models at the next step.

The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen, measured in Elo. This programme’s variable is the composition class of the corrector, measured through the corrector’s own scaling exponent. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the overseen system’s (mG>mHm_G > m_H), and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does; that is a comparison of two slopes, in the shape of the criterion βX>k\beta_X > k, though the objects differ. Their framework has no term for what the overseer is made of: no substrate, no error-correlation structure, no reciprocal identity between a correction exponent and a critical growth rate, and no architecture dependence. In this paper that term is the substrate of the corrector’s own history, which separates classes 3 and 4 of the corrector taxonomy (Section 6.2); it enters the results only through scope condition S1 and through the substrate-ceiling conjecture behind Result 7. The programme’s separate conjecture, discussed in Section 12.1, is a cap for correctors admitted as same-class by a frozen taxonomy. A stack of model overseers is not classified by its number of rungs alone: external grounding and failure-mode dependence are separate axes. The absence of that conjectured bound for a cross-class corrector would not establish an advantage. The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class correctors, at or below one half, as a conjecture on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III (the ARC Ceiling, a law under test), an exponent the registration keeps distinct from Law II’s correction exponent, this paper’s βX\beta_X. That is where the two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it.

2.3 Antecedents and near-misses

On the question. Hutter asked directly whether intelligence can explode (Hutter 2012, arXiv, 28 February 2012, read at its source), undertaking to separate speed from intelligence explosion and to consider possible bounds on intelligence, augmenting the analysis of Chalmers (2010); the question itself goes back to Good (1966). The distinction between speed and structure is therefore at least fourteen years old. This paper does not establish that this literature lacks an architecture-dependent ceiling. Growth exponents of the form used here are older: the scaling framework’s α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L) is the exponent of the elementary solution of a separable growth equation, the structure of the knowledge production function of Jones (1995), whose long-run growth rate carries the factor 1/(1−ϕ)1/(1-\phi). The open comparison is whether an earlier source derives the same capability/correction relation under comparable assumptions; the present contribution is the explicit model relation, not an established priority claim.

On impossibility. Three 2025 arXiv papers argue for limits on alignment or its control: Yao (2025b), “The Alignment Trap: Complexity Barriers” (arXiv:2506.10304, version 1 of 12 June 2025 at 02:30:30 UTC, a time taken from arXiv’s record alone, independently observed by the Internet Archive on 13 June 2025; cited by Spizzirri (2025)); Yao (2025a), “On the Mathematical Impossibility of Safe Universal Approximators” (arXiv:2507.03031, 3 July 2025, the only paper in the arXiv abstract corpus containing the phrase “irreducible uncontrollability”, abstract-search total of one, measured 12 August 2026); and Ball et al. (2025), “On the Impossibility of Separating Intelligence from Judgment” (arXiv:2507.07341, 9 July 2025). All three are worst-case and qualitative: measure zero, coNP-completeness, cryptographic hardness. None reports an average-case scaling exponent or a rate. The third concludes that “safety cannot be achieved by designing filters external to the LLM internals (architecture and weights)” and argues that “an aligned AI system’s intelligence cannot be separated from its judgment”; that argument from filtering intractability is consistent with this programme’s architecture dependence, though it does not test it. Two of the three are by one author. The measured object of all three is verification of a fixed system, and it is closed within its own terms; this programme’s measured object is a different one, persistence of alignment as capability compounds under recursive amplification, and closure of the first does not settle the second.

On the mechanism. Anti-correlated estimates can reduce averaging variance more than independent estimates. That observation does not show that sharing a substrate prevents anti-correlation, nor does an uncertainty exponent determine a correction-capacity exponent. The programme’s same-class cap is a conjecture with additional scaling and measurement premises (Section 12.1), and this paper does not derive an architecture-wide impossibility of decorrelation.

The nearest structural analogue. The quantum error-correction threshold theorem (Aharonov and Ben-Or 2008) also converts a qualitative worry into a critical value. It concerns physical error rates in a fixed architecture, not a corrector’s scaling exponent, so it is a near-miss rather than an occupant; the analogy is one of method. This analogue was identified by the programme’s own search rather than by a referee, and is disclosed accordingly.

On recursive creation. Smolin’s cosmological natural selection (Smolin 1992) is the antecedent for selection-shaped universes. Version 1.11 also quoted the author’s December-era notes on it; that quotation is kept in Appendix A.1 until its dated source is cited. This antecedent is noted for the wider programme’s parity; it is not load-bearing for Paper XIII’s central results.

No priority race is claimed. The competing elements, the reciprocal identity and the architecture dependence, are 2026 work in this programme; the honest position against Engels et al and the impossibility papers is the occupied-gap analysis of Section 2.2 and this section, not a race won on submission dates. No margin in days against an arXiv date is quoted here.

3 The pipes premise, at fixed throughput

The fixed-substrate premise separates an artefact loop’s use of accumulated outputs from growth bought by enlarging its hardware. It does not establish that software is the first or only system able to improve at fixed throughput. Physical resource limits still apply; the Eddington limit, an astronomical example, is a luminosity balance rather than a universal accretion ceiling (Bachetti et al. 2014).

Biological evolution is an analogy here, not evidence for the model or a measured low correction exponent. The theory registration explicitly places biological systems among those “for which the framework offers an analogy and no measurement” (Eastwood 2026k). No biological exponent or exhaustive classification of growth mechanisms is inferred in this paper.

The operational target is an artefact loop on a fixed substrate, where each pass works on accumulated output from previous passes. A model merely thinking longer does not establish this recursive mechanism. The power-law equations are idealised limits: a finite machine has finitely many states and physical processing limits (Margolus and Levitin 1998; Lloyd 2000). An unbounded limit in those equations is therefore not a demonstration that a fixed physical device can sustain unbounded capability. An empirical application must state the range over which its approximation is tested.

Terminology note. The bare phrase “runaway growth” is not used in this paper without qualification: in the physics corpus it denotes planetesimal accretion in planet formation. Where a runaway is meant here, the phrase is “runaway self-improvement” or “uncorrectable growth”, as Section 4.1 explains.

The two frameworks still cannot be compared, because kk is defined against wall-clock time and every quantity above is clock-free. Its usefulness side. The nonshrinking-cycle cap permits continued capability growth, as a power law in the substrate-fixed constant-exponent model. The calibrated cap also permits exponential growth at equality, δ=1/α\delta=1/\alpha. 10 The governor as a scoped modelling contribution

The mechanism considered here is a governor: a restraining mechanism whose effect changes with the output it restrains. Watt’s centrifugal governor is the familiar mechanical illustration, with flyballs responding to engine speed and adjusting the throttle. The comparison motivates a question about recursive systems: can correction remain effective while both the governed system and the corrector change?

Maxwell (1868, 270 to 272) studies regulation of machine speed under changing drive or resistance. His stability test requires disturbances to decay; the characteristic roots’ real parts decide whether motions decay or grow, including oscillatory motions. He also describes how stronger governing action can produce growing oscillations. Wiener (1948, IV, pp. 95 to 98 of the second edition) treats feedback of actual performance, including Watt’s governor, and the possibility that badly adjusted feedback produces oscillation. These comparisons concern the dynamics of deviation from a controlled quantity. Here βX−k\beta_X-k compares the scaling of correction strength with the specific capability-growth rate, within the stated drift model, and decides whether the relative misalignment D/CD/C vanishes. It is not a characteristic root, and that relative conclusion does not by itself bound the absolute burden DD (Section 4).

Earlier work already addresses changing regulation and self-modification, with different objects and guarantees. That observation concerns the regulator’s mapping and the outcome entropy; it does not fix a correction-strength exponent or a recursive cycle time.

Yudkowsky and Herreshoff (2013, sects 2, 3 and 7) study agents that approve similarly designed successors with shared goals. It is a restricted formal treatment of goal-preserving succession, not a measured correction-scaling result. Everitt et al. (2016, Assumption 8 and Theorem 16) treat policies and utilities that can be modified. With modification-independent belief and initial utility, and an initially optimal policy for the realistic value function, the agent anticipates modifications and evaluates their consequences using its current utility. The theorem preserves initial-utility optimality along the histories that the evolving policy produces. It permits value-equivalent changes and gives no guarantee that the initial utility captures human values; off-policy behaviour lies outside that conclusion.

Preserving a chosen objective is also distinct from accepting correction by people. Soares et al. Its examples expose failures of candidate utility combinations, including utility indifference, rather than establish a general solution. Orseau and Armstrong (2016, Assumption 9 and Theorems 14 and 17) prove asymptotic safe interruptibility for Q-learning and a modified Sarsa rule under finite communicating Markov decision processes, bounded rewards, the stated learning-rate conditions, a base policy greedy in the limit, and an interruption-probability bound tending to one while every state-action pair remains infinitely explored. The result concerns learning the non-interrupted task policy while an interruption mechanism remains available; it does not guarantee objective preservation under arbitrary code changes, or alignment after that mechanism is lost.

This bounded comparison, checked against these primary texts on 1 November 2026, supplies neither an exhaustive history nor a priority verdict for the joined exponent relation. Its positive distinction is the question asked: how correction strength and capability growth scale together under the clock and S1 premises. Goal preservation, corrigibility and interruptibility impose additional requirements that the exponent comparison alone does not settle. The scalable-oversight comparison in Section 2.2 supplies a further, instrument-specific comparison.

10.2 An embedded objective as a candidate, without elimination of alternatives

  1. External correction may become inadequate if its effective strength fails to keep pace. That is a conditional statement about scaling, not a general impossibility result for external oversight.

  2. A governor exposed to modification may be weakened, preserved or improved. Reachability alone decides none of these outcomes; the permitted changes and the objective governing them must be modelled.

  3. An objective of the form C×SC \times S is one candidate for making the measured safety term consequential to optimisation. At fixed positive CC, reducing SS reduces that product. If CC also changes, the product can increase while SS falls, and the objective’s own preservation is a further premise.

The contribution is the conditional exponent comparison applied to this design question. Where an implementation satisfies S1, Result 2 relates correction to capability, and Results 5 and 6 state the correctable window in the model. An entangled objective is one candidate implementation; no elimination argument proves it uniquely necessary or proves that its removal must degrade capability. Whether an implementation occupies class 3 and preserves its correction remains to be measured.

People keep oversight, containment and shutdown for as long as they physically can. Embedded correction is the contingency for control lost against our will (Section 1), and this modelling argument supplies no reason to relinquish those controls.

11 Measuring the self-acceleration exponent

δ\delta is the negative slope of cycle duration against capability on logarithmic axes. It is a model parameter with an operational interpretation, not an already validated instrument.

A measurement needs an admissible self-improvement loop; a fixed or explicitly accounted substrate; a definition of each cycle and its stopping rule; paired durations and a positive capability measure on an identified scale; and an evaluation protocol protected against training leakage, changing difficulty and score saturation. A held-out battery alone does not establish a ratio scale for capability. The chosen scale and sensitivity to admissible alternatives must be declared before measurement. Regressing log⁡Δt\log\Delta t on log⁡C\log C estimates the negative slope only where the functional form is adequate; uncertainty must account for error in both variables, dependence across cycles and departures from a constant exponent. Attributing the slope to endogenous improvement additionally requires separating external changes in resources or procedures.

Existing logs may be sufficient, but that is a condition to check. Recording time needs no special clock. Identifying capability, the cycle and effective correction may require a new benchmark, instrument or evaluation protocol. No census of available records is supplied here, so this paper claims neither that suitable pairs are absent everywhere nor that every loop already records them. Suitable records could also identify α\alpha or βX\beta_X; δ\delta has no established exclusivity as the only accessible parameter.

Frozen weights do not predict a nonpositive exponent. A fixed model or rule can generate faster algorithms, reusable code or changing external memory, so effective cycle time may fall without a weight update. Conversely, a self-modifying loop may slow down. Neither sign is forecast here. Release-to-release intervals are not automatically the cycle times this model requires: changing resources, evaluation, selection and release policy can confound them. No shortening trend or typical number of releases is established in this paper.

Possible interpretations, conditional on an admissible measurement:

  • An interval wholly at or below zero finds no positive compression exponent in the measured window under its uncertainty model. A strictly negative value describes slowing cycles, outside Definition 1’s nonnegative branch; the clock relation still extends algebraically. It is not evidence that self-acceleration is impossible in other systems or later windows.

  • A positive exponent below 1/α1/\alpha places the fitted model in its power-law regime. Both parameters and their joint uncertainty must be identified before that ordering is asserted.

  • Equality at 1/α1/\alpha is the exponential model boundary, and an exponent above it is in the super-exponential model regime. A finite interval cannot establish exact equality. Registered equivalence or separation rules are needed for empirical decisions near the boundary.

  • An interval spanning zero or a relevant boundary, insufficient precision, or a failed scale or model check leaves the corresponding classification inconclusive. No point estimate or uninformative interval is converted into a verdict.

These are interpretations, not a replacement for a registration’s frozen admission, estimation and verdict rules. The relative-correction comparison needs βX\beta_X in every growth regime, together with its uncertainty and the standing drift assumptions. Absolute burden and harm require their own outcomes.

Measuring δ\delta can locate a system relative to the model’s growth boundary once α\alpha is identified. No algebra forces δ\delta to rise before a correction failure, since βX\beta_X and other model conditions may also change. The paired observable is not a validated warning system by itself.

12 Falsification criteria

Two different objects can fail. A mathematical result is refuted by a counterexample satisfying every stated premise or by an error in its derivation. An empirical application is rejected when its specified observable consequences fail under a valid, adequately precise protocol. A finite record cannot establish an infinite-capability limit. Before a measurement supplies a verdict, its registration must fix admission, estimands, observation range, uncertainty, rejection and inconclusive rules; no new numerical decision rule is introduced here.

F1.

A corrector that does not satisfy S1’s accumulation and form premises is outside the correction-ratio join. Results 2, 5, 6 and 7, as joined through S1, are not predictions for it. The underlying clock identity and the separately specified corrective model can still be assessed on their own premises.

F2.

An admissible S1 system with βL′>βL\beta'_L>\beta_L would contradict the additional substrate-ceiling conjecture for that class. Empirical rejection requires a registered comparison on the same depth, identified scales and comparable regimes, with uncertainty resolving the inequality. Shared physical substrate alone is insufficient admission. This does not refute the width identity without that ceiling.

F3.

A mathematical solution with the standing gain-drift assumptions, δ>1/α+βX\delta>1/\alpha+\beta_X and D/C→0D/C\to0 would refute the claimed asymptotic boundary. A fraction that falls over a finite observation window does not do so: a decreasing transient can approach a positive floor. An empirical test must instead specify a finite-window prediction of the fully parameterised model, including initial conditions and coefficients, and reject that application if its registered criterion is met. Neither falling relative error nor bounded relative error establishes vanishing absolute burden.

F4.

For fixed exponents with dC/dr=aC1−1/αdC/dr=aC^{1-1/\alpha} and dt/dr=ℓC−δdt/dr=\ell C^{-\delta}, a,ℓ>0a,\ell>0, the chain rule gives Result 3. Changing substrate, a varying exponent or a failed continuous-depth approximation breaks a premise.

12.1 The eclipse: falsification numbers beside the prediction

The same-class ceiling is a conjecture, not a consequence of shared substrate alone. P6 of the theory registration, read at version 1.104, conjectures a correction-leverage exponent at or below one half for correctors admitted as same-class by its frozen taxonomy. Independence of comparable readings gives an uncertainty scaling in the number of readings. To obtain the registered capacity exponent, the argument additionally needs capacity to be inverse uncertainty, uncorrelated channels of comparable variance and quality, and channel count proportional to capability. The registration also requires the inverse-uncertainty and misalignment-removed elasticities to agree within a registered equivalence margin before one substitutes for the other. Shared substrate does not prove the needed error structure, and independently reducing uncertainty does not by itself establish a bound on correction service.

The class contrast and P6 have different verdict rules. The drafted contrast asks whether a cross-class correction exponent exceeds a same-class one. Where the ratio is identifiable, the null ratio is 1.00. An interval merely containing 1.00 does not refute an advantage. A practical-equivalence verdict requires the interval to lie wholly within a symmetric region fixed before data, with the unit of analysis, sensitivity demonstration and separate support, refutation and inconclusive rules specified in the deciding registration. P6’s replicated refuter, exactly scoped. Under version 1.104, the same corrector’s interval must lie wholly above one half on the first fault set and on a second set analysed alone: the same correction type, the same construction and settings, the same window and the first set’s registered level. 15 Limitations

15.1 The burden, stated in the direction that costs the author

The following are material limits on applying the results. They are not an exhaustive list of possible model failures:

  1. Effective correction does not satisfy S1. A fixed procedure can produce accumulating corrections; its fixed implementation does not settle the question. If the specified accumulation and form premises fail, Result 2 is inapplicable to that system.

  2. Iteration time does not follow a constant-exponent law. If Δt\Delta t falls in a way not captured by a single δ\delta, Result 3 holds only locally and the threshold is not a number but a moving target.

  3. The scaling framework’s exponent is not what governs real recursive capability growth. The reported error-decay regression estimate of 0.49 is a different estimand and cannot decide this capability-growth claim (Section 7.3). No paired measurement of the model’s capability-growth and cycle-time exponents is supplied here.

  4. The corrective framework’s two-variable model is too coarse. Its criterion may not survive a treatment with realistic state.

  5. δ\delta may be unmeasurable in practice if capability and cycle time cannot be separated, because a cycle that also raises capability confounds both axes.

  6. The hard-takeoff window may never be reached, if no system sustains a sufficiently positive δ\delta. Relative correction can still fail below it when βX≤k\beta_X\le k, so this does not make every corrective question disappear.

The burden remains with the model application. Its premises and measurement mapping need evidence; listing possible failures does not transfer that burden to a critic. A demonstrated derivation error or failed registered model test requires the corresponding correction or rejection, recorded on the programme’s public corrections page.

15.2 Scope, stated in one place

The results hold inside the stated models and are no general guarantee. Results 2, 5, 6 and 7 apply to class 3 correctors only (scope condition S1); the core relative-correction conclusions use the standing gain-only drift assumptions of Section 4, with equality and extended drift treated separately in Section 12; every quantitative claim is in the substrate-fixed clock (Section 7); and Result 3 assumes a single constant exponent for iteration time, from which an additive serial latency departs (Section 9.2). The cited test-time estimates concern error decay and establish no value of the leverage fraction βL\beta_L (Section 7.2). Two original conditions set for the paper’s deposit remain open (Section 14.1). The primary-source comparison in Section 10.1 establishes the scope of the cited results; it establishes neither historical priority for this model nor objective preservation, corrigibility or interruptibility for an implementation.

16 Conclusion

The clock relation k=δ−1/αk=\delta-1/\alpha connects two programme models. Under the stated constant-exponent clock, δ=1/α\delta=1/\alpha is the growth boundary. Under the separate gain-drift correction assumptions, D/C→0D/C\to0 exactly when βX>k\beta_X>k. With S1’s form condition, the hard-takeoff window satisfying that relative criterion has width βX\beta_X; its conjectural bound of one needs the additional ceiling conjecture. These results do not establish bounded absolute misalignment, a measure of harm or a universal corrector limit. Their practical use is to identify quantities and assumptions a laboratory would need to test on an admissible recursive improvement loop. No paired measurement is supplied here, frozen weights predict neither sign of δ\delta, and finite observations require a registered empirical model test rather than an inference about an unobserved limit.
