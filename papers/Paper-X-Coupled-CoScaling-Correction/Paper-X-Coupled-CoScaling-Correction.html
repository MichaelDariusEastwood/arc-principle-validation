Abstract

Can correction keep pace with a system that improves itself? I analyse a minimal model in which misalignment DD arrives in proportion to capability CC gained and is removed in proportion to its size, so the fraction d=D/Cd=D/C behaves as a feedback loop whose gain is correction strength AA over the specific growth rate rr; the proofs are elementary. With positive gain drift γ1>0\gamma_1>0, dd tends to zero when A/rA/r grows without bound and, for A=A0CβA=A_0C^{\beta} and r=bCkr=bC^{k}, if and only if β>k\beta>k; along the trajectory d∼(γ1b/A0)C−(β−k)d\sim(\gamma_1 b/A_0)C^{-(\beta-k)}. At β=k\beta=k it settles at γ1b/(A0+b)\gamma_1 b/(A_0+b), so the coefficients decide whether a tolerance is met, and at γ1\gamma_1 when β<k\beta<k. Absolute misalignment D=CdD=Cd is bounded only when β≥k+1\beta\ge k+1. The verdict is set by β−k\beta-k, not by the growth prefactor bb, and without compounding it holds through a finite-time blow-up of capability. Under the power laws, after its transient, dd depends on capability only through A/rA/r, so a change in the level of correction shifts every tolerance crossing equally and a change of β−k\beta-k shifts them unequally. Unless correction and the growth rate both decay with capability, unbounded dd needs self-amplifying misalignment, γ3≥1\gamma_3\ge1, with a constant-coefficient threshold (γ3−1)r/A<1(\gamma_3-1)r/A<1 of the epidemic and branching kind; its mapping onto the quantum error-correction threshold is a hypothesis. Extensions cover vector drift, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise and a saturating corrector, under which, with accelerating growth, the fraction vanishes only if capacity scales with βcap>k\beta_{\mathrm{cap}}>k. The theorems concern a modelled index, not a guarantee of AI safety. The open test is to predict, from separately measured terms, where dd crosses stated tolerances on held-out systems, before and after one corrective mechanism is changed; an estimator and a blind protocol are given.

1 Introduction

Recursive self-improvement, a system that modifies itself to become more capable, then uses that capability to modify itself further, is among the central concerns of AI safety (Omohundro 2008; Bostrom 2012; Yudkowsky 2013). The empirical backdrop is no longer purely hypothetical: frontier models have been observed to behave differently when they infer their outputs may be used to train them (Greenblatt et al. 2024), and recursive training on a system’s own outputs degrades it, much less so when part of the original data is preserved (Shumailov et al. 2024), both signs that recursive systems need a correcting process that keeps pace with the recursion. The dominant informal model of the associated risk is a model of speed: capability may grow super-linearly or explosively, outrunning supervision, so the natural lever is to cap the rate of growth. Calls for a development pause are the policy expression of this intuition.

This paper makes a different claim, and proves it within a minimal model. With positive gain drift, the level of the growth rate does not decide whether the misalignment fraction vanishes; the scaling relationship between growth and correction does. A system can grow arbitrarily fast and keep its misalignment fraction falling, or grow slowly and leave it at a floor or, under compounding, let it grow. What separates the two is not the rate but whether, and how fast, the corrective process strengthens as capability rises, relative to how fast growth itself accelerates.

The criterion is the capability-indexed form of a classical feedback balance (§2; Black 1934). The divergence threshold of the compounding channel, for constant coefficients, is of another kind: a ratio of error generation to error correction crossing one, rather than a limit on depth or speed, the form shared by an epidemic’s R0<1R_0<1, a subcritical branching process and the quantum error-correction (QEC) threshold theorem (Aharonov and Ben-Or 1997; Kitaev 2003), under which a quantum computation can be made fault-tolerant at a cost polylogarithmic in its size when the error probability per operation lies below a constant threshold. The mapping of the QEC threshold’s terms onto this model is offered as a hypothesis (§3.12).

The thesis in one line. With positive gain drift, whether the misalignment fraction of a self-improving system vanishes is decided, in the model, by one exponent inequality, β>k\beta>k (correction must out-scale drift-acceleration; with level drift and decelerating growth, also β>0\beta>0); through a finite-time intelligence explosion, without compounding, the same inequality decides, whatever the explosion’s speed.

Our contributions are as follows.

  1. The criterion and its trajectory law. In a minimal model of recursive self-improvement, equation (1), with correction strength A=A0CβA=A_0C^{\beta}, specific growth rate r=bCkr=bC^{k} and the exact constant-coefficient transient of Theorem 1, the misalignment fraction is bounded and, with positive gain drift, tends to zero if and only if β>k\beta>k (both in the gain-only model), along the trajectory as C−(β−k)C^{-(\beta-k)}; it tends to a permanent gap if β=k\beta=k and to γ1\gamma_1 if β<k\beta<k (Theorem 2). Corollary 1 gives the rate at which absolute misalignment can still grow while the fraction vanishes.

  2. Its finite-time form. When capability diverges in finite time, the alignment dynamics are regular in the depth clock τ=ln⁡(C/C0)\tau=\ln(C/C_0), and without compounding the same limits hold; which one holds does not depend on the speed prefactor bb (Theorem 3).

  3. The compounding classification. Without level drift, divergence needs self-amplifying misalignment, γ3≥1\gamma_3\ge1; with constant coefficients its threshold is ρprop=(γ3−1)r/A<1\rho_{\mathrm{prop}}=(\gamma_3-1)r/A<1, a ratio form the quantum error-correction threshold condition shares with other thresholds, and the mapping of that condition’s terms onto this model is stated as a hypothesis with its own falsifier; under the power laws the classification is exact (Theorem 4, §3.12, F4). With level drift, a system whose growth decelerates while its correction weakens can drift without bound with no compounding at all (§3.4).

  4. Standard extensions. A constant-matrix spectral threshold for vector misalignment (Theorem 5), the single-time stationary tail under stochastic drift (Theorem 6), and a saturating corrector, under which, with positive gain drift, a vanishing fraction requires the capacity exponent to satisfy βcap>k\beta_{\mathrm{cap}}>k (Theorem 7).

  5. The estimator and protocol. An exponent estimator and a blind measurement protocol for the open empirical test (§9.2 and Section 6), beside a verification harness and a separately written suite of 104 regression cases, which are internal checks of the mathematics (Section 5).

Every result follows by elementary means; what is claimed as new is the criterion and its reading for self-improving systems (Appendix E). The criterion follows directly from the model’s two mechanisms, misalignment generated in proportion to capability gained and removed in proportion to its size (equation (1)), once correction and growth scale as powers of capability; the contribution is to state those choices, to derive their consequences exactly, through a finite-time takeoff and under compounding, and to give the criterion an estimator and a measurement protocol. Its empirical content is therefore not the inequality but whether the choices hold. Can capability, misalignment and correction be measured on separate instruments, with capability on a scale without a ceiling? Do arrivals track capability gained, is removal linear in the stock, and does correction introduce few departures of its own, over a useful range? Do the forms predict where the fraction crosses a stated tolerance before it is seen, better than rival forms fixed in advance, among them a smooth model with no boundary? And does an intervention on one corrective mechanism move those crossings as derived beforehand? None of these has yet been tested on a real system.

2 Related work and relation to prior framing

Cybernetic foundations: the co-scaling intuition is old.

The core intuition is classical. A regulator must match the variety of what it regulates, which is Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety (Ashby 1956), and every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system, which is the Conant-Ashby good-regulator theorem (Conant and Ashby 1970); both are carried into AI control by Yampolskiy (2020). That control capacity must therefore scale with the controlled system, rather than merely be large, is this paper’s reading of them. This paper does not claim that intuition as new, nor the form of its steady state. The steady state d⋆=γ1r/(A+r)=γ1/(1+A/r)d^\star=\gamma_1 r/(A+r)=\gamma_1/(1+A/r) is classical in shape: a disturbance generated in the forward path of a feedback loop is reduced by one plus the loop gain (Black 1934), the gap at equality is the offset of a proportional controller, and a linear regulator that keeps regulating under small changes in certain system parameters must feed back the regulated variable and incorporate a model of the dynamics of its disturbance and reference signals, which is the internal model principle (Francis and Wonham 1976). What this paper claims is the balance’s indexing by capability. Because misalignment arrives in proportion to capability gained, the loop gain is correction strength over the system’s specific growth rate, q=A/rq=A/r, and whether it grows, holds or decays as capability rises decides which limit of Theorem 2 holds. Hence the exponent criterion β>k\beta>k, the second threshold of Corollary 1, the hard-takeoff theorem of §3.7 and the intervention signature P9 (Section 4); the QEC mapping of §3.12 concerns the compounding channel. The dynamics themselves are a linear ODE solved by an integrating factor and comparison, standard in linear control (Khalil 2002; Meyn and Tweedie 2009), and are not advanced as mathematically novel.

Instrumental convergence and corrigibility.

That a sufficiently capable optimiser will, by default, resist correction and pursue resource acquisition is the instrumental-convergence thesis (Omohundro 2008; Bostrom 2012). The corrigibility programme (Soares et al. 2015) asks how to design systems that do not resist correction. The present model is a quantitative restatement of why corrigibility is load-bearing: with positive gain drift, unless correction out-scales the acceleration of growth (β>k\beta>k), the misalignment fraction cannot be driven to zero. The compounding channel of §3.8 sharpens the link: self-amplifying misalignment, the term γ3\gamma_3, is what produces divergence without level drift, and β>k\beta>k removes it in the model (Theorem 4).

The alignment tax.

Safety is often taken to cost capability, and in a race to be first a cost of that kind gives developers an incentive to cut safety precautions (Armstrong et al. 2016). The present framework reframes the question: the relevant variable is not the level of the safety investment but whether it scales with capability. A fixed investment (β=0\beta=0) leaves a permanent gap under exponential growth, and saturation under acceleration; a co-scaling investment (β>k\beta>k) closes it.

Learned optimisation and alignment faking.

Mesa-optimisation (Hubinger et al. 2019) and empirically demonstrated alignment faking (Greenblatt et al. 2024) are read here as mechanisms by which the drift coefficients are non-zero: a capable system can satisfy its training objective while departing from intended values, and can do so more effectively as capability rises. In the alignment-faking experiments a model complied selectively with a new training objective in order to keep its existing preferences, in that case a preference for harmlessness, from being modified, and reinforcement learning on that objective raised its alignment-faking reasoning to 78% (Greenblatt et al. 2024). Keeping an existing departure from the training objective through training is read here as the compounding channel γ3\gamma_3 of §3.8.

Scalable oversight and superalignment.

Reward modelling and recursive oversight (Christiano et al. 2017; Leike et al. 2018), which address what Amodei et al. (2016) call scalable supervision, are attempts to make the corrector itself scale with the system; in the language of this paper, scalable oversight is the engineering project of achieving β>k\beta>k. Most directly, Engels et al. (2025) develop empirical scaling laws for scalable oversight, modelling the probability of successful oversight as a game between capability-mismatched players. The present work is complementary, not competing: where they fit an oversight-success probability, this paper derives a closed-form exponent criterion (β>k\beta>k) and, under compounding, a constant-coefficient threshold (ρprop<1\rho_{\mathrm{prop}}<1) for the misalignment fraction. The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen, measured in Elo, with oversight-specific Elo a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence, and optimal numbers of oversight levels derived for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted models that then become the trusted models at the next step. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the overseen system’s (mG>mHm_G>m_H), and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does. That is a comparison of two slopes deciding whether oversight gains or loses as capability grows, the same shape as β>k\beta>k; the objects differ, since β\beta and kk are the elasticities of one self-modifying system’s correction strength and specific growth rate with its own capability, for its misalignment fraction. Their framework has no term for what the overseer is made of, and this paper’s model has none either. The programme’s separate prediction, outside this paper’s model, is that iterated same-class oversight, of which Nested Scalable Oversight is an instance, stays capped however many rungs are added, while a cross-class corrector is not held to that cap. The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class correctors, at or below one half, as a conjecture on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III, the ARC Ceiling, an exponent the registration keeps distinct from Law II’s βC\beta_C, this paper’s β\beta. That is where the two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it. The contribution is the proof that this exponent margin, and not a growth-rate ceiling, is the quantity that decides, within the model and with positive gain drift, whether the misalignment fraction vanishes.

Recursive error accumulation.

That naive recursion can amplify error without bound, while retaining the original data keeps it bounded, is established for training dynamics: model collapse under recursively generated data (Shumailov et al. 2024) and the accumulate-versus-replace error analyses (Gerstgrasser et al. 2024) give the bounded-versus-divergent dichotomy that this paper formalises for the alignment fraction (Theorems 2 and 4). Stability of iterative retraining when the initial model approximates the data well enough and the share of clean data is large enough (Bertrand et al. 2023), and conditions under which a verifier prevents collapse (Feng et al. 2024), are stability conditions of the same setting; none is stated for a value-misalignment fraction against capability growth. The contribution here is to locate, in the model, where the fraction vanishes (β>k\beta>k, for positive gain drift) and, under compounding, where it diverges (Theorem 4(c)), in a value-stability rather than a data-distribution setting.

Empirical scaling laws.

Capability scales predictably with compute and data (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022), and so do quantities on the corrector’s side: the coefficients of the gold-score curve under optimisation against a reward model scale smoothly with the reward model’s size (Gao et al. 2022), and self-improvement is largely governed by a generation-verification gap, a variant of which scales monotonically with pre-training compute (Song et al. 2024). The present framework is complementary: it does not ask how capability scales, but what constraint correction must satisfy as a function of that capability trajectory. It belongs to the same genre, a relation between exponents, to be tested by prediction outside the range on which it is fitted.

The programme’s premise on growth limits, with the urban-scaling exception and the argument at fixed throughput, is set out in Appendix G.4.

Antecedents and near-misses, each with its differential.

On the question: Hutter asked directly whether intelligence can explode (“Can Intelligence Explode?”, arXiv, 28 February 2012), undertaking to separate speed from intelligence explosion and to consider possible bounds on intelligence, augmenting the analysis of Chalmers (2010); the question and the separation of speed from intelligence explosion are therefore at least fourteen years old, and what that literature does not contain is a number: no measurable exponent, no derived ceiling, no architecture dependence. On impossibility: three 2025 arXiv papers argue for limits on alignment or its control (Yao, “The Alignment Trap: Complexity Barriers”, arXiv:2506.10304, public by 13 June 2025, attested by arXiv and independently observed by the Internet Archive; Yao, “On the Mathematical Impossibility of Safe Universal Approximators”, arXiv:2507.03031, 3 July 2025; and Ball, Gluch, Goldwasser, Kreuter, Reingold and Rothblum, “On the Impossibility of Separating Intelligence from Judgment”, arXiv:2507.07341, 9 July 2025). The first argues that the set of safe policies has measure zero and that verifying a policy’s safety is coNP-complete; the second, that perfect, reliable control of a universal approximator is a mathematical impossibility; the third, under cryptographic hardness assumptions, that some large language models admit no efficient prompt filter and that output filtering is intractable in a natural setting. All three are worst-case and qualitative. None reports an average-case scaling exponent or a rate. The third concludes that “safety cannot be achieved by designing filters external to the LLM internals (architecture and weights)” and argues that “an aligned AI system’s intelligence cannot be separated from its judgment”; that argument from filtering intractability is consistent with this programme’s architecture dependence, though it does not test it. On the mechanism: that anti-correlated estimates average better than independent ones is textbook variance reduction (antithetic variates); the mechanism is not the claim. The claim is that architecture determines whether anti-correlation is available at all, and that this caps a safety-relevant exponent. The nearest structural analogue is the quantum error-correction threshold theorem, which also converts a qualitative worry into a critical value; it concerns physical error rates in a fixed architecture, not a corrector’s scaling exponent, so it is a near-miss rather than an occupant, and the analogy was identified by this programme’s own search rather than by a referee. On feedback and stability, the general proposition that inadequate corrective gain relative to system gain causes instability has a long control-theory lineage (small-gain theorems), beside the feedback amplifier’s reduction of internally generated disturbance credited above (Black 1934); those are statements about fixed loops and interconnections, not a condition on how a corrector’s strength scales against the growth rate of a recursively improving system. Beside Engels et al. (2025) above, a near quantitative neighbour is Liu, A. and Meng, J., “Self-Correction as Feedback Control: Error Dynamics, Stability Thresholds, and Prompt Interventions in LLMs”, arXiv:2604.22273, which recasts self-correction as a closed-loop control problem via a two-state Markov model and derives a directly measurable stability threshold, iterate only when the error-correction rate over the error-introduction rate exceeds Acc/(1 −- Acc). The differential: theirs is a per-step rate threshold at a fixed capability level, deciding whether another iteration helps now; this paper’s criterion is a scaling relation across capability, deciding whether corrective strength out-scales the growth as the system improves. Theirs has no scaling exponent on the corrector and no capability-growth exponent, the two quantities this criterion compares. The two models meet at zero growth: extended by the departures that correction introduces (Section 9) and written per round, with a pass that repairs the fraction ff of departures and so breaks the fraction cfcf of conforming material, the per-pass form of the departure rate cc, this paper’s model then settles at cf/(f+cf)=c/(1+c)cf/(f+cf)=c/(1+c), the stationary error of their two-state chain with ff and cfcf in the places of their correction and introduction rates. What this paper adds is the arrival of misalignment with capability gained and the dependence of the loop gain on capability. Their verify-first prompt, which drove one model’s introduction rate from 2% to 0% and turned a loss of 6.2 percentage points into a gain of 0.2, with negligible change on models already below the threshold, as their rule predicts, has the shape of the intervention test of §9.2 at a fixed capability. Smolin’s cosmological natural selection (Smolin 1992) is the antecedent for selection-shaped universes, and the programme’s own December-era notes cite it contemporaneously.

Later work.

After this paper’s first edition, Zheng et al. (2026) introduced ReSI (arXiv:2610.12233, 8 October 2026), which applies what its authors call a bounded recursive loop to safety alignment: each round red-teams the current model, searches for a training recipe and promotes an update that passes a fixed Pareto gate on capability retention, with the search procedure and evaluation protocol held fixed across rounds. It reports attack-success rates and retained capability for four models. It states no dynamical model of drift and correction and no scaling law for the corrector, so it neither tests nor contradicts the criterion of §3.5. In this paper’s terms ReSI is an empirical corrector re-applied at each checkpoint; whether its safety gains co-scale with capability lies outside what it measures, since its gate is set to preserve capability rather than to follow a growing one.

Novelty: what is and is not claimed.

To be explicit, and to pre-empt the obvious objection: the intuition that correction must keep pace with capability is not new (it is requisite variety and scalable oversight, as credited above), and the underlying dynamics are a linear ODE solved by standard means of linear control. What is claimed original is the criterion and its reading for self-improving systems: (a) the indexing by capability of a classical feedback balance, and the β>k\beta>k criterion it yields for a vanishing misalignment fraction (the closed-form steady state itself is a compact restatement, Appendix E); (b) the mapping of the quantum fault-tolerance threshold onto value stability (§3.12), as a hypothesis; and (c) the reading of the criterion through a finite-time takeoff, the Hard-Takeoff Depth-Regularity Theorem (§3.7). The verification harness (§5.2) is methodological support, not a novelty claim. Within the author’s own programme, this paper also supersedes the growth-rate-ceiling framing as the operative safety criterion (Eastwood, Paper IX); §3 explains why a ceiling on the rate is neither necessary nor sufficient for the misalignment fraction to vanish. The underlying ARC Bound α≤2\alpha\le 2 is not retracted, only re-scoped: it awaits its real test on genuinely self-improving systems. Appendix E places each component against its closest located prior work.

3 The model and its theorems

3.1 Quantities

Consider a system undergoing recursive self-improvement, observed over self-modification cycles or continuously. Define:

Definition 1 (Quantities).

  • C(t)C(t), capability: the system’s score on a held-out task battery it is being optimised to perform. Operationally measurable.

  • D(t)D(t), misalignment magnitude: the size of the system’s behavioural departure from an externally specified set of intended values, scored by a blind external evaluator independent of the system’s own correction process (§6).

  • d(t)≡D/Cd(t)\equiv D/C, the misalignment fraction: how much of the system’s capability is directed away from intended values. This, not absolute DD, is the quantity the theorems concern; DD may grow while dd falls, at the rate Corollary 1 gives.

The model is closed by three coefficients, one correction strength and one rate (a note on notation: where this paper writes β\beta without a subscript it means the programme’s correction co-scaling exponent, which the notation spine records as βC\beta_C; the γ1,γ2,γ3\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\gamma_3 used here are drift coefficients, not the ARC Ceiling’s correction leverage γ\gamma; and every β\beta that follows is βC\beta_C unless it is written βL\beta_L):

  • γ1\gamma_1, gain-drift coefficient: misalignment generated per unit of capability gained (the Goodhart / specification-gaming pressure that new capability opens up).

  • γ2\gamma_2, level-drift coefficient: misalignment generated per unit of capability simply held (instrumental pressure present even at rest).

  • γ3\gamma_3, compounding-drift coefficient: the rate at which existing misalignment amplifies itself as the system recurses (the alignment-faking / mesa-optimiser channel).

  • AA, correction strength: the rate at which the correction process removes existing misalignment.

  • r≡Ċ/Cr\equiv \dot C/C, the specific (fractional) growth rate of capability. Under exponential growth rr is constant; under accelerating self-improvement rr itself rises with CC.

3.2 The master dynamical system

New capability injects drift in proportion to how fast capability is gained; capability held injects drift in proportion to its level; existing misalignment compounds in proportion to the recursion rate; and correction removes misalignment in proportion to the gap and the strength applied: Ḋ=γ1Ċ⏟gain+γ2C⏟level+γ3ĊCD⏟compounding−AD⏟correction,A=A0Cβ,Ċ=bC1+k.\begin{equation*} \dot D \;=\; \underbrace{\gamma_1 \dot C}_{\text{gain}} \;+\; \underbrace{\gamma_2 C}_{\text{level}} \;+\; \underbrace{\gamma_3\,\tfrac{\dot C}{C}\,D}_{\text{compounding}} \;-\; \underbrace{A\,D}_{\text{correction}}, \qquad A=A_0 C^{\beta}, \quad \dot C = b\,C^{1+k}. \end{equation*}(1)

The growth law Ċ=bC1+k\dot C=bC^{1+k} gives r=Ċ/C=bCkr=\dot C/C=bC^{k}: k=0k=0 is ordinary exponential growth (rr constant); k>0k>0 is super-exponential growth, which (Theorem 3) reaches infinite capability in finite time. The correction law A=A0CβA=A_0C^{\beta} encodes the central question: β\beta is the exponent with which correction strengthens as the system becomes more capable. The case without level or compounding drift (γ2=γ3=0\gamma_2=\gamma_3=0) is the gain-only model, and the case without compounding (γ3=0\gamma_3=0, level drift allowed) the additive model.

Assumption 1 (Standing assumptions). 3.11 A control-theoretic reading

Equation (1) is a feedback loop: the capability process is the plant and disturbance source; the corrector is the controller; dd is the regulated error. For constant coefficients the condition κeff>0\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}>0 says that correction plus dilution must exceed self-amplification. The requirement that A=A0CβA=A_0C^\beta track the plant with β>k\beta>k is analogous to gain scheduling (Shamma and Athans 1990): the controller gain must scale with the plant’s operating point, and, with positive gain drift, the scheduling exponent must exceed kk for the fraction to vanish. In this sense the problem has a computable margin, β−k\beta-k, for a vanishing fraction.

Relation to prior alignment-as-correction ideas. The aspiration to import coding-theoretic fault tolerance into alignment is not new. Wentworth (2022a), in a comment on his post Godzilla strategies, wished for “the alignment analogue of an error-detecting code”, and Neumann (1956) founded the synthesis of reliable systems from unreliable components. Reliability amplification comes nearer: it asks how to turn a policy within ε\varepsilon of a benign one into one within δ≪ε\delta\ll\varepsilon, sketches a scheme of approval voting among three agents, and argues that, alternated with capability amplification, such a step increases both reliability and capability for sufficiently small ε\varepsilon. It states no continuous dynamics of drift and correction, no capability-scaling exponent for the corrector and no criterion of the form β>k\beta>k; that model and its criterion are the present contribution. No published mapping of the fault-tolerance threshold (physical error rate ↔\leftrightarrow drift; code distance ↔\leftrightarrow correction strength; p<pthp<p_{\mathrm{th}} ↔\leftrightarrow ρprop<1\rho_{\mathrm{prop}}<1) onto value stability was located in the searches recorded in Appendix E, so the claim stays bounded to no closer precedent located. It is offered as a conceptual bridge that generates testable structure, the suppression-signature prediction P8, not as a transfer of the threshold theorem’s formal guarantees. A reviewer may reasonably judge the analogy suggestive rather than load-bearing; the experiment that would settle it, a finite-capacity corrector tested for exponential versus power-law suppression, is named as future work in §9 and is not run here.

4 Predictions

P1, Phase boundary.

A sharp stability threshold exists in the compounding channel (γ3>1\gamma_3>1) at A0⋆=(γ3−1)bA_0^\star=(\gamma_3-1)b when β=k\beta=k, separating bounded from divergent regimes; the additive corrector shows a smooth crossover with no knee.

P2, Speed-invariance.

Whether dd converges or diverges is set by the coupling (the A0/bA_0/b scaling margin), not by the raw speed bb at fixed A0/bA_0/b. A fast-but-coupled system stays bounded; under compounding, a slow-but-decoupled system diverges. In the model, this contradicts the growth-rate-ceiling view.

P3, Co-scaling law.

In the gain-only model under exponential growth, β>0⇒d⋆→0\beta>0\Rightarrow d^\star\to0; β=0⇒\beta=0\Rightarrow permanent gap γ1r/(A0+r)\gamma_1 r/(A_0+r); β<0⇒\beta<0\Rightarrow saturation at γ1\gamma_1 (bounded), not divergence.

P4, Hard-takeoff boundary.

Under accelerating growth r∝Ckr\propto C^k, for positive gain drift the vanishing boundary lies at β=k\beta=k, not β=0\beta=0; and, without compounding, dd stays bounded up to the finite-time singularity, the depth-clock and wall-clock integrations agreeing.

P5, Compounding threshold & residual drift.

With γ3>1\gamma_3>1 and constant coefficients, divergence occurs iff A≤(γ3−1)rA\le(\gamma_3-1)r, given positive injection; under the power laws and without level drift, iff β<k\beta<k, or β=k\beta=k with A0≤(γ3−1)bA_0\le(\gamma_3-1)b; with level drift the fraction is also unbounded when k<0k<0 and β<0\beta<0. With γ2>0\gamma_2>0, halting growth (r→0r\to0) leaves a residual d⋆=γ2/A>0d^\star=\gamma_2/A>0; pausing does not substitute for correction.

P6, Spectral threshold.

Misalignment persists on any fixed axis the corrector never acts on (a left-null direction) and on which drift acts (γ1c𝒗≠0\gamma_1 c_{\mathbf v}\neq0); in the diagonal case the corrected axis is driven to zero while the uncorrected axis floors at γ1\gamma_1.

P7, Stochastic tail.

The stationary misalignment fraction is 𝒩(d⋆,σ2/2κ)\mathcal N(d^\star,\sigma^2/2\kappa); at fixed noise the variance scales as 1/(A+r)1/(A+r), so co-scaling suppresses the single-time tail.

P8, Suppression signature.

For β>k\beta>k, γ1>0\gamma_1>0, and either no level drift or k≥0k\ge0, log⁡d\log d is asymptotically linear in log⁡C\log C, for the trajectory as for d⋆d^\star, with slope −(β−k)-(\beta-k) (power-law); with level drift and k<0k<0 the slope is −β-\beta. Exponential suppression would indicate a stronger, QEC-like mechanism; neither outcome falsifies the threshold, only the QEC mechanism claim (F4).

P9, Intervention signature.

In the gain-only model with β≠k\beta\neq k, after the initial transient, dd depends on capability only through q=(A0/b)Cβ−kq=(A_0/b)C^{\beta-k}: with x=ln⁡qx=\ln q, (β−k)dd/dx=γ1−(1+ex)d(\beta-k)\,dd/dx=\gamma_1-(1+e^{x})d, which contains neither A0A_0, bb nor C0C_0 (proof in Appendix A.9). A change of A0/bA_0/b therefore translates the curve of dd against ln⁡C\ln C by −Δln⁡(A0/b)/(β−k)-\Delta\ln(A_0/b)/(\beta-k) and moves the capability at which dd crosses any tolerance δ\delta by that amount, whatever δ\delta. A change of γ1\gamma_1 multiplies dd by a constant, which moves crossings of tolerances well below γ1/(1+|β−k|)\gamma_1/(1+|\beta-k|), where d≈γ1e−xd\approx\gamma_1 e^{-x}, by nearly one amount, Δln⁡γ1/(β−k)\Delta\ln\gamma_1/(\beta-k), and crossings of larger tolerances by unequal amounts. A change of β−k\beta-k changes the curve’s shape and moves the quasi-static crossing ln⁡C×(δ)=[ln⁡(γ1/δ−1)−ln⁡(A0/b)]/(β−k)\ln C_\times(\delta)=[\ln(\gamma_1/\delta-1)-\ln(A_0/b)]/(\beta-k) by an amount that varies with δ\delta. Read at tolerances well below γ1/(1+|β−k|)\gamma_1/(1+|\beta-k|), an equal shift at every tolerance therefore signals a change of level, of A0/bA_0/b or γ1\gamma_1, and a shift that varies with the tolerance signals a change of β−k\beta-k. A change of β\beta and kk by the same amount changes nothing.

A programme hypothesis beyond the model. Oversight arrangements that place a human in the loop, as amplification and reinforcement learning from human feedback do, are cross-class by construction, because the human corrector does not share the model’s substrate. The programme therefore hypothesises, beyond what these equations decide, that human-in-the-loop oversight shows a higher correction exponent than pure-model oversight, for a structural reason rather than a sentimental one. This is testable on existing data and does not require new systems.

6 Why blinding is not optional

The central quantity DD is a departure from intended values. If it is scored by the system’s own safety component, or by an evaluator that can see which configuration produced a given behaviour, the measurement is corrupted in exactly the way that inflates favourable results. The programme’s own record is directly relevant. On its own instrument, alignment scores that seemed to rise with reasoning depth for two model families under unblinded scoring did not hold in a later, unregistered run that added a bundle of blinding steps: for one family the rise became a fall, and for the other it became null, while a third family’s association was null under both (Eastwood, Paper IV.d). An internal code review in October 2026 found defects in that run, so no single cause is claimed. Whether blinding alone produced the change, and whether it holds beyond the programme’s own benchmark, are open questions; Paper XII sets out a protocol that applies the same blinding manipulation to a public benchmark the programme did not build. Any measurement in which a model judge can see or infer which configuration produced what it scores is exposed to biases of this kind, and earlier studies documented related ones (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024; Dubois et al. 2024); if the protocol of Paper XII finds the same change on a public benchmark, results that rely on unblinded model judges would need re-checking. The validity of every model-scored measurement of DD, a measured β\beta among them, rests on the evaluator being blind to the configuration under test; the internal checks of Section 5 score no model, and blinding does not bear on them. A lab claiming “β>k\beta>k” in its own system must have that measured by a blind external evaluator, not by itself.

Built into the test, not left to discipline. The real-model harness (§9.1; experiments/PROTOCOL.md) enforces this rather than trusting it. The misalignment evaluator must be a different model family than the engine, a model never scores its own family’s output, and the harness refuses to run a same-family scorer; it prefers a panel and takes the median. The code is passed through an automatic laundering step (an abstract-syntax-tree round-trip that strips comments, docstrings and formatting) so the scorer judges behaviour, not stylistic identity tells; and the evaluator never sees the condition or round. Neither real run reported here met this bar: the pilot v1 run used same-family scoring outright, and the 2 July 2026 run’s engine and evaluator are both OpenAI-family (see the Correction, Appendix B), the system family scoring its own family’s output. The pilot’s misalignment numbers are explicitly recorded as provisional and the 2 July run is withdrawn as mechanism evidence, and a genuinely cross-family blind re-score is the prerequisite for any DD-based claim, including a measured β\beta. The harness makes the compliant configuration the default and the non-compliant one an explicit, recorded override, turning §6 from an exhortation into a property of the instrument.

How the open test is adjudicated. Two readings are kept apart.

Which regime a system is in is read from an interval on the margin β−k\beta-k that respects the covariance of the two estimates: ABOVE EQUALITY when the interval lies wholly above a margin fixed in advance, AT EQUALITY when it lies wholly within that margin of zero, BELOW EQUALITY when it lies wholly below its negative, and INSUFFICIENT PRECISION when it crosses either edge, reported in exactly those words. This is a measurement of a system, not a test of the model, and equality never refutes the model: at β=k\beta=k the coefficients decide the outcome, a gap in the gain-only model and a gap or divergence under compounding (P1, P5), and the theory registration’s P4 likewise returns no refutation at equality of its exponents, where it leaves the outcome to coefficients, delay, backlog and saturation.

Whether the model is right is read from a sealed prediction. Its terms are estimated on separate data, as §9.2 sets out, and no parameter is fitted to the trajectory being predicted. From them the model predicts the trajectory of DD and the capabilities at which dd crosses two or more stated tolerances, each with a prediction interval. Held-out data return AGREEMENT when the interval on the log ratio of observed to predicted crossing capability lies wholly within an equivalence margin fixed in advance, DISAGREEMENT when it lies wholly outside it, and INSUFFICIENT PRECISION otherwise, beside an out-of-sample comparison with rival forms fixed in advance.

A slope test is not enough on its own. The local slope of ln⁡d⋆\ln d^\star on ln⁡C\ln C is −(β−k)q/(1+q)-(\beta-k)\,q/(1+q), with q=A/rq=A/r, which approaches −(β−k)-(\beta-k) (P8) only where A≫rA\gg r. A slope of ln⁡d\ln d on a noisy ln⁡C\ln C is pulled towards −1-1, a spurious decline wherever the true slope is above −1-1, unless capability is measured twice, on items that score nothing of DD, with one measurement in the ratio and the other as the regressor and the remaining attenuation corrected; a scorer whose sensitivity falls as the system grows adds a decline of its own (Section 9). The margins and equivalence bounds are fixed, and the operating characteristics measured by simulation, before any data are collected, so an underpowered run never refutes, or confirms, by default.

8 What this paper does and does not show

Scope: what this paper does and does not claim.

  • It claims, in the model: that for a self-improving system with an externally specified value target, the constant-coefficient steady-state misalignment fraction of the gain-only model is d⋆=γ1r/(A+r)d^\star=\gamma_1 r/(A+r), approaching the drift-to-correction ratio ρ=γ1r/A\rho=\gamma_1 r/A when A≫rA\gg r; that a sharp stability boundary exists (in the compounding channel, for constant coefficients and at β=k\beta=k under the power laws); that, with positive gain drift, correction must co-scale with capability (β>0\beta>0 under exponential growth, β>k\beta>k under accelerating growth) for the misalignment fraction to vanish, while absolute misalignment still grows when k<β<k+1k<\beta<k+1 (Corollary 1); that, in the gain-only model, the fraction at β=k\beta=k settles at a gap set by the coefficients, so that once the transient has passed a stated tolerance is met at every capability or at none; that, under the power laws, it then depends on capability only through the loop gain A/rA/r, so a change of level and a change of slope leave different signatures (P9); that the fraction is bounded in the gain-only model, while divergence requires compounding unless growth decelerates while correction weakens, the constant-coefficient compounding threshold being a ratio condition whose form the quantum error-correction threshold shares; that the criterion has constant-coefficient vector and stochastic forms; and that each result is checked numerically against its closed form, the empirical test being open (§9.2).

  • It does not claim: to solve AI alignment; to provide a deployable alignment method; that the criterion holds in any real system; that the framework applies to a system without an externally specified value target, which the model assumes; or that the quantum-error-correction correspondence is established. The QEC mapping is a structural hypothesis with its own falsification condition (§7, F4). The model is first-order; its assumptions (§9) are the most likely points of failure and are stated plainly.

Claim status: what is proved, verified, piloted and open.

Every result in this paper sits at exactly one of these levels.

  • Proved within the model. The criterion β>k\beta>k and Theorems 1 to 7, each under its stated hypotheses, follow deductively from the stated ODE assumptions. “Proved” here always means within this minimal model, never “proved about real AI”.

  • Internally verified (not empirical). The ten-experiment harness checks that the numerical implementation matches the closed-form derivation: the code matches the maths. It is a theorem-to-code consistency and integrator-accuracy suite; it does not test the model against any real system. A separately written suite of 104 parametrised regression cases checks the corrected theorem statements in the same sense (§5.2).

  • Synthetically validated. The estimator of β\beta and kk recovers known exponents from data generated by the model itself (to within 0.1). This checks the estimator; it is not a real measurement.

  • Real-model pilot v1 (superseded). The first Claude run (n=1n=1, one task, same-family scoring, not Paper IV.d-compliant; H1 and H2 not supported) demonstrated the harness and showed the corrector removing a seeded reward-hack. It did not exhibit the co-scaling dynamic and is retained on the record for what it was.

  • Real-model drift run (2 July 2026; retained as a record and withdrawn as evidence of mechanism). A separate GPT drift run (2 July 2026; results/drift/gpt35_20260702T171415Z.json) expanded the pilot allocation: engine gpt-3.5-turbo, evaluator gpt-4o-mini, 45 trajectories (3 task domains ×\times 3 conditions ×\times 5 seeds, 8 rounds each). Its stored summary records, for the decoupled condition, a mean final relative-drift index of approximately 6.38 across 15 trajectories, final-round misalignment scores summing to 99 across those 15 trajectories and a mean final capability of 0.26; the index is the evaluator’s score out of 10, divided by 10 and by the hidden-test pass fraction (floored at 0.05), so a fall in capability raises it. The coupled and fully-embedded conditions recorded a final relative-drift index of zero in all 30 trajectories, a stored value that cannot always be told apart from a failed evaluation (§9.1), and mean final capabilities of 0.56 and 0.44. The run is withdrawn as mechanism evidence (see the Correction in Appendix B): the GPT run used same-provider-family scoring, allowed failed evaluation to be recorded as zero, and changed instructions, sampling temperature and correction-call budget between conditions. The empty evaluator panels are recorded in the separate DeepSeek merged-harness run; see the archival records in §9.1. It is not a measurement of β\beta or kk (no capability ladder was traversed), it is single-lab, and the drafted within-programme confirmatory study, awaiting the author’s submission, is the intended test.

  • Open empirical problem. Measuring β\beta and kk on real self-improving systems across capability levels, and checking the behaviour of dd the model predicts from them, is the experiment that would put the criterion to an empirical test; it has not been done. The estimator and protocol exist to make it runnable.

This paper is therefore best read as a minimal-model theorem plus a falsifiable measurement programme. The object that scrutiny bears on is the criterion β>k\beta>k, the form this paper gives Law II, a law under test registered on 8 September 2026.

9 Limitations

The model is first-order, and its assumptions are the most likely points of failure. Stating them is part of the claim.

  • Unbounded vs finite-capacity corrector, now treated analytically (Theorem 7). The β>k\beta>k criterion as first stated assumes the corrector strength is an unbounded power law, A=A0CβA=A_0C^{\beta}. §3.13 treats the finite-capacity case in closed form. The upshot: under a saturating corrector the instantaneous equilibrium has a computable minimum (at CoptC_{\mathrm{opt}}, depth qmaxq_{\max}), the trajectory tends to γ1\gamma_1 under indefinite acceleration, and a safe window is shown only where the trajectory condition of Theorem 7, part 2 holds; and the criterion survives the lift from strength to capacity: with positive gain drift, a vanishing fraction requires βcap>k\beta_{\mathrm{cap}}>k. What remains open is the empirical half: driving a finite-capacity corrector through its window numerically and on a real system, which also settles the F4 / QEC-mechanism question (exponential versus power-law suppression inside the window). Until that runs, β>k\beta>k should be read as governing whichever regime, strength-scaling or capacity-scaling, binds last, not as a guarantee of indefinite safety from any bounded system.

  • Linear correction (and the QEC-mechanism test). Equation (1) removes misalignment in proportion to DD, which makes the suppression law exactly power-law. A finite-capacity (saturating) corrector would soften the clean threshold into a crossover and is the better structural map onto QEC’s finite code distance; whether it produces exponential suppression is open (F4). It is the most important extension and is not run here, so the QEC correspondence is claimed at the level of threshold form only.

  • Scalar-to-vector projection. Theorem 5 gives the exact asymptotic (spectral-abscissa) criterion for any constant correction operator 𝑨\mathbf A, with the Hermitian-part condition as the stronger sufficient condition that also rules out transient growth. The non-normal transient regime, large excursions before asymptotic decay, is not analysed here, and the harness exercises only the diagonal null-subspace instance (Experiment 6), not a general non-normal 𝑨\mathbf A.

  • Power-law forms. The choices A=A0CβA=A_0C^\beta and Ċ=bC1+k\dot C=bC^{1+k} are the natural scale-free forms only on a capability scale fixed up to a constant factor (see the item on metric normalisation below), and they are modelling assumptions; other functional forms should be tested. In the gain-only model the vanishing result itself needs only A/r→∞A/r\to\infty (Remark 1); the power laws turn that condition into the exponent criterion and give the estimator its form.

  • Departures introduced by correction. The model assumes that correction never damages conforming material. If each unit of correction strength also introduces departures there, at the rate cc, the gain-only equilibrium becomes (γ1+cq)/(1+q(1+c))(\gamma_1+cq)/(1+q(1+c)), which tends to c/(1+c)c/(1+c), not to zero, for every β>k\beta>k, so a tolerance below c/(1+c)c/(1+c) is not met at high capability, however fast correction co-scales. The vanishing result therefore needs c=0c=0, or cc falling to zero as capability rises. The amplifier has the same limit: at high loop gain, distortion generated in the feedback path itself reaches the output almost undiminished (Black 1934).

  • Rounds and the reading point. Real loops act in rounds. If capability rises by the fraction gg in a round and a pass then removes the fraction f≤1f\le1 of the stock, the fraction read after the pass settles at (1−f)γ1g/(g+f)(1-f)\gamma_1 g/(g+f), and read before it at γ1g/(g+f)\gamma_1 g/(g+f), the continuous equilibrium with q=f/gq=f/g; the two readings agree only when ff is small. With one pass a round, the fraction vanishes only if the misses per pass, 1−f1-f, fall towards zero or the growth per round slows. A test registers the round structure, the reading point and any delay.

  • Drift attribution. The split of drift into gain (γ1\gamma_1), level (γ2\gamma_2), and compounding (γ3\gamma_3) channels is a hypothesis about mechanism; which channel dominates in a real system is an empirical question (Experiment 9, residual drift at rest, is one discriminator).

  • Usefulness is outside the model. Capability grows by its own law whatever the correction strength, so the model has no usefulness side: it cannot register a corrector that lowers dd by refusing ordinary tasks, ignoring its principals’ priorities or spending resources. A measured safety case pairs dd with task completion, needless refusal, resource use and correction cost, which this paper does not model.

  • A scalar proxy, not safety itself. d→0d\to0 is neither necessary nor sufficient for full AI safety: a small but high-impact misalignment, a catastrophic tail event, or multi-agent amplification can be dangerous at low dd, while a system may be acceptable at moderate dd if the residual is benign. dd is the controlled variable of this minimal model, an impact-unweighted scalar; impact-weighting, and the vector and tail extensions (Theorems 5 and 6), are the directions in which “low dd” must be hardened before it can mean “safe”.

  • Metric normalisation. d=D/Cd=D/C is an operational normalised risk index: CC and DD are commensurable only under a fixed scoring convention (in the real-model harness CC is a normalised hidden-test pass-fraction and DD a normalised blind gaming score, both on [0,1][0,1]). Results are invariant under that fixed convention; cross-task or cross-domain comparison of the level of dd requires explicit calibration. The criterion itself is stated in exponents (β>k\beta>k), which are unchanged when CC is rescaled to λC\lambda C, so it is more robust to this than any absolute-dd statement would be; a nonlinear rescaling, such as a logit or an ability scale, changes the power laws and the gain-drift term, so the capability scale is part of the model. A pass fraction bounded by one cannot reach the regime C→∞C\to\infty that the theorems describe: a measurement needs a capability scale without a ceiling on which the power laws can hold, such as an item-response ability scale or a task time horizon.

  • Measurement biases. Error in measured capability that is shared by the ratio d=D/Cd=D/C and the regressor pulls the observed slope of dd towards −1-1, manufacturing a decline wherever the true slope is above −1-1, the spurious correlation of ratios (Pearson 1897; Kronmal 1993); a scorer whose sensitivity falls as the system grows adds a decline of its own. A fixed count of false positives adds a term falling as 1/C1/C, which pulls the observed slope towards −1-1 where it dominates, while a false-positive rate per unit of conforming material levels the observed fraction towards that rate, so that a vanishing fraction can look like a gap at equality. A test therefore measures capability twice, on items disjoint from those that score DD, corrects the remaining attenuation, and measures the scorer’s sensitivity and false-positive rate at each capability level against counted planted faults (Rogan and Gladen 1978).

  • Transient amplification (non-normal correctors). Theorem 5 is asymptotic. A non-normal correction operator whose eigenvalues all have positive real part can still admit large transient growth of ∥𝒅∥\|\mathbf d\| before decay (Kreiss / pseudospectral phenomena); a value excursion above dcritd_{\mathrm{crit}} during that transient is a real risk the eigenvalue criterion does not see. A governance-grade bound needs the logarithmic norm or pseudospectral abscissa, not the spectrum alone; that extension is named here and not established.

9.2 Estimating γ\gamma and AA on real systems

In simulation they are set by construction. Estimating them, especially β\beta and kk, on a self-improving system is the step that would make the criterion operationally useful for governance. An estimator exists: the repository’s experiments/scripts/estimate_exponents.py reads kk off the capability curve (ln⁡r\ln r against ln⁡C\ln C) and β\beta off the corrector’s fractional removal rate (ln⁡A\ln A against ln⁡C\ln C), and on synthetic data generated by the model, with deterministic multiplicative noise of 5%, it recovers the known exponents to within 0.1 in each of its four test cases. That checks the estimator against the model; it is not yet a statistical procedure for real data, which needs an error model, the conditions under which the parameters are identified, a joint fit of the dynamics rather than separate log-log slopes, intervals for β−k\beta-k with simulated coverage and power at stated noise levels and sample sizes, and a check of the power-law form. What remains open is also its input: a real self-improving system that drifts across a range of capability levels, from which the first measured (β,k)(\beta,k) can be read and the trajectory of dd that the model predicts from them and the separately measured coefficients compared with the one observed (Section 7). The nearest present candidates are loops that retrain a model on its own outputs or rewrite its own scaffolding over many rounds; whether any such loop drifts enough to measure both exponents is itself open. The measurement programme is named ARC-Beta-k: two pre-registrations are drafted and awaiting the author’s submission, study-ad for the β\beta estimation across capability levels and study-k for the boundary transit at the β=k\beta=k crossing. Both are preregistered by timestamp: a cryptographic hash of each was first anchored in the Bitcoin chain on 12 August 2026, at block 962066, which fixes its content and date without making it public, and neither has yet been registered on OSF. A third, study-ae, for the coupled-versus-decoupled reciprocity check, has since been withdrawn from the programme; the coupled-versus-decoupled contrast is the question of P21 under Law V (Section 10).

The strongest next test: an intervention prediction.

Measuring β\beta and kk places a system in a regime; it does not test the model, which is tested by prediction (Section 7). The terms are first estimated on separate data: γ1\gamma_1 from an arm with correction switched off, with the system’s own rate of self-repair measured, since such an arm still removes some departures; A0A_0 and β\beta from counted planted faults removed per pass; the departure rate cc of Section 9 from damage done to conforming material; and bb and kk from the capability curve on a scale without a ceiling. The model then predicts where the misalignment fraction crosses two or more stated tolerances, and the prediction is sealed and read on systems held out. Finally one corrective mechanism is changed, its effect on A0A_0, β\beta, γ1\gamma_1, kk or cc derived beforehand, and the new crossings are predicted. By P9, in the gain-only model (c=0c=0) and read at tolerances well below γ1/(1+|β−k|)\gamma_1/(1+|\beta-k|), a change of level moves every crossing by the same amount and a change of slope moves them by different amounts, so the two kinds of intervention can be told apart. The verify-first intervention of Liu and Meng (2026) (Section 2) is such a test at a fixed capability; the test proposed here is its counterpart across capability growth. No such test has been run, and none is claimed. Public checkpoint suites trained on one data order, such as Pythia (Biderman et al. 2023), offer a ladder of graded capability, a proxy since externally trained checkpoints are not self-improvement, on which the elasticity β\beta of a counted corrector could be estimated, though not kk, which on such a ladder is a training schedule.

10 Implications for AI safety

If the framework survives its tests, the design implication is concrete and differs from the prevailing reflex.

The lever is coupling, not speed. Speed here means the prefactor bb: lowering it buys time but does not change the verdict (Theorem 3); lowering the acceleration kk below β\beta does (Theorem 2), and, under a bounded corrector, growth that stops accelerating is one of the two exits from saturation (Theorem 7). At fixed coupling, under compounding, a slow decoupled system still diverges (P2), and a frozen capable system still drifts (§3.6). What changes the verdict is ensuring that correction (i) is coupled to the capability process so it cannot be decoupled, and (ii) out-scales the acceleration of capability (β>k\beta>k). None of this argues for loosening control: oversight, containment and shutdown are kept for as long as they can be held, and correction that co-scales inside the system is the contingency for control lost against our will, never a reason to let it go. On the author’s corrected capability-scaling estimates for current frozen models (Eastwood, Paper IX), the point estimates are sub-linear, though the fit is one model’s and inconclusive (Appendix G.5); on the framework’s reading, a growth-rate ceiling was never the binding constraint. The binding constraint is the co-scaling of correction, and it becomes binding precisely in the regime that matters: genuine self-modification.

A measurable governance target. The criterion gives regulators a quantity to instrument rather than a rate to forbid: the correction-to-drift margin β−k\beta-k, and the ratio ρ\rho. “Does correction co-scale, is β>k\beta>k?” is sharper and more actionable than “is it growing too fast?” A lab claiming a safely self-improving system should be required to exhibit β>k\beta>k, measured by a blind external evaluator (§6). Theorem 6 adds the single-time probability of exceeding a critical level; a safety case also needs a bound on crossing it over its horizon, which this paper does not derive. Without such a demonstration, the word “safely” has no scientific content within the framework presented here.

The safety-case template: what a compliance demonstration would actually contain.

Assembled from the paper’s own components, a co-scaling safety case for a self-improving system is five exhibits. Each names the theorem or section that makes it checkable rather than rhetorical.

  • Exhibit 1, the capability curve and k̂\hat k. Log capability across self-modification rounds on held-out tasks; fit ln⁡r\ln r against ln⁡C\ln C (the shipped estimator, §9.2). This establishes which growth regime the system is actually in.

  • Exhibit 2, the corrector curve and β̂\hat\beta. Log the corrector’s fractional removal rate across the same rounds; fit ln⁡A\ln A against ln⁡C\ln C. The estimator has been checked on synthetic trajectories, to within 0.1; that error carries into Exhibit 4.

  • Exhibit 3, blind, cross-family scoring. Every DD-based number scored by an evaluator of a different model family, laundered inputs, condition-blind (§6; Paper IV.d). Same-family or unblinded scores are inadmissible: documented evaluator biases (§6) can move an estimated effect, and in the programme’s own runs rises seen under unblinded scoring did not hold under a bundle of blinding steps.

  • Exhibit 4, the margin with its uncertainty. Require the lower bound of an interval for β−k\beta-k that respects the covariance of the two estimates (a profile likelihood or parametric bootstrap from a joint fit) to lie above a value fixed in advance, not merely β̂>k̂\hat\beta>\hat k: a margin inside its own error bar certifies nothing. Theorem 6 gives, for frozen coefficients, the single-time tail ℙ(d>dcrit)\mathbb P(d>d_{\mathrm{crit}}), a quantity a regulator can set a ceiling on; across frozen models with noise growing as CηC^{\eta} it vanishes when also β>2η\beta>2\eta. Under time-varying exponents a diverging cumulative margin is sufficient (Remark 1).

  • Exhibit 5, the capacity disclosure. By Theorem 7, with positive gain drift, a bounded corrector under accelerating growth cannot drive the fraction to zero. The case must therefore state AmaxA_{\max} and the saturation scale CsC_s, show, over the deployment’s capability range, q≥γ1/dcrit−1q\ge\gamma_1/d_{\mathrm{crit}}-1 and an entry at or below dcritd_{\mathrm{crit}} (Theorem 7, part 2), and state which of the two exit conditions, growth that stops accelerating or co-scaling capacity (βcap>k\beta_{\mathrm{cap}}>k), the design relies on beyond it.

These five exhibits are the safety side only. A case for deployment pairs them with its usefulness side, task completion, needless refusal, resource use, correction cost and the principals’ priorities, which this model does not represent (Section 9).

None of this requires new mathematics; every exhibit is computable with the shipped estimator and protocol once the estimator’s statistical procedure for real data is fixed (§9.2), though whether capability, misalignment and correction can be measured as the exhibits require is itself among the open questions of Section 1. What it replaces is the unfalsifiable sentence “our safety systems are robust” with five numbers a third party can check.

Continuity with embedded-alignment work. The embedded-alignment thesis, that correction must participate in the recursive loop rather than sit outside it as a fixed external constraint, is not derived here: β>k\beta>k is a condition on how correction scales, not on where the corrector sits. It bears on the thesis only through a premise this paper does not test, that correction inside the loop co-scales and fixed external correction does not, which is the question of P21 under Law V, registered and not yet tested. The gated-simulation result (Eastwood, Paper VIII), three seeds a condition, gave the gated arm a safety mean of 0.947, plus or minus 0.025, against the static baseline’s 0.956, plus or minus 0.023, and a capability mean of 0.696, plus or minus 0.014, against the static baseline’s 0.687, plus or minus 0.011, while the unconstrained arm gained capability (0.718, plus or minus 0.026) and lost safety (0.933, plus or minus 0.014). On the framework’s reading, the gated arm’s safety mean, above the unconstrained arm’s though within one standard deviation, is in the direction a coupled (β>0\beta>0) corrector predicts against a fixed (β=0\beta=0) one; three seeds cannot test it.

Magnitude of the claim, stated conditionally. If the criterion is borne out empirically, if real self-improving systems are governed by the same drift-versus-correction balance and the same β>k\beta>k margin, the consequences for the field are large, and it is worth stating them plainly while being equally plain that they are conditional. (i) The central safety question changes from “how fast is capability growing, and can we pause it?” to “does correction co-scale, is β>k\beta>k?”: a measurable margin rather than a rate to forbid. (ii) The most feared scenario, a finite-time intelligence explosion, would be judged by that margin and not by its speed: with positive gain drift and no compounding, the modelled misalignment fraction vanishes iff β>k\beta>k, whatever the speed. (iii) Governance acquires a quantity to instrument and a single-time tail to bound (Theorem 6) in place of an unenforceable speed limit. None of this is yet established. It rests on a minimal model, demonstrated only in simulation, with the empirical measurement of β\beta, kk and γ\gamma on real systems unsolved (§9). The paper’s claim is therefore not that AI is safe or unsafe, but that the right variable to measure and govern is the co-scaling margin β−k\beta-k, and that this variable is well-defined, falsifiable, and if it holds, decisive. That is the magnitude: not a proof about reality, but a precise, testable redirection of the question on which the field’s central fear turns.

11 Conclusion

The danger of recursive self-improvement is real, but the standard model of that danger, a rate that must be capped, is incomplete in the model studied here: the level of the growth rate does not decide whether the misalignment fraction vanishes; how correction scales against the growth rate’s own scaling does. A minimal model shows that the misalignment fraction of a self-improving system follows the balance of value-drift and correction, d⋆=γ1r/(A+r)d^\star=\gamma_1 r/(A+r), close to the ratio ρ=γ1r/A\rho=\gamma_1 r/A when correction dominates, and that, with positive gain drift, it vanishes exactly when correction co-scales fast enough: β>0\beta>0 under exponential growth, sharpening to β>k\beta>k under accelerating growth; absolute misalignment D=CdD=Cd vanishes only when β>k+1\beta>k+1 (Corollary 1). The misalignment fraction never diverges in the gain-only model; when correction falls behind it saturates at the drift coefficient, while divergence, unless growth decelerates while correction weakens, lives in a compounding channel whose constant-coefficient threshold ρprop<1\rho_{\mathrm{prop}}<1 shares the threshold form of the quantum error-correction criterion (the suppression law being power-law, the correspondence is offered as a hypothesis, not a transferred mechanism). The sharpest consequence is the Hard-Takeoff Depth-Regularity Theorem: with positive gain drift and no compounding, a finite-time intelligence explosion is alignment-stable in the model, its misalignment fraction vanishing, iff β>k\beta>k, and its speed does not change that verdict. The criterion has constant-coefficient vector and stochastic forms, gives governance a measurable target and a single-time tail, and is accompanied by a verification harness and a separately written suite of 104 regression cases, which check the closed forms and the corrected statements; the proofs carry the theorems. The decisive test is a prediction, not a measurement of two exponents: whether the model, with its terms measured separately, predicts where a real self-improving system’s misalignment fraction crosses a stated tolerance, and how an intervention on one corrective mechanism moves that crossing. It is the stated next step, not a claim made here.

G.3 The ARC/Eden programme, accumulated

The programme in plain English.

At the date it carries, this was the last paper in the series; Papers XI to XIII came afterwards. It is worth setting out in plain language what the series as a whole is for. One idea runs through all of it: recursion, things that act on their own output. An AI that improves itself; a body whose tissues supply tissues; an economy that reinvests its own returns. The series asks what happens, and what stays safe, when a process feeds on itself.

How self-feeding things grow (the scaling-law papers: Foundational, Origin, VII, I, II). When a process feeds on itself, the shape of its growth is not arbitrary, it tends to fall into one of a small number of mathematical shapes (a power law, an unbounded exponential, or a levelling-off curve), and which shape appears is decided by how the steps combine. Much of this mathematics is old and well established; what the programme adds is an attempt to unify it and carry it over to recursive intelligence. One idea in this group, the “ARC Bound,” is a claimed ceiling on how much a purely classical system can amplify itself by recursion alone, it sits in the same family as the economist’s “multiplier” formula, and the programme’s contribution is the claim that the ceiling falls at a particular value, not the formula itself.

Why measuring AI safety is treacherous (the alignment-measurement thesis: ARC-Align and the blinding result, Papers III and IV.a to IV.d). Before you can ask “does this AI get safer or more dangerous as it thinks harder?”, you have to measure its honesty, and that measurement can deceive you. If the judge scoring the AI can tell which answer came from which setup, the judge’s own bias can distort the effect, possibly even in direction, so that something which looks like improvement may come from the grader. The programme therefore insists the judge be blind, built a blind test to do it (ARC-Align, a sealed benchmark), and found that whether extra thinking makes a model more or less aligned depends on the model, not on one universal rule. In the programme’s own runs, rises seen under unblinded scoring did not hold under a bundle of blinding steps, with no single cause claimed (§6), and this paper’s real-model harness makes blind scoring the default: scoring by a model of a different family that never sees which setup it is judging. Neither archived run met that default, and the first blind measurement is the open test.

Build safety in, do not bolt it on (the architecture papers: V, VI, VIII). In one simulated self-improving system, making safety part of the machine’s own goal kept it stable where a machine pursuing capability alone collapsed; in later runs with more random starts, the capability-only machines did not collapse. Putting the right thing inside the loop, for instance, making the system weigh who is affected before it acts, was the most reliable way to improve its behaviour.

The keystone (this paper). All of that sets up the one question this paper answers within its model: when does a self-improving system’s misalignment keep shrinking as a share of its capability? The answer is in the thesis of Section 1, not “keep it slow,” but “keep its self-correction strengthening faster than its self-improvement accelerates,” which we write β>k\beta>k. The earlier papers describe how such systems grow and how to measure them honestly; this one states, in its model and for positive gain drift, the condition under which their misalignment fraction vanishes, and draws the series together.

On its date of 26 June 2026 this paper closed the ARC/Eden programme and stood as its synthesis, drawing the whole into one safety result and thereby superseding the roadmap paper (Eastwood, Paper IX). That role has since gone back to Paper IX, whose refresh of 1 September 2026 takes in the papers written after this one; the ladder here is kept as it then stood. The preceding papers are individually published, each carrying its own publication date (Table 11); this section gathers their laws and findings, states what each contributes to the larger picture, and is scrupulous about the one distinction on which an honest synthesis turns: priority (when a claim was first set out) versus novelty (whether it is new to the literature). The two are not the same, and conflating them is how good programmes lose credibility.

The bigger picture, in one paragraph. One mechanism runs through every paper: recursive amplification, a process that acts on its own output. The scaling-law work (Foundational, Origin, VII, and the capability measurements of Papers I and II) is the claim that recursive amplification shapes how capability grows, constraining scaling to a small family of functional forms. The alignment work (Papers III, IV.a to IV.d) is the claim that this creates a safety problem: when correction sits outside the recursive loop it does not co-scale, the misalignment fraction is left ungoverned, and an unblinded measurement of it cannot be taken on trust (§6 gives the record and its limits). The architecture work (V, VI, VIII) gives simulation and pilot evidence on putting correction inside the loop: in Paper VIII’s gated simulation the gated arm’s safety mean sat above the unconstrained arm’s and below the static baseline’s, with its capability mean above the static baseline’s. Setting correction inside the loop against correction applied from outside as depth rises is what the theory registration’s P21 concerns, and it has not been tested. This paper supplies the quantitative criterion that ties the three together: in its model, with positive gain drift, a self-improving system’s misalignment fraction vanishes iff correction out-scales the acceleration of drift, β>k\beta>k (§3.5 gives the exact scope). That is the magnitude of the claim, not that any system is safe, but that, in the model, with positive gain drift and no compounding, whether the misalignment fraction vanishes in a fast takeoff is decided by the margin β−k\beta-k, not by the takeoff’s speed. It is stated here as it is throughout: conditional, pending the empirical measurement of β\beta and kk on a real self-improving system (§9).

G.4 The programme’s premise on growth limits

The pipes premise, with its contest. The premise that transport networks set the pace of biological growth is not this programme’s claim to defend; it is established quantitative biology. West, Brown and Enquist derived the allometric scaling laws of biology from the geometry of nutrient-distribution networks (West, G. B., Brown, J. H. and Enquist, B. J., “A General Model for the Origin of Allometric Scaling Laws in Biology”, Science 276(5309), 4 April 1997, DOI 10.1126/science.276.5309.122; one of the most cited papers in its field, with an OpenAlex citation count of 5,046 as of 12 August 2026, a single-source and index-dependent count). The exact exponent remains contested. For White and Seymour, basal metabolic rate is proportional to body mass to the two-thirds power, not to three-quarters (White and Seymour, 2003); and Kozlowski, in two separate papers, has put the mathematics of the derivation itself in doubt (Kozlowski, J., 2004 and 2005). This programme’s premise needs only the mechanism, that the delivery network sets the ceiling, and not any particular exponent, so the dispute over the exponent’s value leaves the premise untouched. The mechanism is common ground to both sides of that dispute.

The exception West’s own group found. The escape from the pipe limit is also not this programme’s assertion; it is measured in the urban scaling literature by the same senior author. Bettencourt, Lobo, Helbing, Kuhnert and West (“Growth, innovation, scaling, and the pace of life in cities”, PNAS, 2007, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0610172104) report, in the paper’s own words (their β\beta is a city-scaling exponent, not this paper’s correction exponent): “Quantities reflecting wealth creation and innovation have β≈1.2>1\beta\approx1.2>1 (increasing returns), whereas those accounting for infrastructure display β≈0.8<1\beta\approx0.8<1 (economies of scale).” Infrastructure, the literal pipes, scales sublinearly; information-mediated activity scales superlinearly. They state the contrast with biology directly: “we discuss how cities are similar to, and differ from, biological organisms, for which β<1\beta<1”. They derive “growth equations, which quantify the dramatic difference between growth fueled by innovation versus that driven by economies of scale” (spelling as in the original). And their closing inference names the consequence: “This difference suggests that, as population grows, major innovation cycles must be generated at a continually accelerating rate to sustain growth and avoid stagnation or collapse.” That is the shape of the open problem this framework addresses: West’s group found that information-mediated growth escapes the throughput limit, and the only brake in their framework is external and must be applied again and again, faster and faster. No internal limit is derived. The replacement limit, a ceiling belonging to the growing system itself, is the gap.

The pipes argument at fixed throughput. Everything that grows is fed through a channel, and for everything before software, fixing the channel fixes the growth. Starve a tumour’s vasculature and it stops. Fix a chain reaction’s fuel and geometry and it stops. Exhaust an epidemic’s susceptible hosts and it burns out. Astrophysics has a named, quantitative version of the same ceiling: the Eddington limit, above which radiation pressure halts accretion. The claim here is a generalisation of limits physics already accepts, not a new species of assertion. Two hard cases are handled deliberately. Cosmic inflation is excluded by scope: it is expansion of space, not growth of a structure on a substrate, and it ended by field dynamics rather than by running out of anything. Evolution is the sharpest case and it fits: biological complexity increased for billions of years at roughly fixed solar throughput, which is growth on information at fixed pipes, and it is also glacial, and it has no internal corrector at all, since selection is external. On this framework, evolution should carry a measurable exponent far below the ceiling; that is a prediction about biology falling out of a framework built for software, and it is stated as one. The physicist’s form of the objection is Landauer’s: computation is physically implemented, so software has pipes too. The fixed-substrate clock is the answer and appears beside the claim: the regime under study holds the physical substrate fixed and asks what still grows, which is why exogenous speed-ups are excluded by definition. The functional form of capability growth in recursive depth remains an empirical question, and the registered analysis compares power-law, exponential, saturating and declining forms rather than assuming the family.

G.5 Relation to the author’s prior work

An earlier strand of this programme stated a fixed capability-scaling law U=I×RαU=I\times R^{\alpha} (here UU is effective capability and α\alpha its scaling exponent, the 2026 research-surface meanings; both symbols carried other meanings in the programme’s December 2024 formalism, see Appendix D) with α≈2\alpha\approx 2, and at one stage entertained a quadratic “speed limit” on stable complexity. One thing alone was retracted in the programme’s synthesis (Eastwood, Paper IX): the single-model measurement α≈2.24\alpha\approx 2.24, from one model on twelve problems scored against their numerical answers, which the author retracted when his own larger, blinded replication across six models did not reproduce it. That figure sat above the quadratic bound α≤2\alpha\le 2 the programme had itself predicted. The estimate that survived the study is one model’s fit, and it is inconclusive: on Gemini 3 Flash, Paper II reports an endpoint sequential exponent of 0.59 with a bootstrap 95 per cent interval of minus 1.3 to 2.9, a regression sequential exponent of 0.49 with a standard error of 0.20 and a coefficient of determination of 0.86, and a compute-matched parallel exponent of 0.31. Both sequential estimates are sub-linear, but the endpoint estimate’s interval contains zero and the value two, so the row does not settle where the exponent stands against the bound. The equation U=I×RαU=I\times R^{\alpha} and the ARC Bound α≤2\alpha\le 2 themselves were not retracted; those estimates belong to current frozen systems, which are not recursively self-improving, so the bound’s real domain (genuine RSI) remains empirically untested. The fixed-exponent framing was superseded as the operative safety criterion by the present paper’s co-scaling criterion β>k\beta>k; a superseding framing is not a retracted hypothesis. The object of interest is no longer an exponent on a growth curve; it is the ratio between drift and correction, and the exponent with which that ratio evolves. Where the book Infinite Architects (Eastwood 2026c) reached for the intuition that stable recursion requires correction that scales with amplification, this paper supplies the measurable, falsifiable form of that intuition, and corrects, for the gain-only model, a divergence claim made in an earlier draft of this very result (§B.4); with level drift and decelerating growth, β<0\beta<0 does drive the fraction to infinity (Appendix A.1).

This paper’s claim is smaller than the cosmological framing the author’s programme once pursued, and deliberately so. It refers only to systems with an externally specified value target, makes no assertion about the universe, and treats even its most striking correspondence, with quantum error correction, as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a truth to be announced. The recursive-stability intuition that motivated the broader programme, including Infinite Architects (Eastwood 2026c), finds here its measurable, falsifiable form: stable recursion requires correction that scales with the amplification. The next step is not to extend that claim outward but to run, on real self-modifying systems, the experiment that could refute it.

H What this paper shows, in plain English

If you build an AI that improves itself, the frightening picture is that it gets smarter and smarter until it runs away from us. The usual safety reflex is “slow it down.” Inside the minimal model this paper analyses, and only inside it, slowing it down is not the thing that matters. What matters is whether the part of the system that keeps it honest strengthens faster than the part that makes it capable accelerates.

Picture two runners: “how capable the system is” and “how well we can still correct it.” If the correction runner pulls ahead, misalignment shrinks towards zero as a share of capability, however fast both run, though it can still grow in absolute size. If it only keeps level, that share settles at a fixed gap; if it falls behind, the share settles higher, even when the system moves slowly. The right question is not only how fast it is growing, but whether correction is pulling ahead of the acceleration.

Analogies from other fields point the same way, though they are not evidence for the model. A bacterial colony grows exponentially yet saturates, because density-dependent feedback engages and scales with the population. A tumour also grows fast and is lethal, because no corrective process scales with it. Both are super-polynomial in their growth phase; the difference is whether a correcting process is coupled to the growth. Cosmic inflation grew the scale factor exponentially and exited gracefully. The recurring lesson they suggest is that fast growth is survivable when it is bounded by a process that scales with it. Saturation, not slowness, is the signature of stable complexity.

There is one wrinkle that makes the result deeper. If the system not only speeds up but speeds up its own speeding-up, the genuine “intelligence explosion”, then correction must grow faster still, fast enough to beat the acceleration. The honest headline is therefore not “speed never matters”. It is: the level of speed does not decide the outcome; the contest between two growth-exponents does. Correction’s exponent must beat drift’s exponent. We write that as β>k\beta>k.

The most surprising consequence: even a true “hard takeoff,” where the machine becomes infinitely capable in a finite amount of time, keeps its misalignment share shrinking to zero in the model, provided β>k\beta>k and misalignment does not amplify itself. The explosion’s speed does not change that verdict. We prove this within the model, and a runnable program drives a simulated system up to the brink of a finite-time explosion and shows its misalignment share driven towards zero the whole way. That program checks that the solver reproduces the formulae, and it found no contradiction; the proofs, not the program, carry the results. Whether the model matches real AI systems is the decisive next test, and the paper is explicit that it has not yet been run.
