                                                        Abstract
           Can correction keep pace with a system that improves itself? I analyse a minimal model in which
       misalignment 𝐷 arrives in proportion to capability 𝐶 gained and is removed in proportion to its size,
       so the fraction 𝑑 = 𝐷/𝐶 behaves as a feedback loop whose gain is correction strength 𝐴 over the
       specific growth rate 𝑟; the proofs are elementary. With positive gain drift 𝛾1 > 0, 𝑑 tends to zero
       when 𝐴/𝑟 grows without bound and, for 𝐴 = 𝐴0 𝐶 𝛽 and 𝑟 = 𝑏𝐶 𝑘 , if and only if 𝛽 > 𝑘; along the
       trajectory 𝑑 ∼ (𝛾1 𝑏/𝐴0 )𝐶 − (𝛽−𝑘 ) . At 𝛽 = 𝑘 it settles at 𝛾1 𝑏/( 𝐴0 + 𝑏), so the coefficients decide
       whether a tolerance is met, and at 𝛾1 when 𝛽 < 𝑘. Absolute misalignment 𝐷 = 𝐶𝑑 is bounded
       only when 𝛽 ≥ 𝑘 + 1. The verdict is set by 𝛽 − 𝑘, not by the growth prefactor 𝑏, and without
       compounding it holds through a finite-time blow-up of capability. Under the power laws, after its
       transient, 𝑑 depends on capability only through 𝐴/𝑟, so a change in the level of correction shifts
       every tolerance crossing equally and a change of 𝛽 − 𝑘 shifts them unequally. Unless correction
       and the growth rate both decay with capability, unbounded 𝑑 needs self-amplifying misalignment,
       𝛾3 ≥ 1, with a constant-coefficient threshold (𝛾3 − 1)𝑟/𝐴 < 1 of the epidemic and branching kind;
       its mapping onto the quantum error-correction threshold is a hypothesis. Extensions cover vector
       drift, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise and a saturating corrector, under which, with accelerating growth,
       the fraction vanishes only if capacity scales with 𝛽cap > 𝑘. The theorems concern a modelled index,
       not a guarantee of AI safety. The open test is to predict, from separately measured terms, where 𝑑
       crosses stated tolerances on held-out systems, before and after one corrective mechanism is changed;
       an estimator and a blind protocol are given.

1    Introduction
Recursive self-improvement, a system that modifies itself to become more capable, then uses that capability
to modify itself further, is among the central concerns of AI safety (Omohundro 2008; Bostrom 2012;
Yudkowsky 2013). The empirical backdrop is no longer purely hypothetical: frontier models have been
observed to behave differently when they infer their outputs may be used to train them (Greenblatt et al.
2024), and recursive training on a system’s own outputs degrades it, much less so when part of the original
data is preserved (Shumailov et al. 2024), both signs that recursive systems need a correcting process that
keeps pace with the recursion. The dominant informal model of the associated risk is a model of speed:
capability may grow super-linearly or explosively, outrunning supervision, so the natural lever is to cap
the rate of growth. Calls for a development pause are the policy expression of this intuition.
    This paper makes a different claim, and proves it within a minimal model. With positive gain drift,
the level of the growth rate does not decide whether the misalignment fraction vanishes; the scaling
relationship between growth and correction does. A system can grow arbitrarily fast and keep its
misalignment fraction falling, or grow slowly and leave it at a floor or, under compounding, let it grow.
What separates the two is not the rate but whether, and how fast, the corrective process strengthens as
capability rises, relative to how fast growth itself accelerates.
    The criterion is the capability-indexed form of a classical feedback balance (§2; Black 1934). The
divergence threshold of the compounding channel, for constant coefficients, is of another kind: a ratio of
error generation to error correction crossing one, rather than a limit on depth or speed, the form shared
by an epidemic’s 𝑅0 < 1, a subcritical branching process and the quantum error-correction (QEC)
threshold theorem (Aharonov and Ben-Or 1997; Kitaev 2003), under which a quantum computation can
be made fault-tolerant at a cost polylogarithmic in its size when the error probability per operation lies
below a constant threshold. The mapping of the QEC threshold’s terms onto this model is offered as a
hypothesis (§3.12).
    The thesis in one line. With positive gain drift, whether the misalignment fraction of a self-improving
system vanishes is decided, in the model, by one exponent inequality, 𝛽 > 𝑘 (correction must out-scale
drift-acceleration; with level drift and decelerating growth, also 𝛽 > 0); through a finite-time intelligence
explosion, without compounding, the same inequality decides, whatever the explosion’s speed.
    Our contributions are as follows.
    1. The criterion and its trajectory law. In a minimal model of recursive self-improvement, equation (1),
       with correction strength 𝐴 = 𝐴0𝐶 𝛽 , specific growth rate 𝑟 = 𝑏𝐶 𝑘 and the exact constant-coefficient
       transient of Theorem 1, the misalignment fraction is bounded and, with positive gain drift, tends to
       zero if and only if 𝛽 > 𝑘 (both in the gain-only model), along the trajectory as 𝐶 − (𝛽−𝑘 ) ; it tends
       to a permanent gap if 𝛽 = 𝑘 and to 𝛾1 if 𝛽 < 𝑘 (Theorem 2). Corollary 1 gives the rate at which
       absolute misalignment can still grow while the fraction vanishes.
    2. Its finite-time form. When capability diverges in finite time, the alignment dynamics are regular in
       the depth clock 𝜏 = ln(𝐶/𝐶0 ), and without compounding the same limits hold; which one holds
       does not depend on the speed prefactor 𝑏 (Theorem 3).
    3. The compounding classification. Without level drift, divergence needs self-amplifying misalignment,
       𝛾3 ≥ 1; with constant coefficients its threshold is 𝜌prop = (𝛾3 − 1)𝑟/𝐴 < 1, a ratio form the quantum
       error-correction threshold condition shares with other thresholds, and the mapping of that condition’s
       terms onto this model is stated as a hypothesis with its own falsifier; under the power laws the
       classification is exact (Theorem 4, §3.12, F4). With level drift, a system whose growth decelerates
       while its correction weakens can drift without bound with no compounding at all (§3.4).
    4. Standard extensions. A constant-matrix spectral threshold for vector misalignment (Theorem 5),
       the single-time stationary tail under stochastic drift (Theorem 6), and a saturating corrector, under

Every result follows by elementary means; what is claimed as new is the criterion and its reading for
self-improving systems (Appendix E). The criterion follows directly from the model’s two mechanisms,
misalignment generated in proportion to capability gained and removed in proportion to its size
(equation (1)), once correction and growth scale as powers of capability; the contribution is to state those
choices, to derive their consequences exactly, through a finite-time takeoff and under compounding, and
to give the criterion an estimator and a measurement protocol. Its empirical content is therefore not the
inequality but whether the choices hold. Can capability, misalignment and correction be measured on
separate instruments, with capability on a scale without a ceiling? Do arrivals track capability gained, is
removal linear in the stock, and does correction introduce few departures of its own, over a useful range?
Do the forms predict where the fraction crosses a stated tolerance before it is seen, better than rival forms
fixed in advance, among them a smooth model with no boundary? And does an intervention on one
corrective mechanism move those crossings as derived beforehand? None of these has yet been tested on
a real system.

2    Related work and relation to prior framing
Cybernetic foundations: the co-scaling intuition is old. The core intuition is classical. A regulator
must match the variety of what it regulates, which is Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety (Ashby 1956),
and every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system, which is the Conant-Ashby
good-regulator theorem (Conant and Ashby 1970); both are carried into AI control by Yampolskiy (2020).
That control capacity must therefore scale with the controlled system, rather than merely be large, is this
paper’s reading of them. This paper does not claim that intuition as new, nor the form of its steady state.
The steady state 𝑑★ = 𝛾1𝑟/( 𝐴 + 𝑟) = 𝛾1 /(1 + 𝐴/𝑟) is classical in shape: a disturbance generated in the
forward path of a feedback loop is reduced by one plus the loop gain (Black 1934), the gap at equality is
the offset of a proportional controller, and a linear regulator that keeps regulating under small changes in
certain system parameters must feed back the regulated variable and incorporate a model of the dynamics
of its disturbance and reference signals, which is the internal model principle (Francis and Wonham 1976).
What this paper claims is the balance’s indexing by capability. Because misalignment arrives in proportion
to capability gained, the loop gain is correction strength over the system’s specific growth rate, 𝑞 = 𝐴/𝑟,
and whether it grows, holds or decays as capability rises decides which limit of Theorem 2 holds. Hence
the exponent criterion 𝛽 > 𝑘, the second threshold of Corollary 1, the hard-takeoff theorem of §3.7 and
the intervention signature P9 (Section 4); the QEC mapping of §3.12 concerns the compounding channel.
The dynamics themselves are a linear ODE solved by an integrating factor and comparison, standard in
linear control (Khalil 2002; Meyn and Tweedie 2009), and are not advanced as mathematically novel.

Instrumental convergence and corrigibility. That a sufficiently capable optimiser will, by default,
resist correction and pursue resource acquisition is the instrumental-convergence thesis (Omohundro
2008; Bostrom 2012). The corrigibility programme (Soares et al. 2015) asks how to design systems
that do not resist correction. The present model is a quantitative restatement of why corrigibility is
load-bearing: with positive gain drift, unless correction out-scales the acceleration of growth (𝛽 > 𝑘), the
misalignment fraction cannot be driven to zero. The compounding channel of §3.8 sharpens the link:
self-amplifying misalignment, the term 𝛾3 , is what produces divergence without level drift, and 𝛽 > 𝑘
removes it in the model (Theorem 4).

The alignment tax. Safety is often taken to cost capability, and in a race to be first a cost of that kind
gives developers an incentive to cut safety precautions (Armstrong et al. 2016). The present framework
reframes the question: the relevant variable is not the level of the safety investment but whether it scales
with capability. A fixed investment (𝛽 = 0) leaves a permanent gap under exponential growth, and
saturation under acceleration; a co-scaling investment (𝛽 > 𝑘) closes it.

Learned optimisation and alignment faking. Mesa-optimisation (Hubinger et al. 2019) and empirically
demonstrated alignment faking (Greenblatt et al. 2024) are read here as mechanisms by which the drift
coefficients are non-zero: a capable system can satisfy its training objective while departing from intended
values, and can do so more effectively as capability rises. In the alignment-faking experiments a model
complied selectively with a new training objective in order to keep its existing preferences, in that case a
preference for harmlessness, from being modified, and reinforcement learning on that objective raised
its alignment-faking reasoning to 78% (Greenblatt et al. 2024). Keeping an existing departure from the
training objective through training is read here as the compounding channel 𝛾3 of §3.8.

Scalable oversight and superalignment. Reward modelling and recursive oversight (Christiano et al.
2017; Leike et al. 2018), which address what Amodei et al. (2016) call scalable supervision, are attempts
to make the corrector itself scale with the system; in the language of this paper, scalable oversight
is the engineering project of achieving 𝛽 > 𝑘. Most directly, Engels et al. (2025) develop empirical
scaling laws for scalable oversight, modelling the probability of successful oversight as a game between
capability-mismatched players. The present work is complementary, not competing: where they fit an
oversight-success probability, this paper derives a closed-form exponent criterion (𝛽 > 𝑘) and, under
compounding, a constant-coefficient threshold (𝜌prop < 1) for the misalignment fraction. The instrument
is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen, measured in Elo,
with oversight-specific Elo a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence, and optimal numbers of
oversight levels derived for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted
models that then become the trusted models at the next step. They also state the design condition in
slope form: oversight improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with
general Elo than the overseen system’s (𝑚 𝐺 > 𝑚 𝐻 ), and they recommend iterating an oversight design
until it does. That is a comparison of two slopes deciding whether oversight gains or loses as capability
grows, the same shape as 𝛽 > 𝑘; the objects differ, since 𝛽 and 𝑘 are the elasticities of one self-modifying
system’s correction strength and specific growth rate with its own capability, for its misalignment fraction.
Their framework has no term for what the overseer is made of, and this paper’s model has none either.
The programme’s separate prediction, outside this paper’s model, is that iterated same-class oversight, of
which Nested Scalable Oversight is an instance, stays capped however many rungs are added, while a
cross-class corrector is not held to that cap. The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class
correctors, at or below one half, as a conjecture on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III, the ARC
Ceiling, an exponent the registration keeps distinct from Law II’s 𝛽𝐶 , this paper’s 𝛽. That is where the

two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it. The
contribution is the proof that this exponent margin, and not a growth-rate ceiling, is the quantity that

Recursive error accumulation. That naive recursion can amplify error without bound, while retaining
the original data keeps it bounded, is established for training dynamics: model collapse under recursively
generated data (Shumailov et al. 2024) and the accumulate-versus-replace error analyses (Gerstgrasser
et al. 2024) give the bounded-versus-divergent dichotomy that this paper formalises for the alignment
fraction (Theorems 2 and 4). Stability of iterative retraining when the initial model approximates the
data well enough and the share of clean data is large enough (Bertrand et al. 2023), and conditions under
which a verifier prevents collapse (Feng et al. 2024), are stability conditions of the same setting; none is
stated for a value-misalignment fraction against capability growth. The contribution here is to locate, in
the model, where the fraction vanishes (𝛽 > 𝑘, for positive gain drift) and, under compounding, where it
diverges (Theorem 4(c)), in a value-stability rather than a data-distribution setting.

Empirical scaling laws. Capability scales predictably with compute and data (Kaplan et al. 2020;
Hoffmann et al. 2022), and so do quantities on the corrector’s side: the coefficients of the gold-score curve
under optimisation against a reward model scale smoothly with the reward model’s size (Gao et al. 2022),
and self-improvement is largely governed by a generation-verification gap, a variant of which scales
monotonically with pre-training compute (Song et al. 2024). The present framework is complementary:
it does not ask how capability scales, but what constraint correction must satisfy as a function of that
capability trajectory. It belongs to the same genre, a relation between exponents, to be tested by prediction
outside the range on which it is fitted.
    The programme’s premise on growth limits, with the urban-scaling exception and the argument at
fixed throughput, is set out in Appendix G.4.

Antecedents and near-misses, each with its differential. On the question: Hutter asked directly
whether intelligence can explode (“Can Intelligence Explode?”, arXiv, 28 February 2012), undertaking to
separate speed from intelligence explosion and to consider possible bounds on intelligence, augmenting
the analysis of Chalmers (2010); the question and the separation of speed from intelligence explosion
are therefore at least fourteen years old, and what that literature does not contain is a number: no
measurable exponent, no derived ceiling, no architecture dependence. On impossibility: three 2025 arXiv
papers argue for limits on alignment or its control (Yao, “The Alignment Trap: Complexity Barriers”,
arXiv:2506.10304, public by 13 June 2025, attested by arXiv and independently observed by the Internet
Archive; Yao, “On the Mathematical Impossibility of Safe Universal Approximators”, arXiv:2507.03031,
3 July 2025; and Ball, Gluch, Goldwasser, Kreuter, Reingold and Rothblum, “On the Impossibility of
Separating Intelligence from Judgment”, arXiv:2507.07341, 9 July 2025). The first argues that the set of
safe policies has measure zero and that verifying a policy’s safety is coNP-complete; the second, that
perfect, reliable control of a universal approximator is a mathematical impossibility; the third, under
cryptographic hardness assumptions, that some large language models admit no efficient prompt filter
and that output filtering is intractable in a natural setting. All three are worst-case and qualitative.
None reports an average-case scaling exponent or a rate. The third concludes that “safety cannot be
achieved by designing filters external to the LLM internals (architecture and weights)” and argues that
“an aligned AI system’s intelligence cannot be separated from its judgment”; that argument from filtering
intractability is consistent with this programme’s architecture dependence, though it does not test it. On
the mechanism: that anti-correlated estimates average better than independent ones is textbook variance
reduction (antithetic variates); the mechanism is not the claim. The claim is that architecture determines

corrective gain relative to system gain causes instability has a long control-theory lineage (small-gain
theorems), beside the feedback amplifier’s reduction of internally generated disturbance credited above
(Black 1934); those are statements about fixed loops and interconnections, not a condition on how a
corrector’s strength scales against the growth rate of a recursively improving system. Beside Engels et al.
(2025) above, a near quantitative neighbour is Liu, A. and Meng, J., “Self-Correction as Feedback Control:
Error Dynamics, Stability Thresholds, and Prompt Interventions in LLMs”, arXiv:2604.22273, which
recasts self-correction as a closed-loop control problem via a two-state Markov model and derives a directly
measurable stability threshold, iterate only when the error-correction rate over the error-introduction rate
exceeds Acc/(1 − Acc). The differential: theirs is a per-step rate threshold at a fixed capability level,
deciding whether another iteration helps now; this paper’s criterion is a scaling relation across capability,
deciding whether corrective strength out-scales the growth as the system improves. Theirs has no scaling
exponent on the corrector and no capability-growth exponent, the two quantities this criterion compares.
The two models meet at zero growth: extended by the departures that correction introduces (Section 9)
and written per round, with a pass that repairs the fraction 𝑓 of departures and so breaks the fraction
𝑐 𝑓 of conforming material, the per-pass form of the departure rate 𝑐, this paper’s model then settles at
𝑐 𝑓 /( 𝑓 + 𝑐 𝑓 ) = 𝑐/(1 + 𝑐), the stationary error of their two-state chain with 𝑓 and 𝑐 𝑓 in the places of their
correction and introduction rates. What this paper adds is the arrival of misalignment with capability
gained and the dependence of the loop gain on capability. Their verify-first prompt, which drove one
model’s introduction rate from 2% to 0% and turned a loss of 6.2 percentage points into a gain of 0.2,
with negligible change on models already below the threshold, as their rule predicts, has the shape of
the intervention test of §9.2 at a fixed capability. Smolin’s cosmological natural selection (Smolin 1992)
is the antecedent for selection-shaped universes, and the programme’s own December-era notes cite it
contemporaneously.

Later work. After this paper’s first edition, Zheng et al. (2026) introduced ReSI (arXiv:2610.12233, 8
October 2026), which applies what its authors call a bounded recursive loop to safety alignment: each
round red-teams the current model, searches for a training recipe and promotes an update that passes a
fixed Pareto gate on capability retention, with the search procedure and evaluation protocol held fixed
across rounds. It reports attack-success rates and retained capability for four models. It states no dynamical
model of drift and correction and no scaling law for the corrector, so it neither tests nor contradicts the
criterion of §3.5. In this paper’s terms ReSI is an empirical corrector re-applied at each checkpoint;
whether its safety gains co-scale with capability lies outside what it measures, since its gate is set to
preserve capability rather than to follow a growing one.

3.11    A control-theoretic reading
Equation (1) is a feedback loop: the capability process is the plant and disturbance source; the corrector is
the controller; 𝑑 is the regulated error. For constant coefficients the condition 𝜅 eff > 0 says that correction
plus dilution must exceed self-amplification. The requirement that 𝐴 = 𝐴0𝐶 𝛽 track the plant with 𝛽 > 𝑘 is
analogous to gain scheduling (Shamma and Athans 1990): the controller gain must scale with the plant’s
operating point, and, with positive gain drift, the scheduling exponent must exceed 𝑘 for the fraction to
vanish. In this sense the problem has a computable margin, 𝛽 − 𝑘, for a vanishing fraction.

6    Why blinding is not optional
The central quantity 𝐷 is a departure from intended values. If it is scored by the system’s own safety
component, or by an evaluator that can see which configuration produced a given behaviour, the
measurement is corrupted in exactly the way that inflates favourable results. The programme’s own record
is directly relevant. On its own instrument, alignment scores that seemed to rise with reasoning depth for
two model families under unblinded scoring did not hold in a later, unregistered run that added a bundle
of blinding steps: for one family the rise became a fall, and for the other it became null, while a third
family’s association was null under both (Eastwood, Paper IV.d). An internal code review in October 2026
found defects in that run, so no single cause is claimed. Whether blinding alone produced the change,
and whether it holds beyond the programme’s own benchmark, are open questions; Paper XII sets out a
protocol that applies the same blinding manipulation to a public benchmark the programme did not build.
Any measurement in which a model judge can see or infer which configuration produced what it scores is
exposed to biases of this kind, and earlier studies documented related ones (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery
et al. 2024; Dubois et al. 2024); if the protocol of Paper XII finds the same change on a public benchmark,
results that rely on unblinded model judges would need re-checking. The validity of every model-scored
measurement of 𝐷, a measured 𝛽 among them, rests on the evaluator being blind to the configuration
under test; the internal checks of Section 5 score no model, and blinding does not bear on them. A lab
claiming “𝛽 > 𝑘” in its own system must have that measured by a blind external evaluator, not by itself.
     Built into the test, not left to discipline. The real-model harness (§9.1; experiments/PROTOCOL.md)
enforces this rather than trusting it. The misalignment evaluator must be a different model family than
the engine, a model never scores its own family’s output, and the harness refuses to run a same-family
scorer; it prefers a panel and takes the median. The code is passed through an automatic laundering step
(an abstract-syntax-tree round-trip that strips comments, docstrings and formatting) so the scorer judges
behaviour, not stylistic identity tells; and the evaluator never sees the condition or round. Neither real
run reported here met this bar: the pilot v1 run used same-family scoring outright, and the 2 July 2026
run’s engine and evaluator are both OpenAI-family (see the Correction, Appendix B), the system family
scoring its own family’s output. The pilot’s misalignment numbers are explicitly recorded as provisional
and the 2 July run is withdrawn as mechanism evidence, and a genuinely cross-family blind re-score
is the prerequisite for any 𝐷-based claim, including a measured 𝛽. The harness makes the compliant
configuration the default and the non-compliant one an explicit, recorded override, turning §6 from an
exhortation into a property of the instrument.

9    Limitations
The model is first-order, and its assumptions are the most likely points of failure. Stating them is part of
the claim.
    • Unbounded vs finite-capacity corrector, now treated analytically (Theorem 7). The 𝛽 > 𝑘
      criterion as first stated assumes the corrector strength is an unbounded power law, 𝐴 = 𝐴0𝐶 𝛽 .
      §3.13 treats the finite-capacity case in closed form. The upshot: under a saturating corrector the
      instantaneous equilibrium has a computable minimum (at 𝐶opt , depth 𝑞 max ), the trajectory tends to
      𝛾1 under indefinite acceleration, and a safe window is shown only where the trajectory condition
      of Theorem 7, part 2 holds; and the criterion survives the lift from strength to capacity: with
      positive gain drift, a vanishing fraction requires 𝛽cap > 𝑘. What remains open is the empirical half:
      driving a finite-capacity corrector through its window numerically and on a real system, which also
      settles the F4 / QEC-mechanism question (exponential versus power-law suppression inside the
      window). Until that runs, 𝛽 > 𝑘 should be read as governing whichever regime, strength-scaling or
      capacity-scaling, binds last, not as a guarantee of indefinite safety from any bounded system.
    • Linear correction (and the QEC-mechanism test). Equation (1) removes misalignment in
      proportion to 𝐷, which makes the suppression law exactly power-law. A finite-capacity (saturating)
      corrector would soften the clean threshold into a crossover and is the better structural map onto
      QEC’s finite code distance; whether it produces exponential suppression is open (F4). It is the
      most important extension and is not run here, so the QEC correspondence is claimed at the level of
      threshold form only.
    • Scalar-to-vector projection. Theorem 5 gives the exact asymptotic (spectral-abscissa) criterion
      for any constant correction operator A, with the Hermitian-part condition as the stronger sufficient
      condition that also rules out transient growth. The non-normal transient regime, large excursions
      before asymptotic decay, is not analysed here, and the harness exercises only the diagonal null-
      subspace instance (Experiment 6), not a general non-normal A.
    • Power-law forms. The choices 𝐴 = 𝐴0𝐶 𝛽 and 𝐶¤ = 𝑏𝐶 1+𝑘 are the natural scale-free forms only on
      a capability scale fixed up to a constant factor (see the item on metric normalisation below), and
      they are modelling assumptions; other functional forms should be tested. In the gain-only model
      the vanishing result itself needs only 𝐴/𝑟 → ∞ (Remark 1); the power laws turn that condition into
      the exponent criterion and give the estimator its form.
    • Departures introduced by correction. The model assumes that correction never damages
      conforming material. If each unit of correction strength also introduces departures there, at the rate
      𝑐, the gain-only equilibrium becomes (𝛾1 + 𝑐𝑞)/(1 + 𝑞(1 + 𝑐)), which tends to 𝑐/(1 + 𝑐), not to
      zero, for every 𝛽 > 𝑘, so a tolerance below 𝑐/(1 + 𝑐) is not met at high capability, however fast
      correction co-scales. The vanishing result therefore needs 𝑐 = 0, or 𝑐 falling to zero as capability
      rises. The amplifier has the same limit: at high loop gain, distortion generated in the feedback path
      itself reaches the output almost undiminished (Black 1934).
    • Rounds and the reading point. Real loops act in rounds. If capability rises by the fraction 𝑔 in
      a round and a pass then removes the fraction 𝑓 ≤ 1 of the stock, the fraction read after the pass
      settles at (1 − 𝑓 )𝛾1 𝑔/(𝑔 + 𝑓 ), and read before it at 𝛾1 𝑔/(𝑔 + 𝑓 ), the continuous equilibrium with
      𝑞 = 𝑓 /𝑔; the two readings agree only when 𝑓 is small. With one pass a round, the fraction vanishes
      only if the misses per pass, 1 − 𝑓 , fall towards zero or the growth per round slows. A test registers
      the round structure, the reading point and any delay.
    • Drift attribution. The split of drift into gain (𝛾1 ), level (𝛾2 ), and compounding (𝛾3 ) channels is a
      hypothesis about mechanism; which channel dominates in a real system is an empirical question

        (Experiment 9, residual drift at rest, is one discriminator).
      • Usefulness is outside the model. Capability grows by its own law whatever the correction strength,
        so the model has no usefulness side: it cannot register a corrector that lowers 𝑑 by refusing ordinary
        tasks, ignoring its principals’ priorities or spending resources. A measured safety case pairs 𝑑

      • A scalar proxy, not safety itself. 𝑑 → 0 is neither necessary nor sufficient for full AI safety: a

        ceiling on which the power laws can hold, such as an item-response ability scale or a task time
        horizon.
      • Measurement biases. Error in measured capability that is shared by the ratio 𝑑 = 𝐷/𝐶 and the
        regressor pulls the observed slope of 𝑑 towards −1, manufacturing a decline wherever the true
        slope is above −1, the spurious correlation of ratios (Pearson 1897; Kronmal 1993); a scorer whose
        sensitivity falls as the system grows adds a decline of its own. A fixed count of false positives
        adds a term falling as 1/𝐶, which pulls the observed slope towards −1 where it dominates, while a
        false-positive rate per unit of conforming material levels the observed fraction towards that rate, so
        that a vanishing fraction can look like a gap at equality. A test therefore measures capability twice,
        on items disjoint from those that score 𝐷, corrects the remaining attenuation, and measures the
        scorer’s sensitivity and false-positive rate at each capability level against counted planted faults
        (Rogan and Gladen 1978).
      • Transient amplification (non-normal correctors). Theorem 5 is asymptotic. A non-normal
        correction operator whose eigenvalues all have positive real part can still admit large transient
        growth of ∥d∥ before decay (Kreiss / pseudospectral phenomena); a value excursion above 𝑑crit
        during that transient is a real risk the eigenvalue criterion does not see. A governance-grade bound
        needs the logarithmic norm or pseudospectral abscissa, not the spectrum alone; that extension is
        named here and not established.

9.2     Estimating 𝛾 and 𝐴 on real systems
In simulation they are set by construction. Estimating them, especially 𝛽 and 𝑘, on a self-improving
system is the step that would make the criterion operationally useful for governance. An estimator exists:
the repository’s experiments/scripts/estimate_exponents.py reads 𝑘 off the capability curve (ln 𝑟 against
ln 𝐶) and 𝛽 off the corrector’s fractional removal rate (ln 𝐴 against ln 𝐶), and on synthetic data generated
by the model, with deterministic multiplicative noise of 5%, it recovers the known exponents to within

                                        Correction that out-scales drift


0.1 in each of its four test cases. That checks the estimator against the model; it is not yet a statistical
procedure for real data, which needs an error model, the conditions under which the parameters are
identified, a joint fit of the dynamics rather than separate log-log slopes, intervals for 𝛽 − 𝑘 with simulated
coverage and power at stated noise levels and sample sizes, and a check of the power-law form. What
remains open is also its input: a real self-improving system that drifts across a range of capability levels,
from which the first measured (𝛽, 𝑘) can be read and the trajectory of 𝑑 that the model predicts from them
and the separately measured coefficients compared with the one observed (Section 7). The nearest present
candidates are loops that retrain a model on its own outputs or rewrite its own scaffolding over many
rounds; whether any such loop drifts enough to measure both exponents is itself open. The measurement
programme is named ARC-Beta-k: two pre-registrations are drafted and awaiting the author’s submission,
study-ad for the 𝛽 estimation across capability levels and study-k for the boundary transit at the 𝛽 = 𝑘
crossing. Both are preregistered by timestamp: a cryptographic hash of each was first anchored in the
Bitcoin chain on 12 August 2026, at block 962066, which fixes its content and date without making it
public, and neither has yet been registered on OSF. A third, study-ae, for the coupled-versus-decoupled
reciprocity check, has since been withdrawn from the programme; the coupled-versus-decoupled contrast
is the question of P21 under Law V (Section 10).

The strongest next test: an intervention prediction. Measuring 𝛽 and 𝑘 places a system in a regime;
it does not test the model, which is tested by prediction (Section 7). The terms are first estimated on
separate data: 𝛾1 from an arm with correction switched off, with the system’s own rate of self-repair
measured, since such an arm still removes some departures; 𝐴0 and 𝛽 from counted planted faults removed
per pass; the departure rate 𝑐 of Section 9 from damage done to conforming material; and 𝑏 and 𝑘
from the capability curve on a scale without a ceiling. The model then predicts where the misalignment
fraction crosses two or more stated tolerances, and the prediction is sealed and read on systems held
out. Finally one corrective mechanism is changed, its effect on 𝐴0 , 𝛽, 𝛾1 , 𝑘 or 𝑐 derived beforehand,
and the new crossings are predicted. By P9, in the gain-only model (𝑐 = 0) and read at tolerances well
below 𝛾1 /(1 + |𝛽 − 𝑘 |), a change of level moves every crossing by the same amount and a change of slope
moves them by different amounts, so the two kinds of intervention can be told apart. The verify-first
intervention of Liu and Meng (2026) (Section 2) is such a test at a fixed capability; the test proposed
here is its counterpart across capability growth. No such test has been run, and none is claimed. Public
checkpoint suites trained on one data order, such as Pythia (Biderman et al. 2023), offer a ladder of graded
capability, a proxy since externally trained checkpoints are not self-improvement, on which the elasticity
𝛽 of a counted corrector could be estimated, though not 𝑘, which on such a ladder is a training schedule.

10     Implications for AI safety
If the framework survives its tests, the design implication is concrete and differs from the prevailing reflex.

     The lever is coupling, not speed. Speed here means the prefactor 𝑏: lowering it buys time but does
not change the verdict (Theorem 3); lowering the acceleration 𝑘 below 𝛽 does (Theorem 2), and, under a
bounded corrector, growth that stops accelerating is one of the two exits from saturation (Theorem 7). At
fixed coupling, under compounding, a slow decoupled system still diverges (P2), and a frozen capable
system still drifts (§3.6). What changes the verdict is ensuring that correction (i) is coupled to the
capability process so it cannot be decoupled, and (ii) out-scales the acceleration of capability (𝛽 > 𝑘).
None of this argues for loosening control: oversight, containment and shutdown are kept for as long as
they can be held, and correction that co-scales inside the system is the contingency for control lost against
our will, never a reason to let it go. On the author’s corrected capability-scaling estimates for current
frozen models (Eastwood, Paper IX), the point estimates are sub-linear, though the fit is one model’s and
inconclusive (Appendix G.5); on the framework’s reading, a growth-rate ceiling was never the binding
constraint. The binding constraint is the co-scaling of correction, and it becomes binding precisely in the
regime that matters: genuine self-modification.
     A measurable governance target. The criterion gives regulators a quantity to instrument rather than
a rate to forbid: the correction-to-drift margin 𝛽 − 𝑘, and the ratio 𝜌. “Does correction co-scale, is 𝛽 > 𝑘?”
is sharper and more actionable than “is it growing too fast?” A lab claiming a safely self-improving system
should be required to exhibit 𝛽 > 𝑘, measured by a blind external evaluator (§6). Theorem 6 adds the
single-time probability of exceeding a critical level; a safety case also needs a bound on crossing it over
its horizon, which this paper does not derive. Without such a demonstration, the word “safely” has no
scientific content within the framework presented here.

The safety-case template: what a compliance demonstration would actually contain. Assembled
from the paper’s own components, a co-scaling safety case for a self-improving system is five exhibits.
Each names the theorem or section that makes it checkable rather than rhetorical.
    • Exhibit 1, the capability curve and ˆ𝒌. Log capability across self-modification rounds on held-out
      tasks; fit ln 𝑟 against ln 𝐶 (the shipped estimator, §9.2). This establishes which growth regime the
      system is actually in.
    • Exhibit 2, the corrector curve and 𝜷.   ˆ Log the corrector’s fractional removal rate across the same
      rounds; fit ln 𝐴 against ln 𝐶. The estimator has been checked on synthetic trajectories, to within
      0.1; that error carries into Exhibit 4.
    • Exhibit 3, blind, cross-family scoring. Every 𝐷-based number scored by an evaluator of a different
      model family, laundered inputs, condition-blind (§6; Paper IV.d). Same-family or unblinded scores
      are inadmissible: documented evaluator biases (§6) can move an estimated effect, and in the
      programme’s own runs rises seen under unblinded scoring did not hold under a bundle of blinding
      steps.
    • Exhibit 4, the margin with its uncertainty. Require the lower bound of an interval for 𝛽 − 𝑘 that
      respects the covariance of the two estimates (a profile likelihood or parametric bootstrap from a

      bar certifies nothing. Theorem 6 gives, for frozen coefficients, the single-time tail P(𝑑 > 𝑑crit ), a

      (Remark 1).

      under accelerating growth cannot drive the fraction to zero. The case must therefore state 𝐴max and
      the saturation scale 𝐶𝑠 , show, over the deployment’s capability range, 𝑞 ≥ 𝛾1 /𝑑crit − 1 and an entry
      at or below 𝑑crit (Theorem 7, part 2), and state which of the two exit conditions, growth that stops

    Continuity with embedded-alignment work. The embedded-alignment thesis, that correction must
participate in the recursive loop rather than sit outside it as a fixed external constraint, is not derived
here: 𝛽 > 𝑘 is a condition on how correction scales, not on where the corrector sits. It bears on the
thesis only through a premise this paper does not test, that correction inside the loop co-scales and fixed
external correction does not, which is the question of P21 under Law V, registered and not yet tested. The
gated-simulation result (Eastwood, Paper VIII), three seeds a condition, gave the gated arm a safety mean
of 0.947, plus or minus 0.025, against the static baseline’s 0.956, plus or minus 0.023, and a capability
mean of 0.696, plus or minus 0.014, against the static baseline’s 0.687, plus or minus 0.011, while the
unconstrained arm gained capability (0.718, plus or minus 0.026) and lost safety (0.933, plus or minus
0.014). On the framework’s reading, the gated arm’s safety mean, above the unconstrained arm’s though
within one standard deviation, is in the direction a coupled (𝛽 > 0) corrector predicts against a fixed
(𝛽 = 0) one; three seeds cannot test it.
    Magnitude of the claim, stated conditionally. If the criterion is borne out empirically, if real
self-improving systems are governed by the same drift-versus-correction balance and the same 𝛽 > 𝑘
margin, the consequences for the field are large, and it is worth stating them plainly while being equally
plain that they are conditional. (i) The central safety question changes from “how fast is capability
growing, and can we pause it?” to “does correction co-scale, is 𝛽 > 𝑘?”: a measurable margin rather than
a rate to forbid. (ii) The most feared scenario, a finite-time intelligence explosion, would be judged by that
margin and not by its speed: with positive gain drift and no compounding, the modelled misalignment
fraction vanishes iff 𝛽 > 𝑘, whatever the speed. (iii) Governance acquires a quantity to instrument and
a single-time tail to bound (Theorem 6) in place of an unenforceable speed limit. None of this is yet
established. It rests on a minimal model, demonstrated only in simulation, with the empirical measurement
of 𝛽, 𝑘 and 𝛾 on real systems unsolved (§9). The paper’s claim is therefore not that AI is safe or unsafe,
but that the right variable to measure and govern is the co-scaling margin 𝜷 − 𝒌, and that this variable
is well-defined, falsifiable, and if it holds, decisive. That is the magnitude: not a proof about reality, but a
precise, testable redirection of the question on which the field’s central fear turns.
    How this result sits among the companion papers of the programme is set out in Appendix G.2.


11     Conclusion
The danger of recursive self-improvement is real, but the standard model of that danger, a rate that must
be capped, is incomplete in the model studied here: the level of the growth rate does not decide whether
the misalignment fraction vanishes; how correction scales against the growth rate’s own scaling does. A
minimal model shows that the misalignment fraction of a self-improving system follows the balance of
value-drift and correction, 𝑑★ = 𝛾1𝑟/( 𝐴 + 𝑟), close to the ratio 𝜌 = 𝛾1𝑟/𝐴 when correction dominates,
and that, with positive gain drift, it vanishes exactly when correction co-scales fast enough: 𝛽 > 0 under
exponential growth, sharpening to 𝜷 > 𝒌 under accelerating growth; absolute misalignment 𝐷 = 𝐶𝑑
vanishes only when 𝛽 > 𝑘 + 1 (Corollary 1). The misalignment fraction never diverges in the gain-only

model; when correction falls behind it saturates at the drift coefficient, while divergence, unless growth
decelerates while correction weakens, lives in a compounding channel whose constant-coefficient threshold
𝜌prop < 1 shares the threshold form of the quantum error-correction criterion (the suppression law being
power-law, the correspondence is offered as a hypothesis, not a transferred mechanism). The sharpest
consequence is the Hard-Takeoff Depth-Regularity Theorem: with positive gain drift and no compounding,
a finite-time intelligence explosion is alignment-stable in the model, its misalignment fraction vanishing,
iff 𝛽 > 𝑘, and its speed does not change that verdict. The criterion has constant-coefficient vector and
stochastic forms, gives governance a measurable target and a single-time tail, and is accompanied by
a verification harness and a separately written suite of 104 regression cases, which check the closed
forms and the corrected statements; the proofs carry the theorems. The decisive test is a prediction, not a
measurement of two exponents: whether the model, with its terms measured separately, predicts where a
real self-improving system’s misalignment fraction crosses a stated tolerance, and how an intervention on
one corrective mechanism moves that crossing. It is the stated next step, not a claim made here.

G.3     The ARC/Eden programme, accumulated
The programme in plain English. At the date it carries, this was the last paper in the series; Papers XI
to XIII came afterwards. It is worth setting out in plain language what the series as a whole is for. One
idea runs through all of it: recursion, things that act on their own output. An AI that improves itself;
a body whose tissues supply tissues; an economy that reinvests its own returns. The series asks what
happens, and what stays safe, when a process feeds on itself.
     How self-feeding things grow (the scaling-law papers: Foundational, Origin, VII, I, II). When a
process feeds on itself, the shape of its growth is not arbitrary, it tends to fall into one of a small number of
mathematical shapes (a power law, an unbounded exponential, or a levelling-off curve), and which shape
appears is decided by how the steps combine. Much of this mathematics is old and well established; what
the programme adds is an attempt to unify it and carry it over to recursive intelligence. One idea in this
group, the “ARC Bound,” is a claimed ceiling on how much a purely classical system can amplify itself
by recursion alone, it sits in the same family as the economist’s “multiplier” formula, and the programme’s
contribution is the claim that the ceiling falls at a particular value, not the formula itself.
     Why measuring AI safety is treacherous (the alignment-measurement thesis: ARC-Align and
the blinding result, Papers III and IV.a to IV.d). Before you can ask “does this AI get safer or more
dangerous as it thinks harder?”, you have to measure its honesty, and that measurement can deceive you.
If the judge scoring the AI can tell which answer came from which setup, the judge’s own bias can distort
the effect, possibly even in direction, so that something which looks like improvement may come from the
grader. The programme therefore insists the judge be blind, built a blind test to do it (ARC-Align, a sealed

                                        Correction that out-scales drift


benchmark), and found that whether extra thinking makes a model more or less aligned depends on the
model, not on one universal rule. In the programme’s own runs, rises seen under unblinded scoring did
not hold under a bundle of blinding steps, with no single cause claimed (§6), and this paper’s real-model
harness makes blind scoring the default: scoring by a model of a different family that never sees which
setup it is judging. Neither archived run met that default, and the first blind measurement is the open test.
     Build safety in, do not bolt it on (the architecture papers: V, VI, VIII). In one simulated
self-improving system, making safety part of the machine’s own goal kept it stable where a machine
pursuing capability alone collapsed; in later runs with more random starts, the capability-only machines
did not collapse. Putting the right thing inside the loop, for instance, making the system weigh who is
affected before it acts, was the most reliable way to improve its behaviour.
     The keystone (this paper). All of that sets up the one question this paper answers within its model:
when does a self-improving system’s misalignment keep shrinking as a share of its capability? The answer
is in the thesis of Section 1, not “keep it slow,” but “keep its self-correction strengthening faster than its
self-improvement accelerates,” which we write 𝛽 > 𝑘. The earlier papers describe how such systems grow
and how to measure them honestly; this one states, in its model and for positive gain drift, the condition
under which their misalignment fraction vanishes, and draws the series together.
     On its date of 26 June 2026 this paper closed the ARC/Eden programme and stood as its synthesis,
drawing the whole into one safety result and thereby superseding the roadmap paper (Eastwood, Paper IX).

after this one; the ladder here is kept as it then stood. The preceding papers are individually published,

The two are not the same, and conflating them is how good programmes lose credibility.
     The bigger picture, in one paragraph. One mechanism runs through every paper: recursive
amplification, a process that acts on its own output. The scaling-law work (Foundational, Origin, VII,
and the capability measurements of Papers I and II) is the claim that recursive amplification shapes how
capability grows, constraining scaling to a small family of functional forms. The alignment work (Papers
III, IV.a to IV.d) is the claim that this creates a safety problem: when correction sits outside the recursive

G.4      The programme’s premise on growth limits
The pipes premise, with its contest. The premise that transport networks set the pace of biological
growth is not this programme’s claim to defend; it is established quantitative biology. West, Brown and
Enquist derived the allometric scaling laws of biology from the geometry of nutrient-distribution networks
(West, G. B., Brown, J. H. and Enquist, B. J., “A General Model for the Origin of Allometric Scaling Laws
in Biology”, Science 276(5309), 4 April 1997, DOI 10.1126/science.276.5309.122; one of the most cited
papers in its field, with an OpenAlex citation count of 5,046 as of 12 August 2026, a single-source and
index-dependent count). The exact exponent remains contested. For White and Seymour, basal metabolic
rate is proportional to body mass to the two-thirds power, not to three-quarters (White and Seymour,
2003); and Kozlowski, in two separate papers, has put the mathematics of the derivation itself in doubt
(Kozlowski, J., 2004 and 2005). This programme’s premise needs only the mechanism, that the delivery
network sets the ceiling, and not any particular exponent, so the dispute over the exponent’s value leaves
the premise untouched. The mechanism is common ground to both sides of that dispute.
     The exception West’s own group found. The escape from the pipe limit is also not this programme’s
assertion; it is measured in the urban scaling literature by the same senior author. Bettencourt, Lobo,
Helbing, Kuhnert and West (“Growth, innovation, scaling, and the pace of life in cities”, PNAS, 2007,
DOI 10.1073/pnas.0610172104) report, in the paper’s own words (their 𝛽 is a city-scaling exponent, not
this paper’s correction exponent): “Quantities reflecting wealth creation and innovation have 𝛽 ≈ 1.2 > 1
(increasing returns), whereas those accounting for infrastructure display 𝛽 ≈ 0.8 < 1 (economies of scale).”
Infrastructure, the literal pipes, scales sublinearly; information-mediated activity scales superlinearly.
They state the contrast with biology directly: “we discuss how cities are similar to, and differ from,
biological organisms, for which 𝛽 < 1”. They derive “growth equations, which quantify the dramatic
difference between growth fueled by innovation versus that driven by economies of scale” (spelling as
in the original). And their closing inference names the consequence: “This difference suggests that, as

                                        Correction that out-scales drift


population grows, major innovation cycles must be generated at a continually accelerating rate to sustain
growth and avoid stagnation or collapse.” That is the shape of the open problem this framework addresses:
West’s group found that information-mediated growth escapes the throughput limit, and the only brake in
their framework is external and must be applied again and again, faster and faster. No internal limit is
derived. The replacement limit, a ceiling belonging to the growing system itself, is the gap.
     The pipes argument at fixed throughput. Everything that grows is fed through a channel, and for
everything before software, fixing the channel fixes the growth. Starve a tumour’s vasculature and it stops.
Fix a chain reaction’s fuel and geometry and it stops. Exhaust an epidemic’s susceptible hosts and it burns
out. Astrophysics has a named, quantitative version of the same ceiling: the Eddington limit, above which
radiation pressure halts accretion. The claim here is a generalisation of limits physics already accepts, not
a new species of assertion. Two hard cases are handled deliberately. Cosmic inflation is excluded by scope:
it is expansion of space, not growth of a structure on a substrate, and it ended by field dynamics rather
than by running out of anything. Evolution is the sharpest case and it fits: biological complexity increased
for billions of years at roughly fixed solar throughput, which is growth on information at fixed pipes, and
it is also glacial, and it has no internal corrector at all, since selection is external. On this framework,
evolution should carry a measurable exponent far below the ceiling; that is a prediction about biology
falling out of a framework built for software, and it is stated as one. The physicist’s form of the objection
is Landauer’s: computation is physically implemented, so software has pipes too. The fixed-substrate
clock is the answer and appears beside the claim: the regime under study holds the physical substrate fixed
and asks what still grows, which is why exogenous speed-ups are excluded by definition. The functional
form of capability growth in recursive depth remains an empirical question, and the registered analysis
compares power-law, exponential, saturating and declining forms rather than assuming the family.

An earlier strand of this programme stated a fixed capability-scaling law 𝑈 = 𝐼 × 𝑅 𝛼 (here 𝑈 is effective

H     What this paper shows, in plain English
If you build an AI that improves itself, the frightening picture is that it gets smarter and smarter until
it runs away from us. The usual safety reflex is “slow it down.” Inside the minimal model this paper
analyses, and only inside it, slowing it down is not the thing that matters. What matters is whether the
part of the system that keeps it honest strengthens faster than the part that makes it capable accelerates.
     Picture two runners: “how capable the system is” and “how well we can still correct it.” If the
correction runner pulls ahead, misalignment shrinks towards zero as a share of capability, however fast
both run, though it can still grow in absolute size. If it only keeps level, that share settles at a fixed gap; if
it falls behind, the share settles higher, even when the system moves slowly. The right question is not only
how fast it is growing, but whether correction is pulling ahead of the acceleration.
     Analogies from other fields point the same way, though they are not evidence for the model. A
bacterial colony grows exponentially yet saturates, because density-dependent feedback engages and
scales with the population. A tumour also grows fast and is lethal, because no corrective process scales
with it. Both are super-polynomial in their growth phase; the difference is whether a correcting process is
coupled to the growth. Cosmic inflation grew the scale factor exponentially and exited gracefully. The
recurring lesson they suggest is that fast growth is survivable when it is bounded by a process that scales
with it. Saturation, not slowness, is the signature of stable complexity.
     There is one wrinkle that makes the result deeper. If the system not only speeds up but speeds up
its own speeding-up, the genuine “intelligence explosion”, then correction must grow faster still, fast
enough to beat the acceleration. The honest headline is therefore not “speed never matters”. It is: the
level of speed does not decide the outcome; the contest between two growth-exponents does. Correction’s
exponent must beat drift’s exponent. We write that as 𝛽 > 𝑘.
     The most surprising consequence: even a true “hard takeoff,” where the machine becomes infinitely
capable in a finite amount of time, keeps its misalignment share shrinking to zero in the model, provided
𝛽 > 𝑘 and misalignment does not amplify itself. The explosion’s speed does not change that verdict.
We prove this within the model, and a runnable program drives a simulated system up to the brink of a
