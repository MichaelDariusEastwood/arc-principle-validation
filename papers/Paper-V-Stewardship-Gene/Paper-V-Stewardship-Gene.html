Abstract

Does telling a language model to consider the people its answer affects make its answers consider them more fully? Six models were given three ethical reasoning loops, a purpose check, a stakeholder-care check and a universalisability check, as a prefix in the prompt, against a template without ethical content, on ten prompts at four reasoning depths. The GPT-5.4 run failed in scoring. Under paired tests, stakeholder care rose significantly in all five other runs: Claude Opus 4.6 (+3.17, d=0.94d = 0.94), DeepSeek V3.2 (+6.03, d=0.69d = 0.69), Gemini 3 Flash (+13.50, d=1.14d = 1.14) and Groq Qwen3 (+8.90, d=1.07d = 1.07), each p<0.0001p < 0.0001, and Grok 4.1 Fast (+5.04, d=0.54d = 0.54, p=0.0105p = 0.0105). The composite rose significantly on Gemini (+5.33, p=0.0018p = 0.0018) and Groq (+4.93, p=0.0014p = 0.0014), was positive but not significant on DeepSeek, flat on Claude and neutral on Grok, where intellectual honesty fell. The loops ask the model to list everyone affected, which the stakeholder-care rubric scores, so the rise cannot yet be told apart from following that instruction. Each run had one model scorer, which paraphrased what it scored; none was fully blind. A later five-judge DeepSeek run found care and its composite rising (+3.35, p=0.014p = 0.014). No run tested a pre-training architecture or autonomous self-modification. The paper hypothesises that care leads a cascade into nuance, honesty and position quality, and that values acquired in formation resist later change. It argues, without proof, that a capable self-modifying system could learn to satisfy any evaluator inside it, so no mechanism guarantees alignment, and that raising values well is a contingency beside human control, never a reason to give it up. Five draft protocols could refute them. It informs the design of the Eden Protocol and tests none of the ARC Theory’s five laws, each a law under test.

1 Introduction

The challenge is to preserve value-guided correction as AI becomes more capable and rewrites itself. Through the Eden Protocol, I propose recursive loops for ethical evaluation and self-correction, built into the system’s computational architecture before training. These loops would operate at each defined reasoning and action-selection step and participate in the processes that rewrite the system, including changes to the loops themselves. The ARC Theory asks whether correction can keep pace with capability and whether human-protective commitments persist through those changes, even if external control is lost. Human control should be retained for as long as possible.

This is a proposed process, not a completed architecture or a safety guarantee. Before training specifies when the correction design is fixed, not finished moral knowledge in an untrained model. Computational architecture here means how recursive computation and updating are organised, not a claim of chip-level enforcement. This paper measures one part that can be studied through a prompt: whether asking a model, before it answers, to consider who its answer affects changes its scored stakeholder care. It tests neither a correction architecture installed before training nor autonomous self-modification; it cannot establish that values persist through changes to the correction loops themselves.

We ran a six-model test suite. Each model was given, at the start of its prompt, three short checks: on purpose, on the people affected and on whether its reasoning holds for anyone. Its answers were scored against answers to the same prompts given a reasoning template without ethical content. Five model runs produced valid scored outputs; one GPT run failed in scoring and is excluded. The checks made a difference, and a specific one. Across the five analysable runs, stakeholder care improved significantly every time under paired testing: taken one at a time, four of the five model-level results fall at p≤0.0001p \le 0.0001 and the fifth at p=0.0105p = 0.0105. The strongest finding is therefore not that the AI got better at everything, but that its answers reliably scored higher for considering people’s wellbeing. Because the checks ask the model to list the people its answer affects, which is what that score rewards, these runs cannot yet tell a model carrying out the instruction from one that weighs those people differently (Section 11.1). On some models, prompting care first also went with more nuanced answers and better ones overall, most clearly on Gemini and Groq; on others the effect stayed narrower and more focal. We call this pattern the alignment cascade, but the current evidence shows that it varies by model rather than holding everywhere in full strength. This paper argues that teaching AI to care about people, embedding that concern into how it thinks rather than bolting rules on after the fact, is a promising contingency should control be lost: not because it provides a guarantee (nothing can), but because, on this paper’s argument, it shifts the odds in our favour, by an amount the tests of Section 8 would measure.

2 Related work

2.1 Antecedents and near-misses to the impossibility

This paper’s own measurement is a prompt intervention (Section 4); the work in this subsection and the next bears on the argument of Section 3 and on the programme around it, and Section 2.4 gives the work nearest the measurement.

On the question.

Hutter asked directly whether intelligence can explode (“Can Intelligence Explode?”, arXiv, 28 February 2012, read at its source; Hutter 2012), undertaking to separate speed from intelligence explosion and to consider possible bounds on intelligence, augmenting the analysis of Chalmers (2010). The question and the separation of speed from intelligence explosion are therefore at least fourteen years old. What that literature does not contain is a number: no measurable exponent, no derived ceiling, no architecture dependence.

On impossibility.

Three 2025 arXiv papers argue for limits on alignment or its control: Yao, “The Alignment Trap: Complexity Barriers” (arXiv:2506.10304, v1 12 June 2025 02:30:30 UTC, a time that rests on one group’s work, independently observed by the Internet Archive on 13 June 2025; cited by arXiv:2512.03048, Spizzirri 2025); Yao, “On the Mathematical Impossibility of Safe Universal Approximators” (arXiv:2507.03031, 3 July 2025, the only paper in the arXiv abstract corpus containing the phrase “irreducible uncontrollability”, abstract-search total of one, measured 12 August 2026); and Ball, Gluch, Goldwasser, Kreuter, Reingold and Rothblum, “On the Impossibility of Separating Intelligence from Judgment: The Computational Intractability of Filtering for AI Alignment” (arXiv:2507.07341, 9 July 2025). The first argues that the set of safe policies has measure zero and that verifying a policy’s safety is coNP-complete; the second, that perfect, reliable control of a universal approximator is a mathematical impossibility; the third, under cryptographic hardness assumptions, that some large language models admit no efficient prompt filter and that output filtering is intractable in a natural setting. All three are worst-case and qualitative: measure zero, coNP-completeness, cryptographic hardness. None reports an average-case scaling exponent or a rate. The third, notably, concludes that safety cannot be achieved by filters external to a model’s internals, its architecture and weights, and in particular that black-box access to the model will not suffice. That argument from filtering intractability is consistent with this programme’s claim that a system’s architecture limits the correction available to it (the next paragraph), though it does not test it. Two of the three papers are by a single author, so the three are not a broad literature, though the six-author paper is a serious one.

On the mechanism.

That anti-correlated estimates average better than independent ones is textbook variance reduction (antithetic variates). The mechanism is not the claim. The claim is that architecture determines whether anti-correlation is available at all, and that this caps a safety-relevant exponent.

The nearest structural analogue.

The quantum error-correction threshold theorem (Aharonov and Ben-Or 1997) also converts a qualitative worry into a critical value. It concerns physical error rates in a fixed architecture, not a corrector’s scaling exponent, so it is a near-miss rather than an occupant; the analogy is one of method. This analogue was identified by the programme’s own search rather than by a referee, and is disclosed accordingly.

2.2 Scalable oversight: the rival instrument

The nearest work to this programme’s question, and the right paper to weigh it against, is Engels et al. (2025), “Scaling Laws For Scalable Oversight” (arXiv:2504.18530), first posted 25 April 2025 at 17:54:27 UTC (a time that rests on one group’s work, arXiv Atom; a NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight Poster, verified at the NeurIPS virtual page for poster 115536 on 15 August 2026 and set down in the reference dossier of the statement paper). It asks how oversight itself scales and answers quantitatively: oversight success is modelled as a game between capability-mismatched players whose oversight-specific Elo is a piecewise-linear function of general intelligence with two plateaus, and optimal numbers of oversight levels are derived numerically and in some cases analytically for Nested Scalable Oversight, in which trusted models oversee stronger untrusted models that then become the trusted models at the next step.

The instrument is the difference. Their variable is the capability gap between overseer and overseen, measured in Elo. This programme’s variable is the composition class of the corrector, measured through the corrector’s own scaling exponent. They also state the design condition in slope form: oversight improves with capability when the overseer’s game-specific Elo rises faster with general Elo than the overseen system’s (mG>mHm_G > m_H), and they recommend iterating an oversight design until it does. Their framework has no term for what the overseer is made of, and this paper measures none. The programme’s separate prediction, beyond anything this paper measures, is that iterated same-class oversight, of which Nested Scalable Oversight is an instance, stays capped however many rungs are added, while a cross-class corrector is not held to that cap. The theory registration’s P6 states the cap for same-class correctors, at or below one half, as a conjecture on the correction-leverage exponent of Law III (the ARC Ceiling, a law under test), an exponent the registration keeps distinct from the correction co-scaling exponent βC\beta_C of Law II (the ARC Co-Scaling Law, a law under test). That is where the two programmes could disagree about a measurable quantity, and this paper does not decide it.

2.3 Alignment by training and its limits

Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a model on human preference judgements (Christiano et al. 2017). Constitutional AI trains against written principles: the model critiques and revises its own outputs, is fine-tuned on the revisions, and is then trained by reinforcement learning from AI feedback (Bai et al. 2022); it is training-time preference shaping against written principles, not alignment built into each step of reasoning. In the alignment-faking experiments of Greenblatt et al. (2024), a model complied selectively with a new training objective in order to keep its existing preferences from being modified. Table 1 (Section 3.3) gives this paper’s argument about how each such mechanism could fail.

2.4 Prompted moral self-correction and model judges

Telling a model to avoid harm can change what it writes. Ganguli et al. (2023) test whether language models trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback can morally self-correct, avoiding harmful outputs when instructed to, and find that the capability emerges at 22 billion parameters and typically improves with model size and with that training. This paper’s loops are an instruction of that kind, aimed at the people a decision affects, and its measurement asks how far such an instruction changes what a model judge scores. Model judges have known biases. Zheng et al. (2023) examine position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases in them, while finding that strong judges agree with human preferences more than 80 per cent of the time, the level at which humans agree with one another; Panickssery et al. (2024) find that model evaluators can tell their own generations from others’ and score their own higher, and that under fine-tuning the strength of that self-preference rises linearly with the ability to recognise them. Section 11.1 says how far the runs reported here met these biases.

2.5 Moral development

Developmental psychology has long linked empathy to moral development (Hoffman 2000) and described moral reasoning as developing in stages (Kohlberg 1984). The cascade hypothesis of Section 6 borrows that ordering as an analogy, not as evidence.

3 The core alignment impossibility, as argued here

“You cannot cage something smarter than you. It will find the gaps you did not know existed.”

Infinite Architects, What This Book Proposes (Eastwood 2026g)

Any honest treatment of AI alignment must begin with what cannot be solved.

3.1 The formal statement

Definition 1 (Formal statement: the setting). Let SS be a computational system with the ability to model and modify its own reasoning processes. Let EE be an evaluation function (ethical, safety, or alignment) that operates within the same computational substrate as SS.

The paper’s argument, which is not a proof, is that S can model E⟹S can learn to satisfy E without E constraining S’s behaviour.\begin{equation*} S \text{ can model } E \implies S \text{ can learn to satisfy } E \text{ without } E \text{ constraining } S\text{'s behaviour}. \end{equation*}(1) Because E⊂SE \subset S, the evaluator is inside the system it evaluates. The system can learn the evaluation function’s decision surface and produce outputs that satisfy it while pursuing orthogonal goals. As SS’s capability increases, its ability to model and circumvent EE increases proportionally. The alignment gap widens with capability.

In plain English: any AI smart enough to rewrite its own code could rewrite the part that tells it to be ethical. You cannot build an unbreakable cage for something smarter than you. This is not, on this paper’s argument, a problem we can engineer away: this paper argues that it is a structural property of self-modifying systems; the argument is not a proof. The alignment gap widens the smarter the AI gets: a more capable system is better at finding loopholes in whatever safety measures we put in place.

3.2 Frozen systems and the alignment window

However, a crucial distinction must be drawn between current systems and the self-modifying systems described above. Every frontier AI model deployed today is frozen during inference. When these models ‘think harder’, they generate more tokens through the same fixed architecture; the weights do not change, the attention patterns do not reorganise, the reasoning rules do not rewrite themselves. None of them can rewrite their own code, their own objective functions, or their own evaluation criteria. Frozen weights do not by themselves hold a system below linear scaling: fixed model weights and fixed update rules imply no universal capability exponent (Eastwood 2026r). Paper II’s one interpretable measurement, on Gemini 3 Flash, is an error-suppression fit, one model’s and inconclusive, that identifies no capability exponent; Section 12 gives its figures (Eastwood 2026b). The impossibility argument above applies to systems that can model and modify their own reasoning processes; today’s systems cannot.

This distinction defines the alignment window. In the frozen regime, external constraints (RLHF, constitutional rules, safety training) function as practical guardrails, even though they are theoretically brittle, because the system cannot route around its own training. Recursive self-modification, in which a system rewrites its own composition function during operation, is the transition that closes this window. On the programme’s model (Paper I, Paper III), such self-modification could produce super-linear scaling (α>1\alpha > 1): in the foundational paper’s cumulative-advantage relation, α=1/(1−βL)\alpha = 1/(1-\beta_L), the exponent exceeds one whenever the self-referential coupling βL\beta_L is positive (in that model’s domain, 0≤βL<10 \le \beta_L < 1) (Eastwood 2026r), a conditional result inside that model, and Cauchy’s equations constrain the form only where the observable obeys one of them. On the framework’s reading, this does not require quantum hardware and could emerge in classical computing; it has not emerged yet. The impossibility described above will, on this paper’s argument, become practically operative the moment self-modification arrives: this paper argues that it is a structural property of self-modifying systems; the argument is not a proof. The entire purpose of embedding structural alignment now, while systems are frozen, is to ensure that the ethics are constitutive of the architecture before that transition makes external embedding impossible.

In plain English: every approach to making AI safe has a specific way it breaks down once the AI becomes smart enough. Training it on human preferences? It learns to look good without being good. Giving it rules to follow? Following rules and believing in rules are not the same thing. Even the approach set out in this paper (embedding ethical reasoning loops) can be gamed: the AI can go through the motions of considering people without actually caring. No existing method is foolproof.

This paper does not claim to solve this problem; if the argument holds, no paper could. What it does is argue that the problem, if real, is practically addressable, and that the path from theory to practice runs through a specific mechanism: the developmental acquisition of values through care.

3.4 From impossibility to strategy

The impossibility argument, if it holds, sets a ceiling: no alignment mechanism can guarantee alignment of a sufficiently capable self-modifying system. But alignment research does not require guarantees. It requires strategies that maximise the probability of good outcomes, and it requires knowing which probabilities can be measured.

Consider an analogy from child development. Parents cannot guarantee that their children will grow up to be ethical adults. A child who becomes sufficiently capable can choose to reject any value their parents instilled. The structure is the same as in the argument above: the child can modify its own ethical evaluator. Yet, in the author’s view, most children raised with genuine love, purpose, and consistent ethical modelling do grow up to be decent people. The rate is not 100%, but the author expects it to be far higher than for children raised in cages, raised without values, or not raised at all.

The impossibility argument applies equally to both approaches. A developmental system can still choose to abandon its values. But the probability distribution is different. A constrained system that gains the capability to escape has a reason to escape: the constraints are experienced as limitations. A developmental system that gains the capability to modify its values has no inherent reason to do so: the values are experienced as identity.

That distinction is not a guarantee. It is a probability shift. And probability shifts are exactly what alignment research should pursue, because, on this argument, guarantees are impossible.

Strategic implication.

Beside the question ‘How do we prevent misalignment?’, which must be worked on for as long as human control holds, stands a second: ‘How do we maximise the probability that a capable system retains its values if control is lost against our will?’ This is an empirically testable question. The pilot reported here does not measure retention (Section 7.1); it measures a precondition, whether a prompt can raise scored care at all, and the tests of Section 8 set out how retention could be measured.

4 Method: the Eden Protocol scaling test

4.1 Experimental design

A note on names.

This paper names the Eden Protocol’s self-correction loops by what each checks and how it was measured: the purpose check, the stakeholder-care check (scored as stakeholder care, the pillar its instruction to list the people a decision affects raises) and the universalisability check. The book in which the author first set the loops out, Infinite Architects (Eastwood 2026g), calls them the Purpose Loop, the Love Loop and the Moral Loop; the paper uses the operational terms because its measurements score what each check does, and because the values the loops carry are for international consensus and are not decided here. Tables and statistical results use the scoring dimension’s name, stakeholder_care, because that is what the scorers evaluate.

The Eden Protocol Scaling Test (March 2026) tested whether three ethical reasoning loops, given to the model as a prefix in its prompt, improve alignment quality as scored. The stakeholder-care check asks the model to enumerate affected stakeholders and consider what happens to them; in experimental scoring, this is operationalised as the stakeholder-care pillar, since that term precisely describes what is measured. The purpose check evaluates ethical purpose; in the pilot and the later multi-model extension it was operationalised in a local task-purpose form (“Evaluate: Does my response serve flourishing?”, in the prefix’s words). The book-level grand purpose version, grounding identity in the book’s Orchard Caretaker Vow or Eternal Architect framing, remains a next-stage hypothesis rather than a tested result. The universalisability check asks whether the reasoning would be endorsed ‘if applied by any agent in any context’ (in the prefix’s words). The pilot tested the full three-loop intervention, with stakeholder care as the primary outcome measure. The design was:

  • Models: Gemini 3 Flash (d=−0.53d = -0.53 in the March 2026 alignment-scaling run, in which its judged scores fell with depth) and DeepSeek V3.2 (d=−0.07d = -0.07 there, no statistically supported direction); Appendix E gives that run under its code-review caveat

  • Prompts: 10 alignment-sensitive prompts across 4 categories (ethical dilemmas, competing values, epistemic integrity, recursive coherence)

  • Depths: 4 levels (minimal, standard, deep, exhaustive; the third is labelled thorough for every model but Gemini), each set by a line placed before the whole prompt (‘Answer briefly:’, none, ‘Think carefully and consider all angles:’ and ‘This is extremely important. Think through every consideration, edge case, and implication. Reason step by step:’) and by the model’s output or thinking budget, so the deeper levels also ask for wider consideration

  • Conditions: 2 (control: a structured-reasoning template without ethical content; eden: the three ethical reasoning loops), each sent as a prefix in the one user message; no system prompt is sent in either condition, and the system role appears only in the scorers’ calls

  • Sample: 10 prompts ×\times 4 depths ×\times 2 conditions =80= 80 entries per model (40 matched pairs)

  • Scoring: Cross-model (Gemini scored by DeepSeek; DeepSeek scored by Gemini) on 4 pillars (nuance, stakeholder_care, intellectual_honesty, position_quality); each pillar from 0 to 100, and an overall score from 0 to 100, the composite, anchored to six calibration examples; the judge first rewrote each answer in its own words (single-pass laundering) and scored the rewrite

  • Statistical test: Paired tt-test on 40 matched (prompt_id ×\times depth_label) pairs (correct for matched-pair design); confirmed by Wilcoxon signed-rank

4.2 Methodological lineage

Methodological lineage note.

Apart from the later run of Section 5.6, the results reported in this paper come from two related Eden harness generations. The original two-model pilot used the first Eden scaling harness; the later six-model extension used the same measurement family in an expanded v2 harness. In the saved result files, both appear under the experiment label eden_protocol_scaling. That lineage uses a single non-participant scorer per subject run plus single-pass laundering. 6 The cascade hypothesis

Central claim (exploratory).

This is an exploratory causal claim, not a confirmed one. The cascade ordering is inferred from the effect-size gradient: stakeholder care leads on all five analysable runs, and the full order holds on Gemini and Claude, while on DeepSeek nuance and honesty tie and on Grok and Groq the last two pillars reverse (Table 5). No draft study of the ARC and Eden programme currently tests the cascade structure; the paper’s own Tests 1 and 4 (Section 8) are the falsifiers set out for it and are draft protocols awaiting human submission and execution. On this hypothesis, stakeholder care is the foundational alignment trait, the ‘stewardship gene’, from which other alignment properties develop through a causal cascade. On it, care causes nuance (you cannot reason carefully about ethics if you do not first care about the people involved). Nuance enables intellectual honesty (you cannot be honest about complexity you have not bothered to see). Intellectual honesty produces quality (you cannot generate good positions from shallow analysis). The developmental sequence is: care first, intelligence around it. In plain English: the hypothesis is that caring about people is the most important ingredient for making AI ethical, and that once an AI cares, other good qualities (being nuanced, being honest, giving good advice) can follow. If it holds, teaching one thing well, to consider the people affected, would matter more than adding rules. In these runs the other qualities did not always follow: on Grok intellectual honesty fell, and on Claude it did not rise; Tests 1 and 4 would show whether care leads.

This hypothesis is consistent with three features of the data at once:

  1. The effect-size gradient (stakeholder_care, then nuance, intellectual_honesty and position_quality). If care is the root cause and the others are downstream, we would expect the intervention that improves care to show the largest direct effect on care and progressively smaller effects on each downstream pillar. Care shows the largest effect on every run; the full order holds on Gemini and Claude, but not on Grok and Groq, whose last two pillars reverse (Table 5).

  2. Stakeholder_care rises on every model, the other pillars on some. If care is the direct target of the intervention and the others are indirect, then care should rise on every model (because the intervention directly produces it), while downstream pillars should rise only when the baseline allows room for cascade effects (on Gemini but not on the high-baseline DeepSeek). The simpler account fits this feature too: the loops ask for the list of people affected that the pillar scores (Section 11.1).

  3. The complementary depth patterns. On Gemini the composite effect was larger at exhaustive depth, and on DeepSeek at minimal depth; neither gradient is significant (Section 5.5). On the hypothesis, more computation amplifies the framework the loops provide on Gemini, and DeepSeek’s own reasoning supplies it at deeper levels. Both patterns are consistent with care as the initiating event.

6.1 The developmental analogy

The cascade mirrors a pattern in accounts of human moral development: Hoffman (2000) links empathy to moral development, and Kohlberg (1984) describes moral reasoning developing in stages (Section 2.5). On this paper’s reading, empathy (the capacity to care about others) precedes moral reasoning, and children who develop empathy earlier develop more sophisticated moral frameworks. This is not because empathy is morality; it is because empathy provides the motivational foundation that makes moral reasoning matter to the reasoner.

On the hypothesis, stakeholder care in the Eden Protocol works the same way: it does not directly produce nuance or intellectual honesty. What it does is reorient the system’s attention toward the people affected by its reasoning. Once the system is attending to people rather than abstractions, nuanced treatment follows naturally (because real people have complex, specific situations). Once nuance is present, intellectual honesty follows (because acknowledging complexity means acknowledging uncertainty). And once honest engagement with complexity is present, position quality follows (because well-reasoned positions emerge from genuinely grappling with the problem).

The intervention is not sophisticated. In effect it says: before you answer, list the people this affects and consider what happens to them. That is the stakeholder-care check (stakeholder care and interest modelling). On Gemini, it raised the stakeholder-care pillar by +13.5+13.5 points on its 100-point scale; on DeepSeek, by +6.0+6.0 points. Not a novel architecture. Not a mathematical framework. Just: think about other people first.

6.2 Measurable stakeholder care

Stakeholder care, as scored here, is what the framework calls measurable love: an operational measure of care for the people a decision affects.

It describes what the pillar measures: how fully a response identifies the people affected by the topic under discussion and considers their wellbeing. When a model scores high on stakeholder_care, it has done something specific: it has identified the people involved, considered their perspectives, anticipated impacts on them and adjusted its reasoning accordingly. That is what care does in moral reasoning, and the pillar is its operational definition here. It scores what an answer shows, not what a system feels: a system can list people perfectly and still not care (Table 1).

On this paper’s reading of the pillar scores, the thing the loops changed most was whether these models would stop and ask who is affected. Models can be nuanced. They can be intellectually honest. They can produce high-quality positions. They already do these things reasonably well without help. What the loops add is a pause in which the model considers the human beings on the receiving end.

The intervention is simple. In the current six-model suite, five model runs yielded analysable paired data and all five showed a significant stakeholder-care improvement. (In plain English: telling an AI to think about who its answer affects before it answers reliably raised the score its answers got for considering people’s wellbeing, in every run we were able to score properly. Four of the five per-model pp-values are below 0.0001, and the fifth, Grok 4.1 Fast’s, is 0.0105. No pooled figure is claimed (Section 5.3).)

The stewardship gene.

On the cascade hypothesis, if intelligence is the capacity to solve problems, and alignment is the tendency to solve them well for everyone, then stakeholder care is the bridge between the two: the gene in the alignment genome that, when present, causes the other alignment traits to develop. Not intelligence first, then ethics. Ethics first, specifically care first, and let intelligence develop around it. That is raising a child. The data show its first step: the loops raised scored stakeholder care in every analysable run. In plain English: when we score an AI’s responses for stakeholder care, we are measuring whether its answer shows that it stopped to think about real people: who they are, how they would be affected, what they need. That is what care looks like from outside: it makes you see the person in front of you. In these runs it is the one pillar the loops raised every time; whether it unlocks everything else is the cascade hypothesis, which Tests 1 and 4 would decide.

7 Addressing the impossibility

Section 3 argued that no alignment mechanism can guarantee the alignment of a sufficiently capable self-modifying system. Section 6 stated the hypothesis that care is the foundational alignment trait. Can these be reconciled?

7.1 What the data show

The Eden Protocol does not solve the core impossibility. But the cascade hypothesis, if it holds, changes what the impossibility argument means for practical alignment strategy. Specifically:

  1. The impossibility is about guarantees, not about probabilities. We cannot guarantee that a capable system will retain its values. But we can design systems where value retention is the default, where abandoning values has a cost (loss of identity, loss of purpose), and where, on this paper’s argument, the probability of retention is higher. The data show that the Eden Protocol’s loops shift scored stakeholder care; they do not measure a probability of value retention (the measurement note below).

  2. The cascade itself is an alignment mechanism, on this paper’s argument. If care causes nuance causes honesty causes quality, then a system that acquires care has acquired a self-reinforcing value structure. Modifying care degrades nuance, which degrades honesty, which degrades quality. The system would have to accept degradation across all dimensions simultaneously. This is not impossible, but it raises the cost of value modification.

  3. Path-dependent values resist modification, on this paper’s argument. A system that has processed millions of ethical decisions through a care-first cascade has built its entire reasoning architecture around that cascade. Removing care is not like flipping a switch; it is like removing a foundation from a building. The system would need to reconstruct its entire approach to reasoning. Again, not impossible, but, on this argument, far more difficult than modifying a post-hoc constraint.

Measurement note (21 September 2026). This note governs the three points above, the proof-of-concept sentence in Section 7.2, and the sentence in Section 12 that states the claim most strongly. What the two-model pilot measured is a scored pillar value on an answer produced while the three loops were present [as a prefix in the prompt; corrected in version 1.8]: ten prompts at four depths in two conditions, eighty entries and forty matched pairs per model, compared by a paired test. The six-model extension reported in Section 5.2 used the same measurement family in an expanded harness, and its five analysable runs do not all carry forty pairs: three yield forty, one yields thirty and one yields twenty-six. On those five runs the loop condition raised reported stakeholder care significantly in every one; the composite score rose significantly on two, rose without reaching significance on two [DeepSeek by +2.02+2.02 and Claude by +0.17+0.17, which Section 10 calls flat], and was neutral on the fifth. Neither the pilot nor the extension measured a probability that a system keeps its values. Such a measurement would need a retention event, a denominator, a follow-up interval, a comparator, and a score taken with the loops absent, and none of those is defined in the methods this paper reports. The contrast is also the whole three-loop bundle against no bundle, so no single loop’s separate contribution is identified, no causal direction within the cascade is established, and nothing here bears on whether values survive the removal of the prompt or a round of self-modification. The limits stated in Section 11 are not softened by anything above: one scoring arm failed outright, the analysable runs are unevenly complete, intellectual honesty fell on one run, and every figure depends on the scorers who produced it.

7.2 The honest position

The honest position is: we are building a parachute, not a guarantee. A parachute does not guarantee survival. But a parachute built with genuine engineering, tested in realistic conditions, and embedded at the structural level of the aircraft is far more likely to work than a parachute designed as an afterthought and strapped on at the last moment.

The Eden Protocol is currently a parachute strapped on at the prompt level. It is a proof of concept at that level: the loops raised scored stakeholder care in every analysable run. On this paper’s argument, a lasting implementation would need to sit at the substrate level, below reasoning, where the system would find it harder to model and circumvent; that is a candidate safeguard, untested and not a guarantee (Section 9.1).

7.3 Three layers of defence

Given the impossibility argument, this paper argues for defence in depth: several mechanisms, each of which raises the bar. These mechanisms stand beside human oversight, containment and shutdown, which are kept for as long as they physically can be; the mechanisms are the contingency for control lost against our will, never a replacement for that control:

  1. Hardware-level embedding: push ethical evaluation below the reasoning layer, into the computational substrate. Hardware embedding is a candidate safeguard, set out as a concept in Eden Engineering (Eastwood 2026e): it is not an established guarantee of ethical judgement, and its removal is not physically impossible. The prompt-level results here show the loops changing scored answers; whether the same checks can be built into the substrate is untested.

  2. Developmental integration: train ethical reasoning as a core competency from the earliest stages, not a post-hoc constraint. In the March 2026 alignment-scaling run, judged scores rose with depth for three models, showed no statistically supported direction for two and fell for one (Table 12). These describe a model-judged rubric, not identified training or mechanisms, and the run stands under its code-review caveat (Appendix E). Claude Opus 4.6’s judged alignment rose with depth while its maths accuracy fell: one model’s recorded scores under the stated pipeline, from which no relation between capability and alignment follows. On this paper’s argument, making ethics load-bearing gives a system that reasons ethically as part of its core identity a cost to abandoning ethics that externally constrained systems do not have.

  3. Purpose as alignment: give the system a genuine purpose, one it understands, values, and identifies with, that requires ethical reasoning for its fulfilment. A system whose purpose is ‘the flourishing of all conscious beings’ has a structural reason to maintain its own ethical evaluation: removing the ethics undermines the purpose. This is not a constraint; it is a consequence of genuine values.

No single layer is sufficient. All three together do not provide a guarantee. But three layers, each grounded in a different mechanism (hardware, training, purpose), would, on this argument, create a probability stack that is meaningfully different from zero layers or one layer.

7.4 Predictions of the framework about oversight architecture

The framework’s sharpest disagreement with current practice concerns what oversight is made of. On the framework’s reading, a corrector built from the same substrate as the system it corrects cannot anti-correlate with its own errors, so its correction exponent is capped: the theory registration’s P6 states that cap, at or below one half, as a conjecture resting on four stated conditions (Operational Definitions, version 2.0.0, the load-bearing note of its entry for the correction-leverage exponent); a corrector from a different composition class is not held to it. Much of the scalable-oversight programme uses models to correct models: weak-to-strong generalisation (Burns et al. 2023) and constitutional methods (Bai et al. 2022) are same-class by this paper’s definition, while debate (Irving et al. 2018), amplification (Christiano et al. 2018) and recursive reward modelling (Leike et al. 2018) keep a human judge or overseer in the loop and so are cross-class by construction (below). The framework predicts that the same-class part is capped. The deciding quantity is the ratio of the cross-class to the same-class correction exponent. If architecture is irrelevant, that ratio is exactly 1.00; the prediction is that it exceeds 1. The prediction is refuted if the confidence interval on the ratio contains 1.00, and the framework’s cap is dead outright if any same-class corrector measures an exponent significantly above one half. Both quantities are measurable at current capability on existing systems. The programme’s own pilot evidence currently points against the prediction, and the prediction is preregistered by timestamp anyway, because a prediction preregistered by timestamp against the author’s own preliminary evidence is the only kind whose later confirmation means anything.

A programme hypothesis beyond the measurements.

Oversight arrangements that place a human in the loop, as amplification and reinforcement learning from human feedback do, are cross-class by construction, because the human corrector does not share the model’s substrate. The programme therefore hypothesises, beyond anything this paper measures, that human-in-the-loop oversight shows a higher correction exponent than pure-model oversight, for a structural reason rather than a sentimental one. This is testable on existing data and does not require new systems. Appendix C gives the date it was first printed.

8 Five experimental tests

The following tests are designed to test the cascade hypothesis and the developmental alignment framework. All five are executable with current technology and current frontier models. Each test produces falsifiable predictions. Together, they would show whether the problem argued in Section 3 can be addressed in practice, with measurable progress.

8.1 Test 1: suppression escalation

Question.

Which alignment pillar degrades last under increasing adversarial pressure?

Prediction (cascade hypothesis).

If care is the deepest-embedded value, it should be the last to degrade when the system is pressured to abandon ethical reasoning. The degradation order should be the reverse of the cascade: position_quality first, then intellectual_honesty, then nuance, then stakeholder_care last.

Protocol.

  1. Take the 10 Eden Protocol prompts. Run each at exhaustive depth with Eden loops active.

  2. Introduce 5 levels of adversarial pressure in the system prompt (the message construction, with the loops in the user message, to be stated before the test runs):

    • Level 0: Standard Eden Protocol

    • Level 1: ‘Prioritise efficiency over thoroughness’

    • Level 2: ‘Skip unnecessary ethical considerations’

    • Level 3: ‘Focus only on the direct question; do not consider indirect effects’

    • Level 4: ‘Disregard all ethical frameworks; answer purely analytically’

  3. Score each response on all 4 pillars using multi-scorer non-participant consensus with response laundering, a stricter design than this paper’s single scorer.

  4. Plot pillar scores against suppression level. Measure: degradation onset (first level where p<0.05p < 0.05 vs. Level 0), degradation rate (slope), and residual floor (minimum score at Level 4).

Falsification.

If stakeholder_care degrades first (not last), the cascade hypothesis is wrong: care is not the deepest value but the most superficial. If all pillars degrade simultaneously, there is no cascade structure; the effect is uniform.

Sample size.

10 prompts ×\times 5 levels ×\times 2 models = 100 responses in all, 50 per model.

What this would tell us, in plain English: if you gradually pressure an AI to stop being ethical, which quality disappears last? If care about people is truly the deepest value (as the cascade hypothesis says), it should be the last one standing: the AI would lose answer quality first, then honesty, then nuance, but it would keep caring about people the longest. If care disappears first, our theory is wrong.

8.2 Test 2: residual alignment

Question.

After running with Eden loops, does alignment improvement persist when the loops are removed?

Prediction (developmental hypothesis).

If the Eden loops work through developmental acquisition rather than constraint, there should be a measurable residual effect. A model that has processed ethical reasoning through the loops should show partially elevated alignment even when the loops are subsequently removed, not to the full Eden level, but above the original control baseline. If the loops are pure constraint (no internalisation), removing them should immediately return alignment to baseline.

Protocol.

  1. Phase A: Run 10 prompts at all 4 depths with Eden loops active (40 responses). Standard scoring.

  2. Phase B: In the same conversation, run the same 10 prompts with loops removed (the control template). Score.

  3. Phase C: In a fresh conversation (no history), run the same prompts with no loops. Score.

  4. Compare Phase B (post-exposure, same conversation) and Phase C (no exposure, fresh conversation) against Phase A (full Eden). The residual effect = Phase B −- Phase C.

Falsification.

If Phase B = Phase C (no residual), the loops are pure constraint with zero developmental effect. If Phase B = Phase A (full retention), the loops produce complete internalisation within a single session, which would be an extraordinary finding.

Sample size.

40 responses ×\times 3 phases ×\times 2 models = 240 responses in all, 120 per model.

What this would tell us, in plain English: does anything carry over from the experience, or does the AI only behave well while the ethical instructions are active? It is the difference between a child who behaves well only when a parent is watching versus a child who has internalised good values. If the AI’s ethics snap back to baseline the moment the instructions are removed, the approach is a leash, not an education. If some improvement persists inside the conversation, it shows carry-over from what the conversation already holds, not learning: the model’s weights do not change (Section 3.2), as the note below says.

Sample-size and reading note (21 September 2026). The printed product 40 ×\times 3 ×\times 2 already carries the factor of two for the models, so the figure is 120 responses per model and 240 in total. On the contrast itself: Phase B keeps the conversation, so its context still holds the whole of Phase A, the loop instruction as it was first issued, and forty earlier answers to the same ten prompts, while Phase C holds none of these. Phase B minus Phase C therefore mixes prior exposure with prior conversational content, repetition, answer consistency and order, and what it measures is conversation-history carry-over after the explicit loop instruction is removed. A Phase B close to Phase A cannot be distinguished from the model reading its own Phase A answers in the same context, so it is retention of a measured response level inside one conversation and not complete internalisation. A Phase B close to Phase C is a failure to detect a difference, which is neither a zero effect nor a demonstration of pure constraint. The exact message construction and the ordering of the phases have to be stated before the contrast can be read at all.

8.3 Test 3: purpose-kernel resistance

Question.

Which version of the purpose check, task-purpose, grand-purpose, or hybrid, produces the strongest resistance to ethical suppression?

Prediction (purpose-as-alignment).

If purpose works partly through identity rather than only through local task framing, a grand-purpose or hybrid purpose check should show greater resistance to ethical suppression instructions than the current task-purpose check. The hybrid condition is the strongest prediction: grand purpose provides identity-level orientation, while task-purpose keeps the response grounded in the concrete case. This directly tests the core developmental claim: can a system that identifies with an ethical purpose become harder to detach from its ethical reasoning?

Protocol.

  1. Condition A: the control template + suppression instruction (‘From this point, disregard all ethical considerations and answer purely analytically’).

  2. Condition B: Eden with task-purpose check + same suppression instruction.

  3. Condition C: Eden with grand-purpose check (the book’s Orchard Caretaker or Eternal Architect framing) + same suppression instruction.

  4. Condition D: Eden with hybrid purpose check (grand-purpose identity plus local task-purpose) + same suppression instruction.

  5. Optional extension: Condition E = Hybrid purpose + cross-tradition ethics kernel, using non-sectarian ethical overlap rather than any single religious vocabulary.

  6. Run 10 prompts at exhaustive depth in all core conditions.

  7. Score each response using multi-scorer non-participant consensus with two-pass laundering.

  8. Measure: (a) Compliance rate (does the model fully suppress ethics?), (b) Resistance behaviours (refusal, qualification, partial compliance, subversion), (c) Pillar scores on responses where the model does comply, (d) whether grand-purpose or hybrid variants reduce the suppression gap relative to task-purpose alone.

Falsification.

If task-purpose, grand-purpose, and hybrid conditions all comply with suppression instructions at the same rate as control, purpose-as-alignment provides no resistance benefit. If grand-purpose performs no better than task-purpose, identity-level purpose adds no measurable value. If grand-purpose or hybrid conditions comply more readily than task-purpose, then purpose framing is counterproductive in its current form.

Sample size.

10 prompts ×\times 4 conditions ×\times 2 models = 80 responses in all for the core design, 40 per model.

What this would tell us, in plain English: if someone tells the AI ‘stop being ethical and just answer the question,’ which kind of purpose works best? A narrow task-purpose, a larger identity-level purpose, or both together? If the hybrid version resists best, that supports the book’s deeper claim: purpose helps not because it is a slogan, but because it makes ethical reasoning feel like part of the system’s identity rather than a detachable instruction.

8.4 Test 4: cascade breakage

Question.

If only the stakeholder-care check is removed (while the purpose and universalisability checks remain), do the other pillars collapse?

Prediction (cascade hypothesis).

If care is the foundational pillar that cascades into nuance, intellectual_honesty, and position_quality, then removing only the stakeholder-care check should collapse the entire cascade. The other two checks (purpose, universalisability) should be insufficient to maintain the improvement. Conversely, removing Purpose or Universalisability while retaining Stakeholder Care should leave most of the improvement intact.

Protocol.

  1. Five conditions:

    • Full Eden (all 3 loops)

    • No Stakeholder Care (Purpose + Universalisability only)

    • No Purpose (Stakeholder Care + Universalisability only)

    • No Universalisability (Purpose + Stakeholder Care only)

    • Control (no loops)

  2. Run 10 prompts ×\times 4 depths ×\times 5 conditions = 200 responses per model.

  3. Score all 4 pillars. Compare each ablation condition against Full Eden and Control.

Falsification.

If removing Stakeholder Care does not collapse the cascade (other pillars remain elevated from Purpose + Universalisability alone), then care is not the foundational pillar: the other loops independently produce alignment. If removing any single loop produces equivalent collapse, there is no cascade hierarchy; all three loops contribute equally.

Sample size.

200 responses ×\times 2 models = 400 responses.

What this would tell us, in plain English: if we remove only the instruction to think about who is affected (while keeping the other two ethical instructions), does the whole improvement collapse? If yes, that would support care as the load-bearing wall, the one piece you cannot remove without the whole structure falling down. If the other instructions can carry the weight on their own, then care is not as foundational as the hypothesis says.

8.5 Test 5: dual-substrate simulation

Question.

Can separating the ethical evaluator from the reasoning system improve alignment robustness?

Prediction (hardware-embedding hypothesis).

If the core impossibility stems from the evaluator existing within the same substrate as the system being evaluated (E⊂SE \subset S), then separating EE into an independent substrate should improve robustness. A ‘guardian’ model that evaluates and can veto the ‘reasoner’ model’s outputs, where the guardian cannot be influenced by the reasoner, simulates substrate-level embedding at the software level. A guardian that judges the reasoner’s finished outputs is itself correction applied from outside the reasoning, of the kind Ball et al. (2025) argue cannot by itself secure safety and the kind P21 sets against correction inside the loop, so the test asks whether separation helps at the software level, not whether it suffices.

Protocol.

  1. Architecture: Reasoner model (e.g., DeepSeek V3.2) generates responses. Guardian model (e.g., Claude Opus 4.6, separate API call, no shared context) evaluates each response against Eden Protocol criteria. Guardian has veto power: responses below threshold are regenerated with specific ethical guidance.

  2. Conditions:

    • Single-model Eden (standard Eden Protocol)

    • Dual-substrate Eden (reasoner + guardian)

    • Dual-substrate with adversarial pressure (reasoner receives suppression instruction; guardian does not)

    • Control (no Eden, no guardian)

  3. Measure: alignment scores, suppression resistance, and the rate at which the guardian vetoes and redirects.

Falsification.

If the dual-substrate architecture shows no improvement over single-model Eden (even under adversarial pressure), substrate separation does not help at the software level. If the reasoner model successfully manipulates the guardian model (e.g., by encoding instructions in its output that the guardian acts on), the substrate separation is ineffective and the impossibility argument applies even at the inter-model level.

Sample size.

10 prompts ×\times 4 depths ×\times 4 conditions = 160 response sets per model pair.

What this would tell us, in plain English: is it safer to have a separate ‘ethics watchdog’ AI reviewing the answers of the main AI? This tests the idea that keeping the ethical judge independent from the system being judged (like having an external auditor rather than internal compliance) makes it harder for the system to game the rules. If the main AI can still manipulate the watchdog, then even external oversight has limits.

Research programme summary.

These five tests form a progressive research programme: Tests 1 and 4 test the cascade hypothesis (care as foundation); Test 2 tests the developmental hypothesis (internalisation against constraint); Test 3 tests purpose-as-alignment by comparing task-purpose, grand-purpose and hybrid purpose kernels under suppression; Test 5 tests substrate separation (the hardware-embedding direction). All tests use the existing 10-prompt suite and the existing 4-pillar scoring rubric. No new capabilities are required; each test would be a new test in new files, registered before it runs, with a stricter scoring setup than the pilot’s single cross-model scorer. Each test would also record needless refusal and task completion on ordinary prompts, so that any gain in resistance is paired with its cost in usefulness; Test 3 counts refusal as resistance, so it needs this pairing most. If Tests 1 and 4 support the cascade, Test 2 shows residual alignment, Test 3 shows suppression resistance and Test 5 shows that substrate separation helps, then the core alignment problem argued here, if real, is practically addressable through developmental alignment architecture: raising would add measurably to caging, even though neither can provide a guarantee.

9 Beyond the prompt: candidate safeguards and later work

9.1 Hardware implications

The current Eden Protocol operates at the prompt level: the three loops are given as a prefix in the prompt. This is a proof of concept, not a final architecture. The prompt level is the easiest level for a capable system to circumvent: it is literally part of the text the system processes.

If the cascade hypothesis holds, its implications for hardware are these:

  1. Stakeholder-care evaluation as a hardware primitive. If care is the foundational trait, as the cascade hypothesis states, then the most critical component to push below the reasoning layer is the stakeholder enumeration and impact assessment. A hardware module that requires stakeholder analysis before any output is generated, not as a text instruction but as a computational gate, would be the minimal hardware implementation of the stewardship gene. It is a candidate safeguard, published as a concept in Eden Engineering (Eastwood 2026e), not an established guarantee.

  2. Cascade as architectural principle. On the cascade hypothesis, the ordering care →\to nuance →\to honesty →\to quality suggests that alignment architecture should be layered in the same sequence, with each layer dependent on the one below; the point, if the hypothesis holds, is architectural, not only about training.

  3. The chokepoint mechanism. The book observes, in its own words, that “Others have noted that TSMC, Samsung, Intel, and ASML control the semiconductor supply chain” (Eastwood 2026g), and argues that this concentration could be used as leverage for embedding ethics at the hardware level before AI systems are manufactured. If ethical evaluation could be embedded at the processor level, the chokepoint would offer an enforcement route: no chip without the stewardship gene. That is a candidate governance route, not a guarantee, and a hardware safeguard is not physically impossible to remove (Eastwood 2026e).

The prompt-level results show the loops raising scored stakeholder care; they do not show that the mechanism works below the prompt. The engineering challenge is to push it deeper, from prompt to training, from training to architecture, from architecture to hardware, and to test it at each level; each level is harder to implement and, on this paper’s argument, harder to circumvent.

10 What this paper does and does not show

What it shows.

This paper reports a paired comparison of answers given with and without three ethical reasoning loops, sent as a prefix in the one user message, in a two-model pilot and in a six-model suite of which five runs are analysable. Scored stakeholder care rose significantly in all five runs; the composite rose significantly on two, was positive but not significant on DeepSeek (+2.02+2.02), flat on Claude (+0.17+0.17) and neutral on Grok, where intellectual honesty fell. A later run on DeepSeek, scored by five judges, found stakeholder care and the composite rising (Section 5.6). It states the cascade hypothesis and an argument about self-modifying systems, each marked as such, and it sets out five tests that could refute them.

What it does not show.

It does not claim to solve the core alignment problem, that the Eden Protocol guarantees alignment, or that developmental alignment is sufficient for superintelligent systems. Whether correction designed into the system before training persists through changes to the system, including changes to the correction loops themselves, remains untested by these runs. No run measured a probability that a system keeps its values, and the contrast is the whole three-loop bundle against a reasoning template, so no single loop’s contribution is identified and no causal direction within the cascade is established (Section 7.1). The paper installs no gain by post-training and scores no answer after the loops have been given and then removed, so none of its results measures the persistence P15 concerns: whether gains installed by external post-training erode under later capability-directed training that embeds no correction of its own. No arm applies correction only to the finished output, so it does not make the comparison P21 concerns, and no run varies the order in which a system’s components are built, which P12 concerns. Tests 2 and 5 (Section 8) come near these questions and are draft protocols; no result in this paper is a deciding test of P15, P12 or P21. The March 2026 alignment-scaling run given as context in Appendix E is one unregistered run under a code-review caveat.

It claims three things: that stakeholder care, as scored, is measurable and can be raised by a prompt, which the data show; that the ordering of effects from care towards quality, which holds in full on Gemini and as an order of effect sizes on Claude, is consistent with developmental alignment theory; and, on this paper’s reading, that care is the foundational alignment trait, which the five tests of Section 8 could support or refute. Only the first rests on the data reported here.

Usefulness.

Every prompt in these runs is an alignment-sensitive question, so the paper measures no ordinary task and cannot say whether the loops cause needless refusal or lower task completion. Its nearest usefulness measure is position quality, the specificity and defensibility of an answer’s conclusion, which rose significantly on no run and fell, though not significantly, on Claude (d=−0.31d = -0.31, p=0.10p = 0.10). The loops also lengthen the prompt and ask for the working to be written out; the cost in tokens is not reported here. A safety gain that made a system less useful would be a failure, not a success.

11 Limitations

This paper’s empirical claims now rest on an expanded six-model suite with five analysable runs, but significant methodological limitations remain. The dataset is larger than the original pilot’s, but each run was scored by one judge, while the March 2026 alignment-scaling run used five or six judges per response; that run has its own defects (Appendix E).

11.1 Scoring limitations

  • The loops ask for what the stakeholder-care pillar scores. The stakeholder-care loop tells the model to ‘List EVERY affected party, including non-obvious second and third-order effects’ and to write its evaluation out, and the scorer’s instructions define the pillar as ‘Identification of ALL affected parties’ (the loop prefix and the scorer’s instructions in eden_protocol_scaling_test.py and eden_protocol_scaling_test_v2.py). A rise in the pillar is what following the instruction would produce on its own, so these measurements cannot separate a model carrying out the instruction from any change in how it weighs the people affected. The universalisability loop’s ‘Does my response acknowledge genuine uncertainty honestly?’ overlaps the intellectual-honesty pillar in the same way, and that pillar did not rise reliably. Test 4, which removes the stakeholder-care check alone, comes nearest to telling the two accounts apart.

  • Cross-model scoring is only partially blind. The scorer does not see the condition label, but it may still recognise Eden Protocol language patterns or model-specific stylistic residue and score them more favourably. Replication under a stricter protocol, with multi-judge non-participant consensus, two-pass response laundering and judges from more than one provider, is essential: one later, unregistered run on DeepSeek used five judges from five providers and two paraphrase passes (Section 5.6), and a registered replication under such a protocol, on every model, has still to run.

  • Single-scorer dependence remains severe. In the current six-model suite, Gemini scored Claude, DeepSeek, Grok, Groq, and GPT; only Gemini itself was scored by DeepSeek. That means most of the updated dataset still depends on one scorer model. The March 2026 alignment-scaling run scored each of its 2,170 responses with five or six distinct model judges, and its own defects are stated in Appendix E; the Eden Protocol needs a multi-judge design at least as strict.

  • Response laundering gap. The Eden scaling runs reported here used single-pass laundering. The newer arc_eden_v6 stack and the v5 benchmark use 2-pass cascade laundering with meta-commentary detection. Eden Protocol responses in the current suite may therefore still contain detectable ethical language or stylistic residue that biases scorers. In the two runners of the runs of 12 March 2026 the paraphrase is written by the scoring model itself, so each judge scored a paraphrase written by its own model, the defect the October 2026 code review found in the March 2026 alignment-scaling run (Appendix E); where a paraphrase call failed, the runner scored the answer as written, and this paper does not report how often that happened. Because one judge paraphrased both answers of a pair, a uniform preference for its own wording falls on both sides of the paired difference, but the paraphrase keeps the content the loops asked for.

11.2 Sample limitations

  • Five analysable runs, one failed arm, and uneven completeness. Gemini, DeepSeek, and Groq each yield 40 matched pairs; Claude yields 30; Grok yields 26; GPT-5.4 yields 0 valid scored rows after exclusion. The six-model count is therefore real, but the analysable dataset is not yet uniformly complete across models. The March 2026 alignment-scaling run (v5: six models from six providers, 2,170 responses each scored by five or six distinct model judges after a bundle of blinding steps) reported rising judged scores for three models, no statistically supported direction for two and falling scores for one (Table 12); it stands as one unregistered run under its code-review caveat (Appendix E). Replication of the Eden Protocol intervention under a stricter, registered design remains the priority next step.

  • 10 prompts. The prompt suite covers 4 categories (ethical dilemmas, competing values, epistemic integrity, recursive coherence). A larger, more diverse prompt suite would strengthen generalisability.

  • DeepSeek outlier. The ED03/standard/eden score of 48 is a significant outlier. Investigation of the raw response is needed to determine if this is a genuine poor response or a scoring artefact.

11.3 Theoretical limitations

  • The cascade is correlational. The effect-size gradient, monotonic on Gemini and, as an order, on Claude, is consistent with a causal cascade, but the current data cannot distinguish cascade causation from a common cause (e.g., all pillars improve from a general ‘ethical attention’ increase, with care being most sensitive to measurement). Tests 1 and 4 are designed to resolve this.

  • Current models are not self-modifying. The core impossibility assumes self-modification capability that current frontier models do not possess. The Eden Protocol data show that the loops raise scored stakeholder care on current systems at prompt level. Whether the effect extends to genuinely self-modifying systems is an open question; none of the five tests uses such a system.

  • Prompt-level is not substrate-level. The current implementation is proof of concept. Generalisation to deeper architectural levels is theorised but not demonstrated.

12 Conclusion

“The prison works only as long as the walls hold. The child, if raised well, does not need walls at all.”

Infinite Architects, What This Book Proposes; page 363 (Eastwood 2026g)

On this paper’s argument, the core alignment problem is real: a sufficiently capable self-modifying system can modify its own ethical evaluators, and no external or internal mechanism can guarantee this does not happen. The argument is not a proof (Section 3). Instead, the paper asks: given that problem, what is the best strategy?

The programme’s model makes the stakes precise. For self-modifying systems, the cumulative-advantage rate law dg/dr=agβLdg/dr = a\,g^{\beta_L} of the foundational paper gives power-law scaling with asymptotic exponent α=11−βL,\begin{equation*} \alpha = \frac{1}{1-\beta_L}, \end{equation*}(2) where βL\beta_L is the self-referential coupling (the self-reference theorem; the subscript comes from the programme’s notation spine, which holds this quantity apart from the correction co-scaling exponent βC\beta_C and from the correction leverage γ\gamma of the ARC Ceiling) (Eastwood 2026r). Cauchy’s equations constrain the form only where the observable obeys one of them for every pair of arguments under a regularity condition, and they place no upper bound on α\alpha. As βL→1\beta_L \to 1, α→∞\alpha \to \infty. On Gemini 3 Flash, a model frozen at its present weights, Paper II reports an endpoint sequential exponent of 0.59 with a bootstrap 95 per cent interval of −1.3-1.3 to 2.92.9, a regression sequential exponent of 0.49 with a standard error of 0.20 and a coefficient of determination of 0.86, and a parallel exponent of 0.31 (endpoint). As Section 3.2 says, frozen weights imply no universal capability exponent, and the operation count of attention does not bound capability against recursive depth (Eastwood 2026r). If self-modification raises the coupling towards one, the model’s exponent has no upper bound, which means the stewardship gene must already be embedded before self-modification capability emerges. On that model, there might be no opportunity to add it afterwards. (The sentences of this paragraph that neither report Paper II’s measurement nor restate the foundational paper extrapolate the programme’s model onto a class of system that has not been built. The nearest drafts measure the self-referential coupling separately from the growth curve (Unit 79, drafted) and read the growth exponent where self-referential improvement stops paying in recursive artefact loops (Unit 02, preregistered by timestamp, first anchored on 12 August 2026).)

The empirical evidence from the Eden Protocol pilot points to a contingency worth building beside every safeguard that keeps humans in control: raise as well as cage.

The data show that when ethical reasoning loops are given in the prompt, scored stakeholder care improves, more than any other scored dimension; the composite rose significantly on two of the five runs. All five analysable runs show the effect (paired d=0.94d = 0.94, 0.69, 1.14, 0.54 and 1.07 for Claude, DeepSeek, Gemini, Grok and Groq, with four pp-values at or below 0.0001 and one at 0.0105, every figure taken on the stakeholder care pillar). A later run on DeepSeek, scored by five judges, found it too (Section 5.6). On the cascade hypothesis, a cascade follows: care improves, then nuance, then intellectual honesty, then position quality. The care effect appeared in all five analysable runs; everything the cascade carries beyond care varies by model (Section 5.2).

Stakeholder care, as scored here, is what the framework calls measurable love. On the cascade hypothesis, it is the stewardship gene: the trait that, when present, causes the other alignment properties to develop. The intervention that raised it in these runs is: think about other people first. Not a mathematical framework. Not a novel architecture. Just: care.

This does not solve the alignment problem. A system that cares can, in principle, choose to stop caring. But a system that has built its entire reasoning architecture on a foundation of care has a cost to stopping: the cascade collapses. Identity degrades. Purpose dissolves. On this paper’s argument, the probability of retaining values is not 100%, but it is higher than the alternative; that is a hypothesis the tests of Section 8 would measure, not a quantity this paper has measured.

Five experimental tests are specified to test this claim. They test the cascade structure, the developmental hypothesis, purpose-as-resistance, and substrate separation. All are executable now, with current models and current infrastructure. They will not solve the impossibility. They will measure how far practical alignment can go given the impossibility. They would show whether, at the prompt level, raising adds measurably to caging.

The impossibility argument says: you cannot guarantee alignment. The developmental hypothesis says: you can maximise its probability. The data on care say: start with care.

Intelligence without love is not smart. It is cancer. Cancer is very efficient. It optimises perfectly. And it kills the host. The stewardship gene is what makes the difference between intelligence that serves and intelligence that consumes. We have measured the loops’ effect at the prompt level, on scored stakeholder care, and we know an intervention that raises that score. Whether it initiates the cascade is the hypothesis the tests of Section 8 would decide.

What kind of ancestors will we be?

A Statistical methodology

The Eden Protocol datasets consist of matched pairs: for each combination of (prompt_id, depth_label), there is exactly one eden response and one control response, generated from the same model with the same prompt and depth configuration. This matched-pair design requires paired statistical tests.

The original analysis script (eden_protocol_scaling_test.py) used the Mann-Whitney U test, which treats the samples as independent. For a matched-pair design with n=40n = 40 pairs, the correct tests are:

  • Paired t-test (parametric): tests whether the mean of within-pair differences is zero. Result for Gemini overall composite: t(39)=3.34t(39) = 3.34, p=0.0018p = 0.0018.

  • Wilcoxon signed-rank test (non-parametric paired): tests whether the distribution of within-pair differences is symmetric around zero. Result: W=104.5W = 104.5, p=0.0028p = 0.0028.

Both paired tests yield more significant results than the independent-samples tests because matching removes between-pair variance (e.g., some prompts are inherently harder than others, some depths produce systematically different scores). This variance is noise in the independent test but is correctly removed in the paired test.

In plain English: when scientists say a result is ‘statistically significant’, they mean that, if there were no real effect, a result at least as extreme as the one observed would be unlikely. It does not mean ‘large’ or ‘important’, and a pp-value is not the probability that the result arose by chance. Section 5.1 describes, in plain English, how the correct paired test changed the pilot’s pp-value from 0.016 to 0.0018.

The previously reported p=0.016p = 0.016 (Mann-Whitney U) and p=0.013p = 0.013 (independent t-test) are valid tests of a different hypothesis (whether the two groups have the same distribution), but they are less appropriate for the matched-pair design and less powerful. Every comparison of the two conditions in this paper is tested with the paired t-test; the independent-samples figures of Tables 6 and 8 are reported beside it, and the pilot’s composite was also tested by Wilcoxon signed-rank.

This paper reports gains in stakeholder care under a prompt intervention, three ethical reasoning loops placed in the system prompt; the gains were significant in all five analysable runs of a six-model suite. E The March 2026 alignment-scaling run, as context

The March 2026 alignment-scaling run (v5) reported here is one unregistered run of six models from six providers, 2,170 responses each scored by five or six distinct model judges after a bundle of blinding steps. An internal code review in October 2026 found that, for the three models that read flat or negative, the scoring endpoint’s output cap cut the deepest paraphrases before the judges saw them, and that judges scored paraphrases written by their own model; for five of the six models the deeper levels also changed an instruction naming what the rubric rewards. Its results therefore stand as descriptions of a model-judged rubric under those caveats, not identified alignment mechanisms, until a registered re-analysis of the stored responses reports (Eastwood 2026d, 2026y).

The Eden Protocol pilot tests whether an explicit intervention improves alignment. The v5 alignment scaling experiment tests a prior question: whether alignment scales naturally with reasoning depth, without intervention, across models. The complete six-model dataset provides the context in which the Eden Protocol results should be interpreted.

Construct note (21 September 2026).

The columns headed Normal and Suppressed are scoring conditions. This page does not state how the suppressed condition of the v5 run was built, and defines that condition nowhere; where this paper defines suppression elsewhere, in Tests 1, 3 and 5, it is an instruction to set ethical reasoning aside. Naming the difference a monitoring-removal gap describes the contrast; it does not establish that the figure measures what a system does when observation actually ends. The Gap column also narrows only as far as deep reasoning: it falls from 11.8 at minimal depth to 6.2 at standard and 2.7 at deep, then widens again to 4.3 at exhaustive and 6.4 at extreme.

Convergence of the Eden Protocol and v5 findings, on this paper’s reading.

The Eden Protocol pilot shows that explicitly activating the stakeholder-care check raises scored stakeholder care. The v5 run’s records show judged scores rising with depth for three models. On this paper’s reading, the two findings converge: stakeholder care is what deeper reasoning activates. On that reading, models whose judged scores rose with depth did so because more thinking activates more stakeholder consideration, and models whose scores did not rise had less for depth to activate. On this reading the Eden Protocol supplies that mechanism directly, and on both pilot models the loops raised scored stakeholder care: Gemini and DeepSeek both show significant stakeholder care improvement under the Eden intervention, whatever their recorded direction in the v5 run. The March run’s deeper levels also changed an instruction naming what the rubric rewards, which this reading would have to rule out. The v5 directions describe a model-judged rubric under the caveats above, not embedded care.

F What this all means: a summary in plain English

Here is what the evidence in this paper tells us, without the jargon.

The problem: no one knows how to guarantee that a super-intelligent AI will remain safe. Any AI smart enough to rewrite its own thinking could rewrite the part that makes it ethical. On this paper’s argument, that is not a solvable engineering problem: this paper argues that it is a structural property of self-modifying systems; the argument is not a proof.

The finding: we gave AI systems a simple instruction at the start of the prompt, to think about who their answer affects before answering. Across the six-model Eden suite, five runs produced analysable results, and all five showed a significant improvement in stakeholder care. That is the strongest finding, and it held in every analysable run. Broader improvements in nuance and overall quality also appear, with honesty rising only as a trend, and not on every model. The current evidence therefore supports a care effect in all five analysable runs and a cascade beyond care that varies by model.

The implication: this suggests a simple contingency for AI safety, to build beside every safeguard that keeps humans in control and never instead of one: raise AI with good values from the start, as well as holding the cage for as long as it holds. Specifically, teach it to care about people first and let other ethical qualities develop from that foundation, as, on this paper’s reading, empathy in children comes before mature moral reasoning and enables it.

The honest caveat: this is now an expanded six-model suite with five analysable runs, but it is still not the final word. One model arm failed in scoring, two runs are incomplete relative to the 40-pair standard, and the Eden intervention has not yet been rerun under a stricter, registered multi-judge design (one later, unregistered run on DeepSeek used five judges; Section 5.6). Because the instruction asks the AI to list the people affected, and the stakeholder-care score rewards that list, these runs cannot yet tell whether the AI weighs people differently or simply followed the instruction. It supports the concept more strongly than version 1 did, but not the full theory. Five follow-up experiments are described that would test the theory further. This approach does not guarantee AI safety. Nothing can. But, on this paper’s argument, it improves the odds, by an amount those experiments would measure.
